B1
 
 
 
Balcony Solar 
Description 
Colorado has created new rules that make it easier to 
use small solar systems at home including apartment 
balconies, patios, and other small spaces.  
These are often called “balcony solar” or “patio 
solar” systems. They are designed to plug into a 
standard wall outlet and help lower electricity costs 
by producing some of the power used in your home.  
What are the benefits of owning a plug-
in solar system? 
1. It can help offset a portion of your home's
electricity use with clean, renewable energy.
2. It can be installed in small spaces, making
solar power more accessible for apartment
residents, renters, and homeowners.
3. It may help reduce monthly utility costs.
4. It provides a way for customers to generate
some of their own electricity without the
typical rooftop installation.
5. It is a simple path to explore residential solar
generation before investing in a larger
system.
Requirements 
 Devices must meet national safety standards.
 Devices must be listed by a nationally
recognized testing laboratory.
 Devices must automatically stop sending
electricity to the grid during a power outage.
 Installation must comply with applicable
building and fire safety codes.
 Only one device may be connected per
address.
 Springs Utilities do not require approval or
charge a fee for qualifying devices.
 Springs Utilities is not liable to any damage
caused by these systems.
System requirements (by size) 
Systems producing 391 Watts or less: 
 These systems may be plugged into a standard
home electrical outlet.
 Springs Utilities does not require approval before
installation or use.
 These systems are generally intended for plug-in
installation and typically do not require an
electrician or electrical permit.
Systems producing between 392 Watts to 1920 Watts: 
 These systems must comply with local electrical
and building code requirements.
 Springs Utilities does not require approval before
installation or use.
 Additional  electrical work may require a licensed
electrician and permits.
Systems producing more than 1920 Watts: 
 These systems are not considered portable-scale
solar generators under Colorado law.
Questions? Learn more at 
Visit Csu.org/plug-in-solar

Strategic Growth & Water Supply Elements 
Addendum to 2023 Lyons Thrive Comprehensive Plan 
 
 
   
Purpose  
The Town of Lyons Comprehensive Plan was last updated in January of 2023. In 
2024, the State passed SB24-174 which requires certain municipalities, including 
the Town of Lyons, to:  
• Complete local or regional Housing Needs Assessments 
• Incorporate new water supply and strategic growth elements into their 
comprehensive plans 
• Adopt Housing Action Plans that include affordability and displacement 
mitigation strategies.  
Additional Components Required by SB24-174 
HOUSING NEEDS ASSESSMENT 
Over the past ten years, the Town of Lyons has taken significant steps to 
advance affordable housing policy and affordable housing development, 
including the following: 
2016: The Town adopted a goal that 10% of the local housing stock be 
affordable to residents earning 80%  or less of the Boulder County area median 
income (AMI). 
2017: The Town adopted the Boulder County Regional Housing Partnership’s 
Priorities and Strategies for Expanding Access to Diverse Housing for the 
Community, including the countywide goal of 12% affordable housing stock. 
2023: The Lyons Thrive Comprehensive Plan includes numerous goals for 
housing and affordable housing, discussed later in this Addendum.

The Strategic Growth Overlay map on the following page reflects appropriate areas for infill and 
redevelopment, and development of vacant and undeveloped lands in and near the Town.  These 
properties are considered appropriate for the following reasons: 
• Most of the properties shown on the overlay are already served by Town utility infrastructure.
• Properties along the commercial corridors are potential candidates for mixed-used redevelopment
and infill housing development.  While Town zoning allows for two-story, and in some cases three-
story development, many underutilized sites currently contain one-story structures.
• All property within the overlay map contains some form of existing development or impact to the
natural state through quarrying.  This limits impacts to natural and agricultural lands. 
• A large portion of land surrounding the Town of Lyons is owned or restricted by Boulder County as
open space acquisitions.  The overlay does not include any open space property owned by Boulder
County or under a Boulder County conservation easement. 
• New housing development along the commercial corridors would likely not be in the form of single-
unit detached housing, currently the most common form of housing in Lyons today.  Alternative
housing types, such as townhomes, apartments, or housing units above commercial uses, would 
expand housing diversity and choice. 
• The overlay map does not propose growth to the north and west.  Lands north of Town are generally
uphill, and extending infrastructure to higher elevations is technically challenging with higher
development costs.  Further, the Town’s municipal code includes extra requirements for water and
wastewater development above the “Blue Line” at 5,450 feet elevation. Lands west of Town in Apple
Valley and along the South St. Vrain river are primarily developed with a rural residential character
and not currently served by Town utilities.

Water Supply Element 
Lyons serves a population of just over 2,100 through its municipal water and wastewater utility. The service 
area generally follows the Town of Lyons boundaries, except for approximately 40 properties along Apple 
Valley Road in Boulder County. The Town receives treated water from the City of Longmont under an 
intergovernmental agreement first established in 2003 and reviewed and updated several times over the 
past two decades. That agreement helped incorporate water planning into the Town municipal code and 
long-range growth planning. In fall 2022, Lyons staff attended a WaterSmart workshop and developed an 
action plan to integrate water efficiency and water supply considerations into planning and development 
processes. The Town’s comprehensive plan was most recently updated in 2023 and in 2025, the Town 
reviewed its water supply and demand needs with Longmont. 
 
Lyons retains its own water rights portfolio, which includes native waters from the South St. Vrain, Ralph 
Price Reservoir and Lake MacIntosh as well as supporting water rights from Northern Water Conservancy 
District's Colorado Big-Thompson Project. The Town has maintained a strong water rights portfolio through 
its initial agreement with Longmont and its requirement for the dedication of water rights based on actual 
water service connections and not the amount of land that might be annexed into the water utilities service 
area.  This water rights dedication policy is also found in our municipal code.  In 2019, the town 
collaborated with Longmont to amend the IGA so it could accept a more affordable water right for 
development that included affordable housing. 
 
WATER CONSERVATION POLICIES 
Lyons loses approximately 8% of its water, compared with the EPA-cited national average of 14%. In 2027, 
the Town plans to budget for a capital improvement project to replace about 50 aging water meters. This 
upgrade will allow our Advanced Metering Infrastructure to measure water usage in gallons rather than 
increments of 1,000 gallons, enabling faster leak notifications for customers. 
 
The 2026 drought conditions have increased coordination with neighboring jurisdictions on water resource 
concerns, conservation messaging, and potential drought restrictions. 
 
In 2026, Lyons developed a Water Shortage Action Plan. For water supply planning, the Town evaluates the 
system using 3/4-inch service connection equivalents and estimates 46,000 gallons per 3/4-inch 
connection. The Town is working to establish a reduction goal for this amount.

VITAL LANDSCAPES & RESOURCES – 
Lyons lies at the confluence of North and South St. Vrain Creeks, which join to form the St. Vrain River, a 
tributary of the South Platte River. The Town recognizes this landscape as both its water source and a vital 
ecological and economic resource.  
The North and South St. Vrain Creeks and the St. Vrain River corridor are defining natural features of Lyons. 
They have shaped the Town’s geography, history, identity, economy, and pattern of development. The 
waterways provide important wildlife habitat, support native vegetation, offer opportunities for recreation 
and environmental education, and contribute significantly to the community’s scenic character and quality 
of life. 
The St. Vrain corridor is also central to Lyons’ community life and local economy. Its parks, trails, open 
spaces, and public gathering areas connect residents and neighborhoods while attracting visitors to local 
businesses and community events. Protecting public access, water quality, ecological health, and the 
natural character of the corridor is therefore essential to both community well-being and long-term 
economic vitality. 
At the same time, the creeks are powerful and dynamic natural systems. The September 2013 flood 
demonstrated the community’s vulnerability to flooding, erosion, debris, and changes in channel 
conditions. Future land-use and infrastructure decisions must respect the waterways’ natural functions, 
preserve adequate flood conveyance, avoid increasing risks to people and property, and account for 
changing precipitation patterns and other climate-related hazards. 
As Lyons grows, the Town will treat the St. Vrain waterways as essential community infrastructure and 
irreplaceable natural assets. Growth and redevelopment should protect riparian areas, maintain or improve 
water quality, support healthy aquatic and terrestrial habitat, conserve water supplies, and avoid placing 
additional development in high-risk locations. Public improvements should incorporate watershed health, 
flood resilience, environmental stewardship, recreation, and community character as complementary—not 
competing—objectives. 
The Town will continue to work with property owners, regional partners, ditch companies, water providers, 
nonprofit organizations, and state and federal agencies to protect the watershed; improve flood resilience; 
maintain appropriate public access; restore habitat; and ensure that the St. Vrain remains a healthy and 
defining feature of Lyons for future generations.
