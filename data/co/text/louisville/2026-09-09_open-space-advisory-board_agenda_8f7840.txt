Dear Chair McEachern and Members of the Open Space Open Space Advisory Board: 
 
As you finalize OSAB's comments on the PROS and Trails Plans, we offer comments about 
both the areas where Louisville Space Advocates (LOSA) is thinking along the same lines as 
you and where we have differences.    
 
LOSA  is 100% supportive of OSAB's desire to: 
• Explicitly use Article 15 of the City Charter :   Because it is foundational to the 
management of our Open Space, it is important to both include the actual language 
of the City Charter in the PROS and Trails Plans and to use it as the governing 
authority for Open Space policies and priorities.   The importance of the use of the 
actual language is highlighted by incorrect interpretations of the charter's 
requirements in the current draft.   LOSA agrees that mere reference is not enough. 
 
Note that the City Charter is not referenced in the Implementation Plan,  and should 
be.   At a minimum, references to the City Charter should be added to Strategies 0S 
1.1 and OS 1.3. (Page 111.) 
• Reconsider proposed land classifications, particularly the 6% designated as 
preserve :   When the Charter's four land classifications were established, the 
understanding was that Open Space lands would be distributed between the four 
classification categories in roughly equally proportions of about 25% each.*     
 
But the proposed new classification in the PROS Guidebook is just Preserve 6% with 
the remaining 94% allocated as Protected Lands 30%, Visitor Lands 40%, and Other 
Lands 19%.    
 
The City should use information from the new vegetation mapping; wildlife and other 
ecological surveys and mapping; combine it with commonly accepted recreation 
ecology protection  practices, including buffers; and identify another 19% of the land 
that meets the preserve qualifications.   Please drive this point home, as failure to 
classify more Open Space as Preserve does not comply with the Charter's direction 
that our Open Space is managed in a manner that: (a) is "consistent with good 
stewardship and sound ecological principles "; and (b) "preserve(s) and promote(s) 
native plants, native wildlife, and their habitats. "  
 
Note that this change needs to be carried forward to strategy OS 1.3 (page 111) 
which deals with clarifying the land classification system. 
 
LOSA is very concerned about how the concept of "balance " has been applied and asks 
OSAB to remove the word "balance " and revise all policy statements predicated on 
"balance. "  Additionally, we ask OSAB to reconsider and make up for all the years that 
recreation received preferential treatment: 
• Whether the City Charter actually allows "a  balance between ecological protection 
and recreational access? "  Summary of OSAB PROS and Trails Plan Comments  It is 
important to note that while the Charter allows "appropriate passive recreation 
activities, " the word balance does not appear anywhere in Article 15 (nor in any 
other article of the Charter).   Instead of "balance, " the management standards for 
Open Space are grounded first in "good stewardship and sound ecological principles " 
then in the preservation and promotion of "native plants, native wildlife, and their 
habitats " followed by  "cultural resources, agriculture, scenic vistas, and [finally] 
appropriate passive recreation activities. "   Basically you preserve and protect first - 
then consider everything else. 
 
The notion that just 6% of our Open Space should be classified as "Protect " shows 
how things get off kilter when ecological protection is "balanced " with 
recreation.   The City Charter's primary mandate is to manage "consistent with good 
stewardship and sound ecological principles "; There is no balancing test.**   In both 
guidebooks, the “balance” struck between wildlife, the environment, and recreation 
are heavily weighted towards recreation to the detriment of wildlife and the 
environment.   Please bring the PROS and Trails plans into alignment with the City 
Charter by removing the concept of balance. 
 
Note that this change needs to be carried forward to Goal OS.4 (page 115), which 
currently reads "Balance ecological benefits with recreation access. "  Also, note that 
this strategy strays from the City Charter by setting up an explicit, but unauthorized 
balancing test.   
 
With regard to the implementation plan, LOSA disagrees with the timeframe and priority of 
the following: 
• Strategy OS 1.2.3 (page 111)  because it only evaluates the feasibility of 
establishing appropriate criteria for buffer area setbacks for select riparian corridors 
for new projects and impacts AND is given a low priority and 6-10 year 
timeframe.   G ood stewardship and sound ecological principles require that  OS 
1.2.3 should be revised to include at least existing undesignated trails; high priority 
status; and a 1-5 year timeframe.   Because of the critical need for ecological 
protection, buffer areas should be included in these Plans or at least completed 
within a year. 
 
Finally, LOSA asks for the following corrections, which if not made now are unlikely ever to 
be made:   
• Protect the entire Coal Creek floodplain at Dutch Creek near Coal Creek Trail and 
close the undesignated trails in this area and at Hecla Lake . With regard to the 
Dutch Creek Floodplain,   open space “Preserve” was inexplicably applied to only part 
of the “Eastern Cottonwood…Floodplain Woodland” vegetation association.   (See 
2026 Louisville Vegetation Survey map for the Dutch Creek area, not paginated, and 
compare it to Trail Guidebook p. 23).   Additionally, as an accepted best practice 
and  to be consistent with the initial 2003 open space inventory 
recommendation,  this riparian area plus a 120-foot buffer, should also be placed in 
the Preserve classification. The entire area definitely meets the Preserve Charter 
Criteria of “characterized by a moderate to high level of relative ecological 
importance.”  For Preserve lands, the Charter directs that a “sufficient buffer area to permit 
the successful management of the open space-preserve land " is established. 
Further, here and at Hecla Lake, the numerous undesignated trails - damaging to the 
environment and wildlife - should be closed and restored. 
• Remove Trail 18 (p. 54 of the trails plan) and other ecologically damaging trail 
alignments.   Aligning a trail along the Goodhue Ditch directly damages sensitive riparian zones, 
fragments critical habitat, disrupts agricultural operations, and presents severe crossing hazards at 
Dillon Road. If a connection in this area is necessary, connectivity should be routed along 96th Street 
using existing signalized crossings rather than intruding into sensitive open space.  
 
 
• Correct Figure 10's graphic on fragmentation .   The representation that fragmentation is 
permitted for each of the 4 categories is highly misleading. Section 15-4 of Charter does not grant 
permission for fragmenting any category of land. Instead, it simply recognizes that the baseline level 
of fragmentation varies and uses the baseline level as a criteria for slotting the land into the correct 
category. In fact, Section 15-4(3)(B) explicitly mandates that even Open Space-Visitor Land must be 
managed so that recreational opportunities are "designed to encourage resource protection. " Section 
15-4(2) directs that "recreational impacts are contained in order to prevent any adverse 
effect on Open Space-Preserve land. "  For Open Space-Preserve land, Section 15-4(1) 
provides that "When there is a real conflict between human use and any area or item 
of ecological importance in this classification, preference shall be given to sustaining 
the area or item of ecological importance. "  Mischaracterizing descriptive baseline 
attributes as "permitted management outcomes " misleads the public and undermines Article 15.  
 
Thanks for reading through this lengthy comment letter and considering our input.  
 
Best, 
/s Cathern Smith for Louisville Open Space Advocates (LOSA)

Dear Mayor Leh and Members of City Council,  
On behalf of Louisville Open Space Advocates (LOSA), we want to thank staff, consultants, and 
our advisory boards for the tremendous care and effort invested in the Draft Trail System Plan 
and PROS Guidebook. City Council has a unique and powerful opportunity with these master 
plans to shape the long-term vision of our community. As the ultimate policy-making body, 
Council is uniquely positioned to set clear, forward-looking priorities that honor the spirit of the 
City Charter and ensure our open spaces are thoughtfully stewarded for generations to come. 
We urge you to consider and take action on the following recommendations.  
1. Prioritize Master Plan Goals to Reflect Charter Intent  
Currently, the Trail System Plan draft states that all goal statements are "equally applied in the 
decision-making process. " Stating that every goal is equal abdicates the primary purpose of a 
master plan. High-level planning requires making deliberate choices about what comes first, 
especially when balancing recreational access with environmental protection.  
Article 15 of the City Charter establishes ecological protection as the foundational requirement 
for our open space system. To honor the Charter and reflect leading stewardship practices, 
Council must establish a clear hierarchy of prioritized goals for both plans rather than treating all 
goals as equal.   We recommend:  
Trail System Plan Goals (Ranked Priority Order):  
1. Protect natural resources through data-informed management practices. (Primary 
Mandate)  
2. Enhance safety for all users.  
3. Formalize trail design standards, maintenance, operations, and construction 
protocols.  
4. Improve trail connectivity.  
5. Adopt a social trail policy.  
6. Support funding and partnerships.  
PROS Guidebook Open Space Goals (Ranked Priority Order):  
1. Establish a comprehensive ecosystem of natural open spaces in Louisville.  
2. Advance science-based data to support informed land management decisions.  
3. Promote environmental stewardship.  
4. Ensure recreational access is designed and managed to protect underlying ecological 
integrity. (Revised from generic "balance " to clarify that environmental protection is the 
baseline priority)  
5. Support funding and partnerships.  
2. Restore System-Wide Ecological Balance  
When the four charter land classifications were originally established, the intent was to 
maintain an equitable distribution to ensure robust protection across the system. 
However, the proposed breakdown in the PROS Guidebook allocates only 6% to 
Preserve and 30% to Protected Lands, while Visitor and Other Lands absorb nearly 
60%.  
 
To restore true balance and protect habitat integrity, Council should direct staff to utilize 
accepted recreation ecology practices alongside our new 2026 vegetation mapping to 
rebalance the system, aiming for roughly 50% dedicated to Preserve and Protected 
status (with ~25% in Preserve).  
 
3. Apply Data-Informed Protection: Dutch Creek Riparian Area  
Dutch Creek near the Coal Creek Trail serves as a clear example of where land 
classification needs correction. In this area, the "Preserve " designation was inexplicably 
applied to only a fraction of the "Eastern Cottonwood...Floodplain Woodland " vegetation 
association, leaving vital riparian habitat unprotected.  
 
Under Section 15-4, Preserve lands must be characterized by moderate to high 
ecological importance and supported by sufficient buffers. Council should direct staff to 
expand the Preserve classification to cover all contiguous riparian areas along Dutch 
Creek, including a standard 120-foot buffer, while actively closing and restoring the 
damaging undesignated social trails in this sensitive corridor.  
 
4. Remove Ecologically Damaging Trail Alignments (Trail 18)  
To demonstrate a genuine commitment to resource protection, Council should remove 
proposed Trail 18 (Page 54 of the Trails Plan), which proposes an off-street connection 
between Campus Drive extension and the Coal Creek Regional Trail.  
 
Aligning a trail along the Goodhue Ditch directly damages sensitive riparian zones, 
fragments critical habitat, disrupts agricultural operations, and presents severe crossing 
hazards at Dillon Road. If a connection in this area is necessary, connectivity should be 
routed along 96th Street using existing signalized crossings rather than intruding into 
sensitive open space.  
 
5. Correct Misleading Charter Interpretations in Plan Graphics  
Finally, plan graphics stating that "HIGH HABITAT FRAGMENTATION IS PERMITTED " 
under "Visitor " and "Other " classifications are inaccurate. Section 15-4 of the City Home 
Rule Charter does not grant permission for high fragmentation; it simply recognizes that 
these lands already experience higher relative fragmentation as an existing baseline 
condition.  
 
In fact, Section 15-4(b)(3)(B) explicitly mandates that even Open Space-Visitor Land 
must be managed so that recreational opportunities are "designed to encourage 
resource protection. " Mischaracterizing descriptive baseline attributes as "permitted 
management outcomes " misleads the public and undermines Article 15.  
 
Staff and consultants have built a solid foundation, but setting policy hierarchy and 
ensuring strict Charter compliance is Council's responsibility. We ask Council to direct 
staff to incorporate these priority rankings, mapping corrections, and graphic revisions 
before adopting the final plans.  
 
Thank you for your leadership and dedication to Louisville's open spaces.  
 
Respectfully submitted,  
 
Helen Moshak, Louisville Resident on behalf of  
Louisville Open Space Advocates (LOSA)

over the long term, primarily in their deep root systems and the surrounding soil .

our Open Spaces have rare and unique plants and animals.   The decisions made

and restoring our Open Space.

simplification of the  City Charter could lead to real harm.   In Preserve Lands, the

and promotes the long-term viability of native plants and native wildlife, restoration, 
restoration potential, and ecologically sound agricultural use. "  Section 15-
4(b)(1)(B).   Therefore, fragmentation is only  permissible when it preserves and 
promotes the long-term viability of native plants and native wildlife, restoration, 
restoration potential, and ecologically sound agricultural use.   Also, Figure 10 is 
ignoring that "No or very low levels of  passive recreational activities shall be 
permitted. "

importance of preventing fragmentation of   large tracts of land and promoting 
connectivity to protect endangered species and to prevent more species from 
becoming endangered. 

 
 
5. Add references to the City Charter  where the draft PROS Plan is only referencing 
City Ordinances as the policy in the ordinances flows from the Charter.   If  the 
PROS Plan is only going to reference one source of authority then it should 
reference the primary source of authority, the City Charter. 
6. I agree with other comments submitted that: [1]   with only 6% of the land designated

area to the trail network,  with appropriate language for already-developed parcels, 
and for the trail to connect from Davidson Mesa through to McCaslin Station. 
  
The proposed GDP also has no standard for what the trail connection should look like. A 4-
foot wide sidewalk, like those lining most of Centennial Valley today, invites conflict between 
trail users. And how many of our families will trust their children's lives to a strip of white 
paint in the road? Centennial Valley needs 10-foot wide multi-use paths with safe crossings, 
buffered from traffic lanes. Painted bike lanes are not appropriate infrastructure for 
families. Please recommend that the GDP be conditioned on trail design standards 
that encourage residents of all ages and abilities to safely walk and bike. 
  
Finally, it's time that Louisville updates our planning processes to get the kinds of 
development that we want. As I mentioned at PPLAB last week, our current planning 
process is designed to produce car-oriented subdivisions.  You can see that  in this GDP 
amendment, where the applicant submitted conceptual yield studies based on designs with 
enormous surface parking lots, and a 314 page traffic study in which only 4-6 paragraphs 
are dedicated to bikes and pedestrians. In the traffic study, they collected hundreds of 
pages of detailed measurements at 12 intersections. But when it comes to pedestrians, 
cyclists, and transit users, the study notes the existence of some sidewalks and bike lanes, 
applies a standard adjustment of 5%, and calls it a day without any real data or analysis. If 
we want designs that encourage people to get out of their cars, we must require planning 
that prioritizes people over cars. 
 
PPLAB recommended the following condition, and I would encourage OSAB to 
recommend the same or similar: 
As a condition of future Planned Unit Development (PUD) submittals for Parcels B1, C, F1, 
G3, and L2B, the applicant shall submit a comprehensive multimodal circulation plan 
demonstrating Complete Streets streetscape cross-sections, traffic-calming measures, and 
pedestrian/bicycle level-of-traffic-stress (LTS) analysis for all crossings connecting 
residential units to the proposed pocket parks, transit facilities, and regional trails. 
  
If we do it right, Centennial Valley will be an example of how to convert underused 
commercial areas into vibrant, walkable communities. The conditions are already in place 
for this development to succeed, including: proximity to transit, nearby jobs, a major grocery 
store, shops and restaurants, trails and open space. But the best outcome is not 
guaranteed. We could just as easily end up with each new parcel being  isolated like an 
island in a sea of roads and parking lots. Building a place that moves beyond cars requires 
a strong vision and clear rules to overcome the inertia of decades of suburban design. 
Please use your voice as an advisory board and call for these rules to be written into the 
GDP. Let's not leave it to chance.

1. The proposed development comprises our viewshed.  Davidson Mesa is at least a

is magnificent and the trail system significant enough that Davidson Mesa is a go-to
for many locals.  The feeling when on the parts of Davidson Mesa where the view is
