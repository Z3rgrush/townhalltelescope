Colorado’s pollinators are struggling. 
Gov. Jared Polis signed an executive order Wednesday committing the state to a plan to
restore pollinator habitats and protect the important species that “touch everything we
love about Colorado,” he said.

In 2024, the state released the 300-page Colorado Native Pollinating Insects Health
Study, which showed that in the last 35 years, populations of some pollinator species in
the state have dropped by more than half.
The study, which was ordered by the legislature in 2022, was the first comprehensive
report on the health and conservation status of Colorado’s native insects on a statewide
level.
Read more:
Climate change is making it tough on bees, butterflies, and other pollinators; now the
state may try to protect them
It also called for the state to protect pollinators, protect and restore their habitats,
mitigate environmental impacts, reduce pesticide risk and monitor pollinator health.
“We've been working on pollinator protection during my entire time in office, but this is
the logical next step,” Polis said while standing in front of a native wildflower bed during
a news conference at the Denver Zoo. “To do more and up our game on protecting
pollinator habitat and helping to improve the strength of our ecosystems.”

The order has three main points:
Establish a statewide strategy
Polis said he wants to build on the 2024 study of pollinator health across the state by
both continuing to track and measure how the state is doing, and using that research to
implement changes. 
“To turn that research into action and a real statewide strategy to track pollinator
health, measure it, and, of course, understand how our land management can better
have a positive impact, to act on improving pollinator health and improving those
metrics,” Polis said.
Make state land more pollinator friendly

Polis will be directing the departments of Natural Resources, Agriculture,
Transportation, Corrections, and Human Services to reduce harmful pesticide use and
expand pollinator habitat on the lands they manage.
Those agencies will now need to prioritize using pollinator-friendly options for pest
control and reduce overall use of pesticides when possible. Polis also mentioned
establishing pesticide-free buffer zones near “sensitive pollinator habitat.”
They will also be required to consider pollinators when landscaping. 
“That includes everything from right of ways …for our state highways, to state landboard
land, to parks land,” Polis said. “We really have tremendous opportunities to contribute
to pollinator health and ecosystem health.”

“We want to make it easier for local governments and private landowners to take the

Memorandum 
 
To: Louisville City Council  
From: PPLAB  
Date: September 2, 2026 
 
PPLAB requests that the Louisville City Council prioritize the revision of the 
Louisville Integrated Weed and Pest Management Program (IWPM) during the 
coming year of Council Agendas.  It has been approximately ten years since 
Louisville adopted the current IWPM and discontinued the use of glyphosate and 
2-4D in City Parks, Playgrounds and selective other City properties.  During the 
ensuing time, newer pesticides and herbicides have become available and are   
currently in use in both the Parks and Open Space Departments.  The PPLAB 
members believe that a review of the IWPM is to consider possible revisions and 
evaluations of the plan.  PPLAB would like to be included in the discussion, along 
with other members of the community, including other Louisville Advisory Boards.    
We recognize that this is a major undertaking that would require significant 
investment of time and resources on the part of City Staff, Council, and members 
of the community.  However, a review of the IWPM is timely and necessary to 
support continued health and safety of the community and the local environment.   
 
Thank you for your consideration.   
 
Respectfully, 
John Webb, Committee Chair, PPLAB

Memorandum 
 
To: Louisville City Council  
From: PPLAB  
Date: September 2, 2026 
 
PPLAB requests that the Louisville City Council prioritize the purchase of 
pesticide-free plant stock for Public Landscaping, Parks and Open Space 
requirements.  A recent webinar produced by the Xerces Society for 
Conservation of Invertebrates highlighted the common occurrence of multiple 
pesticides and fungicides in commercially available plant stock, including plant 
material available from big box stores, independent, and commercial nurseries.  
Many of these pesticides are systemic and are present in plant tissues, pollen 
and nectar that can result in exposure of people, pets, and wildlife (including 
pollinators) to potentially harmful substances.  Non-systemic pesticides that are 
present in potting medium or included soils can leach into surrounding soil and 
groundwater, impacting beneficial soil biota and creating a potential 
contamination hazard.  
Pesticide-free (“organic”) plant stock is currently available from a few local 
commercial and privately operated growers on a limited basis.  Currently, the 
availability of pesticide-free plant stock from commercial growers is limited due to 
a perceived lack of community demand, but the Xerces webinar emphasized the 
potential market impact of requests from municipal customers that could result in 
better availability and increased variety of both pesticide-free ornamental and 
native plant stock.  
We recognize that this request would require some investment of time and 
resources on the part of City Staff. However, the improvements in continued 
health and safety of the community, wildlife habitat, and the local environment 
would be worth the effort.   
 
Thank you for your consideration.   
 
Respectfully, 
John Webb, Committee Chair, PPLAB

There has also been initial discussion about potentially organizing a mural festival in Louisville,
which could involve up to 10 murals by 10 different artists throughout the community over a
single weekend. The concept being discussed would include a combination of public and
private spaces, with several local business owners having expressed interest in installing
murals on their properties. A festival of this scale would require funding beyond the existing
public art budget.
 
Any mural project involving the train cars would also require coordination with Public Works, as
they have an interest and involvement with the train cars.
 
In summary, a mural on the train cars is possible, but the process and approach would need to
be determined based on the public art process, the historic designation of the cars, funding, and
coordination with the appropriate City departments.
