DOWNTOWN  MERRIAM MASTER PLAN  
Plan Framework — Working Draft 
What this document is  
A single working draft that pulls together the pieces developed to date — the Vision Statement, 
the Guiding Principles, a first set of Downtown-activation strategies, and the three vision 
frameworks (A/B/C) that describe scale of change. 
 
These are DRAFT for discussion at Steering Committee Meeting #3 on August 18, 2026. 
  
2
Working Draft — For Review 
1. DOWNTOWN VISION STATEMENT  
Draft Vision Statement 
“Downtown Merriam is an everyday place — local shops, food, makers, and 
artists, with parks and gathering places along Turkey Creek where the whole 
community comes together. It has what a Downtown should have, and it looks 
like nowhere but Merriam.”  
 
NOTE TO STEERING COMMITTEE: The Downtown Vision Statement will hold true whether the 
community chooses Vision A, B, or C. Scale is of the vision is what changes. We will discuss and 
refine this at the August Steering Committee meeting.  
2. GUIDING PRINCIPLES  
The 26-principle, 7-theme set below is the Steering Committee working set carried through SC #2 and 
refined for SC #3. 
 
Theme 1 — Safety & Flood Readiness  
1. Safety comes before growth.  
A flood warning system and a workable evacuation plan are in place and operating before occupancy 
or assembly uses expand in regulatory floodplain areas. Growth follows safety, not the other way 
around. 
2. Don’t put people where flooding can trap them.  
No residential development on blocks where access stays in the floodplain until protection is in place. 
3. Grow in the right sequence.  
Flood-neutral areas — ground already out of the floodplain — develop first. Flood-dependent sites are 
planned now and held for construction until protection is in place. 
4. Merriam Drive should come out of the floodplain.  
Merriam Drive sits in the Turkey Creek floodway today. Which flood mitigation approach the City 
pursues drives both the cost and the timeline for getting it out. 
Theme 2 — Turkey Creek, Stormwater & Open Space  
5. Turkey Creek is an asset, not just a hazard.  
The creek is Downtown’s strongest natural feature. Plan around it as a place people want to be, not 
only a flood risk to manage. 
6. Reduce pollutants flowing into the creek.  
Site and infrastructure improvements should improve water quality, not just move stormwater through 
faster. 
7. Nature can provide cleaner water and beauty.  
Rain gardens, native plantings, and green stormwater infrastructure do double duty — function and 
place-quality — in the Turkey Creek buffer. 
8. Open space does double duty.  
Parks, plazas, and gathering spaces serve as both community amenity and stormwater storage where 
the floodplain requires it.

Working Draft — For Review 
Theme 3 — A Walkable, Connected Downtown  
9. Downtown reads as one place.  
Continuous sidewalks, safe crossings, street trees, and trail connections tie the district together rather 
than leaving it as disconnected parcels. 
10. Connect across barriers.  
Merriam Drive, the BNSF rail line, and the creek should be crossed easily on foot and bike, not just by 
car. 
11. Address parking and circulation first.  
Parking and access decisions come early in framework planning, not as an afterthought once land use 
is set. 
Theme 4 — A Working Downtown  Economy  
12. A living, working Downtown, around the clock.  
Downtown should support activity beyond the 9-to-5 workday — evenings and weekends, not just 
weekday lunch traffic. 
13. The right housing in the right place.  
Housing belongs where it is safe to put it, sized and positioned to support Downtown businesses rather 
than compete with them for ground floor space. 
14. A locally grounded business mix.  
Live/work units and small maker spaces give local entrepreneurs a foothold; Downtown should grow 
local businesses, not just recruit chains. 
15. A Downtown for everyday life.  
Daily-needs coverage — food, services, things people already need — comes before destination retail. 
16. Build to demand and  help create it.  
New development should respond to real market signals while public investment works to grow that 
market over time. 
Theme 5 — Taxpayer Return on Investment  
17. Public investment leads private investment.  
Sequence public streetscape, infrastructure, and placemaking investment to de-risk and attract private 
development — don’t wait for developers to go first. 
Theme 6 — Identity and Design  
18. Downtown has its own identity and vibe.  
Design and signage should feel distinctly Downtown Merriam, not a generic commercial corridor. 
19. Distinctly Merriam — build on our own assets.  
Lean into what already makes Merriam – “Merriam” rather than copying another city’s Downtown. 
20. Preserve what people already value.  
Mature trees, the trail, small-town character, and older buildings worth reusing are assets to protect, 
not obstacles to redevelopment. 
21. Identity, placemaking, and public art are core, not extras.  
Gateways, wayfinding, and public art are part of the framework plan from the start, not add-ons at the 
end of budget. 
22. A place to gather.  
Family-friendly spaces with things to do — events, programming, places to sit — build community 
gathering, not just foot traffic.

3. STRATEGIES TO BRING MORE PEOPLE DOWNTOWN
These strategies translate the Vision and Guiding Principles into direct action. Each strategy 
names what the City and plan will do — and, where relevant, how it depends upon whether a 
flood control project is pursued, which approach, and the timing. 
Strategy 1: Concentrate more housing within a half mile  of the center of 
Downtown . 
With only one housing unit in Downtown and limited housing units nearby retail and restaurant 
demand hasn’t materialized despite a strong trade area. Adding housing close to Downtown — 
not scattered across the wider area — builds the walk-in customer base Downtown businesses 
need. 
We will: 
• Direct new housing to sites that are out of the regulatory floodplain first – to the North of
Turkey Creek bridge up to 55th Street.
• Concentrate units within a half mile of the core rather than spreading density thinly across
the wider trade area.
• Pair housing with the retail and service mix the plan is trying to grow.
We won’t: 
• Approve housing on blocks where access remains in the floodway/floodplain until
protection is in place.
Strategy 2: Facilitate an anchor that fills a market gap — performing arts  
or social entertainment  venue. 
Downtown's daytime worker base is large, but there is little reason to stay after 5 p.m. An 
anchor use that fills a market gap — a performing arts venue or a social/entertainment 
destination — gives people a reason to come Downtown at night and on weekends as well as 
daytime. 
We will: 
• Identify and evaluate sites capable of supporting an anchor venue, prioritizing land already
out of the floodplain or advanceable.
• Confirm the specific gap the anchor should fill before targeting a use.
• Frame the anchor as a catalyst that pulls complementary food, beverage, and retail uses
around it.
We won’t: 
• Chase a regional destination that competes with, rather than reinforces, the everyday-place
vision.
Strategy 3: Preserve open space where the community can gather.  
5
Working Draft — For Review 
Open space is not leftover land — it is Downtown's shared gathering ground and, where the 
floodplain requires it, its flood storage. Every vision scenario keeps meaningful open space; the 
plan should protect it from being treated as the default site for anything else. 
We will:  
• Identify open space areas early in the framework plan and hold them, regardless of which 
vision scenario the community selects. 
• Design open space to serve double duty — community gathering and 
stormwater/floodplain function — where it sits in the regulatory floodplain. 
• Program open space for regular events, not just passive green space. 
We won’t:  
• Treat open space as a placeholder land use to be redeveloped once market demand 
appears. 
• Design open space only for engineering function without a gathering-space purpose. 
Strategy 4: Encourage artisans , makers  and unique local shops.  
A locally grounded business mix is what makes Downtown feel like Merriam rather than any 
other commercial corridor. There's no rail spur or siding along the BNSF line here — just 
frequent through-trains, day and night. Traditional Downtown uses — restaurants, boutique 
retail, offices, and housing — prefer sites set back from the tracks, where train noise and 
vibration don't compete with a quiet patio or a quiet night's sleep. That same noise and 
vibration is exactly what makes the area between Kessler Lane and the BNSF line a strong fit for 
artisans and makers instead: these uses tolerate sound and vibration that other uses can't, and 
they need durable, flexible space with street-facing loading and service access — which reuse 
buildings in this corridor already offer. Reuse some existing buildings in this corridor for artisan 
and maker space wherever possible. 
We will:  
• Support small-footprint, flexible commercial and workspace suited to artisans and makers, 
particularly in reuse buildings between Kessler Lane and the BNSF line. 
• Lead with train noise and vibration — not rail service or the floodplain — as the rationale for 
artisan, maker, and light-production uses that can tolerate sound other uses can't. 
• Prioritize local business retention and growth tools alongside recruitment. 
We won’t:  
• Design storefronts and lease sizes only around national retail chains. 
• Displace existing local businesses without a transition path. 
Strategy 5: Invest in public art and create unique public spaces.  
Public art and distinctive public spaces are part of the framework plan from the start, not an 
amenity added after the budget is set. They are how Downtown signals its own identity rather 
than a generic commercial district. 
We will:  
• Integrate public art and placemaking into the streetscape, gateway, and open space 
framework, not as a separate later phase. 
• Use public art and unique public spaces to reinforce the “distinctly Merriam” identity rather 
than importing another city's aesthetic. 
• Pair public space investment with programming that activates it. 
We won’t:  
• Treat public art as decoration added after redevelopment is complete. 
6
Working Draft — For Review 
• Duplicate another Downtown's identity rather than building on Merriam's own assets. 
Strategy 6: Upgrade infrastructure and utilities.  
Public infrastructure investment leads private investment —support public investment as a 
market catalyst. Streetscape and utility upgrades reduce risk for private developers and should 
come early, not after redevelopment has already started. 
We will:  
• Sequence public streetscape, utility, and infrastructure investment to de-risk priority 
redevelopment sites ahead of private investment. 
We won’t:  
• Wait for private developers to invest first before upgrading public infrastructure. 
• Spread infrastructure investment so thin that no single area reaches the critical mass 
needed to attract development. 
Strategy 7: Make it easy to get Downtown  and get around — on foot, by 
bike, by car, or by bus.  
It should be simple to reach Downtown and simple to move around once you're there, no matter 
how you're traveling. Today, getting Downtown by car is straightforward, but walking, biking, 
and finding parking are harder than they should be. Fixing this is what turns visitors into 
regulars. 
We will:  
• Fix sidewalks, crossings, and lighting so walking Downtown feels safe and comfortable, not 
just possible. 
• Add clear wayfinding so people can find parking, businesses, and the creek trail without 
guessing. 
• Place parking and manage parking so it is where people need it. 
• Plan for bikes and transit alongside cars — bike routes that connect to the regional trail 
network, and a Downtown that works for someone arriving by bus. 
We won’t:  
• Design streets and parking around cars only, at the expense of people walking or biking. 
Strategy 8: Make Downtown  feel comfortable.  
Feeling comfortable is different from being told a place is safe — it comes from lighting, activity, 
and human-scale design, not signage or enforcement. A downtown with people, shade, seating, 
and light after dark feels comfortable because it's being used, not because someone said it's 
fine. 
We will:  
• Design streets and public spaces with shade, seating, and lighting that make people want to 
stay, not just pass through. 
• Use ground-floor activity and evening programming — not just security presence — to make 
Downtown feel comfortable after dark. 
• Build comfort in from the start on every public realm project, not as a fix added after a 
space turns out to feel empty. 
We won’t:  
• Rely on signage or security alone to create a feeling of comfort. 
• Design public spaces that are technically safe but empty and uninviting. 
7
Working Draft — For Review
Strategy 9: Offer regular events and programming.  
A great space doesn't bring people back on its own. Regular events and programming — not 
just an annual festival — give people a reason to return again and again and turn first-time 
visitors into regulars. 
We will: 
• Build a regular, ongoing calendar of markets, festivals, and community events Downtown,
no
t just one big event a year.
• Program public spaces — plazas, open space, streets — for activity across all seasons, not
only in good weather.
• Coordinate event programming with local businesses so events send customers their way,
not around them.
We won’t: 
• Let public spaces sit unused between big annual events.
