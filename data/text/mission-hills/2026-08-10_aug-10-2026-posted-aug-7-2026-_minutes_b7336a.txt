B. Legislative Update 
Ms. Krstulic provides an update on Senate Bill 418, the By-Right Housing Development Act, 
noting that both the bill text and a legislative summary prepared by the Kansas Legislative 
Research Department have been included in the packet so the Council can review the language 
directly. Ms. Krstulic describes this as an ongoing area of exploration, noting many cities across 
the state are wrestling with the same questions. Ms. Krstulic flags what she sees as a significant 
point of protection for Mission Hills and similarly situated communities because the area's 
topography is hilly and prone to drainage issues. She notes  the whole thrust of the bill is to 
speed up development approvals by requiring administrative, non-discretionary approval within 
30 days for qualifying homes, the bill's exclusion of any project requiring a drainage study 
matters a great deal, since Mission Hills requires a drainage study for all new homes and other 
significant construction. Ms. Krstulic notes the act would apply only to new homes in any case, 
and that an open question remains as to whether it is meant to apply only to new construction on 
vacant lots or also to redevelopment of already built-out property, which is what a city like 
Mission Hills is more likely to encounter. Ms. Krstulic says she does not have a definitive 
answer but believes there is a good argument since it does not  apply to redevelopment. 
Councilperson Davidson asks whether the bill defines affordable housing to which Ms. 
Krstulic confirms no. Councilperson Thorne asks whether that means any project requiring a 
drainage study is automatically excluded to which Ms. Krstulic confirms that is correct, it would 
not qualify as by-right housing, which she calls a helpful protection. Councilperson Moran asks 
under what circumstances the act would become an issue. Ms. Krstulic explains the bill would 
mainly affect smaller homes, under 2,500 square feet, which would be required to follow 2018 
building codes with limits on architectural finish and single-car garages.  Ms. Krstulic notes the 
bill makes a bigger change elsewhere as it eliminates the right to file a protest petition for 
rezonings tied to residential or multi-family projects, calling this very interesting given that 
multi-family developments, including apartments and townhomes, could fall under the act as 
well. Ms. Krstulic says she cannot imagine neighbors being unable to file a protest petition for a 
multi-family project and expects that provision to generate significant pushback. Ms. Krstulic 
adds that the bill contains a number of internal inconsistencies that are causing consternation 
statewide and  she expects to be discussing this for some time. She adds that she will  be 
attending the city attorneys' CLE in October, where legislative updates are a standing feature and 
where she expects this bill to be a major topic. 
Councilperson Bruning asks whether the City’s lot split ordinance conflicts with the

that the City’s minimum lot size requirement applies. Councilperson Bruning notes that the

C. Discuss Renewal Amendment Terms for Solid Waste Services 
Ms. Woolbright presents the Council with two options; to accept the proposal Waste 
Management has submitted to continue the City's current level of service or open the contract to 
competitive bid. Ms. Woolbright notes she let Waste Management know the Council would be 
discussing this tonight and would ultimately decide whether to move to a bid process, and that 
the company confirmed it would participate in a bid if that is  the direction the Council chooses. 
Ms. Woolbright shares that under the proposal currently on the table, the base monthly rate per 
household would increase, as a one-time adjustment, from $23.81 to $26.81, or $321.72 
annually, followed by 4.7 percent annual increases in years two through five. Ms. Woolbright 
notes the City had already budgeted for an anticipated 5 percent increase for 2027 and highlights 
several features unique to the current contract that might not carry over through a competitive 
bid process: one citywide day of unlimited yard waste collection, two bulky-item pickups 
annually, live holiday tree disposal, one week of unlimited trash collection each year, and the 
7 
  
 
fact that yard waste is collected separately from trash and recycling. Ms. Woolbright explains 
she's highlighting these because the current contract has evolved through a series of renewals and 
amendments rather than a fully open competitive process, so the City likely would not retain 
identical service levels through a bid, though that does not  mean the City cannot pursue a bid if 
the current costs or services are not what the Council wants to pursue. Ms. Woolbright notes 
Mission Hills is one of only four communities in the Kansas City metro area, and the only one on 
the Kansas side, that folds its solid waste bill into the property tax bill, calling it a distinctive 
amenity for residents. Ms. Woolbright adds that even with the proposed increase, the City’s 
proposed 2027 cost remains within about $30 of the 2025 metro median cost. She notes that staff 
is not  making a specific recommendation but wants the Council to have the full picture before 
deciding whether to bring back a five-year renewal resolution or open the bid process instead. 
Councilperson Bruning asks for clarification on whether this locks in a fixed proposal or 
leaves room for further negotiation. Ms. Woolbright confirms the City would be accepting a 
fixed proposal. Mr. McQuaid asks whether there's a contingency in place should something 
happen to David Vogt, the City’s beloved driver who is most associated with the route. Ms. 
Woolbright explains the arrangement is specified in the contract and that the City has discussed 
this with Waste Management directly and they indicated Mr. Vogt  intends to stay in his role. 
Mr. McQuaid asked what would happen if that changed to which Ms. Woolbright says the City 
would retain the same service levels but would need to work out a new driver assignment. Mayor 
Weed emphasizes how much value the relationship provides beyond just the driver himself, 
noting the City does not charge for yard waste, that residents can specify exactly how they want 
trucks positioned in their driveways, and that there are countless small accommodations built 
into the relationship over time, saying he does not  believe the City would ever be able to fully 
replace this arrangement if it went out to bid. Mr. Holmes, with professional experience in the 
waste services industry, notes that a 4.7 percent annual increase is attractive relative to typical 
industry pricing this year, which runs closer to 5.5  percent, even though this year's one-time 
base adjustment is somewhat higher than that trend, since providers are generally targeting 
internal cost inflation in the low single digits plus a modest margin spread. 
Councilperson Davidson moves to renew the Waste Management contract with a five-
year extension, Councilperson Thorne seconds, and all councilpersons agree.  Ms. Lee shares 
that Council will need to revote when the formal resolution is brought to Council at the 
September  meeting.

A. Presentation of Potential Art Display Program Inside City Hall 
Ms. Lee introduces Lauren Reddin, who has spent the summer working with the City as an intern 
ahead of returning to law school at Washburn this fall, to present a proposal originating from an 
idea brought to the Mayor by Mission Hills resident Ms. Bremyer. Ms. Reddin explains that over 
the summer, sitting in on ARB, City Council, and BZA meetings, she noticed two recurring 
themes in the City's work, a commitment to aesthetics and a focus on community engagement, 
and that Ms. Bremyer's suggestion, of displaying art that residents may own or collect at City  
combined both. Ms. Reddin proposes a City Hall art display program under which residents 
would loan artwork for rotating display periods of approximately three months, with her 
recommended location being the back wall of the Council chambers, which she describes as 
ideal because the space just seems to call for artwork. The program would not be limited to 
residents who own a piece of art or a photograph. Ms. Reddin notes the application would 
include a certificate of ownership, a liability waiver, and the City's current insurance includes a 
8 
  
 
fine arts coverage extension for artwork owned by others while in the City's care, limited to 
$50,000 per occurrence. Residents would be responsible for coordinating installation and 
removal with City staff and selections would be made by a proposed three-member arts 
commission consisting of one Council member, one City staff member, and one resident with an 
interest in the arts. Ms. Reddin has also prepared sample application materials and promotional 
documents that could serve as a starting point if the Council chooses to move forward. 
Councilperson Bruning raises the possibility of controversy, recalling his prior service as 
chairman of the Missouri Arts Council and a widely known instance of a religious object 
displayed within a bodily-fluid-filled container under a state arts program, stating that artists are 
tough birds and that the City has some artists in town who are politically involved and visible, as 
such  the Council should go into the program aware there could be some blowback, though he 
calls the idea fascinating. Ms. Reddin responds that she has considered this, noting that 
comparable programs in other cities typically set guidelines excluding artwork that is political in 
nature or derogatory toward gender, race, religion, or sexuality, and that having a commission 
select process should help mitigate extreme submissions. Ms. Bremyer is invited by the Mayor to 
speak and shares that the idea originated from thinking about how the Church property, recently 
acquired by the City, may eventually create flexible community spaces, potentially for the arts, 
and that she did not want the idea to disappear even as those plans remain uncertain. Ms. 
Bremyer shares she serves as a docent at the Kemper Museum, holds a fine arts degree from the 
School of the Art Institute of Chicago, and that a great deal of art sits archived and unseen. She 
adds  this program could eventually expand to a second wall and welcomes art beyond that 
created by Mission Hills residents. Ms. Bremyer highlights Ms. Woolbright’s skills and 
collegiate knowledge of art  display and archiving and offers to be involved in whatever way 
makes sense. 
Mayor Weed notes he would like to see the Council move ahead but believes some 
guardrails are needed, and suggests the concept be studied further before coming back. 
Councilperson Moran calls it a great idea. Councilperson Bruning observes that getting involved 
with the arts may bite the Council at some point but calls it a worthwhile public role, adding that 
the group may want to think about regulations, though it is unclear how one regulates art. Ms. 
Reddin notes that comparable programs typically include guidelines such as prohibiting 
discriminatory artwork. Ms. Krstulic advises that if the guidelines touch on anything religious, 
they should apply evenly across all religions given the relevant case law, and that the City would 
aim to do the same here. Councilperson Bruning suggests a sophisticated task force could simply 
recognize potential problems as they arise, provided it has full discretion. 
Mayor Weed asks whether a new committee is needed, noting the City already has a 
Community Engagement Committee, and raises broader questions of should this become an arts 
commission, under or separate from the Park Board, noting the current proposal is it limited to 
indoor art. He noted should it eventually extend to outdoor art, given how much outdoor art 
residents already display or want to display this could engaged the Park Board. Councilperson 
Thorne notes park statuary and fountains already fall under the Park Board. Mayor Weed 
mentions a piece of art recently placed at 59th Street and Oakwood Road through a private 
donation went through the Park Board process. Mayor Weed circles back to the core question of 
where art starts and stops, and whether the Council should take time to define the concept fully 
or move forward with a simple committee like the Community Engagement Committee. 
Councilperson Moran responds that the proposal can stay simple for now and grow later if a need 
arises. Councilperson Davidson agrees, saying the Council should keep it simple and focus on 
this specific effort rather than trying to solve everything at once.
