PUBLIC SAFETY  
A. Police Report 
Captain Putthoff reports there was no property crime to report for the month of August 
describing it as a team effort and crediting proactive policing overnight and residents who lock 
their cars and keep garages closed. Mayor Weed states that in his nine years on the council this is 
the first time he has seen a report with no property crime. Captain Putthoff thanks residents for 
the crime prevention habits the department speaks about so often. 
Captain Putthoff states that the Chief has heard from several Councilpersons on behalf of 
constituents that the department has fielded questions since License Plate Readers (LPRs) 
became a common practice among police departments, and that the topic has recently become a 
hot topic both locally and nationally. He notes that the LPR information is on the Prairie Village 
Police Department’s website and encourages residents to look there. Captain Putthoff states that 
the Chief and the department understand the concerns about privacy, accountability, and bad 
actors, and acknowledges that people hear stories of corrupt officers across the nation who do 
things they are not supposed to do. He states that the department is committed to holding its 
people accountable and to putting restrictions and audits in place. 
Captain Putthoff explains that Flock has become synonymous with all cameras, but the 
City does not have any Flock cameras. Its cameras are made by Axis and managed by Genetec, 
the data is stored locally, and the City owns all of it, which is completely different from what 
3 
  
 
Flock does. Captain Putthoff states that the data is not sold and that the only reason it would be 
shared is if an outside agency needs to track criminal activity within the City, in which case the 
only way to obtain data on a specific plate is through a written request, and those requests are 
tracked. Captain Putthoff reports that there are about 25 LPR intersections in Mission Hills and 
that access is limited to police officers and dispatchers, who attend training. Captain Putthoff 
states that he believes the City is setting the standard in the area but most agencies retain LPR 
data for one to two years, and the City recently changed from one year to 90 days. He 
acknowledges that this caused discussion among staff about whether 90 days is enough for a big 
crime or pattern crime and whether some data from four months ago would be missed, and states 
that the answer is potentially yes. However, the department feels 90 days captures as much data 
as it can for crimes in Mission Hills while communicating to citizens that it wants to protect their 
data and will not abuse the system. Captain Putthoff explains that the system keeps audit logs 
and that he conducted a couple audits a month ago, showing which officer searched for a plate, 
who accessed it, and when. He states that anything on a computer is automatically logged, and 
that the audit reviews, which occur every six months, will be included in the annual report. The 
policies and procedures are online and can be read word for word. Captain Putthoff states that 
overall he hopes to set minds at ease, that he understands people's fear of being tracked, and that 
he believes the department is doing things right. 
Captain Putthoff then describes two uses. The first is investigative for when a house is 
broken into or a car is stolen or burglarized and officers get a suspect plate; they can search 
where that plate has been in Mission Hills and Prairie Village over the past 90 days. The second 
is live hits. For example, at two in the morning, a stolen car comes across from Missouri, the 
plate registers as stolen, and every officer and dispatcher is alerted, whether in the office or in a 
car. As the vehicle hits different LPRs, officers respond to the area, and even if the car flees or is 
not stopped, the department believes it has prevented some property crimes. Live hits also flag 
warrants, misdemeanors, and felonies, and the department has had success stories. He states that 
the department believes it has struck a balance between privacy and the benefit of solving and 
even preventing crime. 
Ms. McKenna states that the explanation of how the department balances these issues 
was helpful and asks whether the data collected is publicly available on the website, as she is 
interested in both the data and the system's security, adding that she appreciates the limited 
access and retention. Captain Putthoff asks what she means by data, and Ms. McKenna asks 
whether anything is captured beyond the plate. He responds that the license plate itself may be 
linked to a home address, a name, or registered owners, but in order to get that information the 
department must go to another source, and that all the reader captures is the plate. Captain 
Putthoff adds that the information is isolated and not associated with the plate, except that a 
stolen car will carry certain information about a suspect or registered owner. Ms. McKenna states 
that from a data security standpoint this makes sense, that she loves the audit trail, and that the 
department has clearly thought this through. Ms. McKenna asks whether the cameras have had a 
deterrent effect and if they could be linked to the month without property crime, explaining that 
the question arose when her son asked whether the City has Flock cameras while walking 
through the neighborhood. Captain Putthoff responds that he would be speaking out of turn if he 
said the month without property crime was the result of LPRs alone, that it is a culmination of 
things, including other tools such as the police grappler, which can catch and seize cars that flee, 
and other crime prevention. He states that LPRs and cameras alone will not deter all crime or 
catch all criminals, but that they are another tool and have had a good effect.  
4 
  
 
Mayor Weed asks whether caught criminals  remark on Mission Hills during interviews. 
Captain Putthoff responds that detectives sometimes hear from people in custody for property 
crimes that they do not mess around in Mission Hills or Prairie Village, and that the same could 
be true of Leawood and Westwood. He states that together with the LPR cameras and the 
grappler, the steps City Council has invested in have created an atmosphere in which word has 
gotten out.  
Councilperson Sullivan asks whether the cameras capture anything more than the license 
plate, such as a photo of the driver or a person in the car. Captain Putthoff responds that the 
traffic cameras and the LPRs are different. The traffic cameras capture the overall view, are not 
automatically recorded, and can be reviewed back to the same retention time. The LPR has two 
views, an overall view and a specific plate view, and while it may capture other vehicles or 
people in the area, it is focused on the plate. Captain Putthoff states that it can capture an 
individual driver but that the quality is not good enough for identification. Councilperson 
Sullivan asks whether photos of a person in a vehicle would be given to an agency such as ICE. 
Captain Putthoff responds that, given the department's legal mandate to assist federal law 
enforcement partners, it would cooperate with ICE if the request were similar to another outside 
agency request, meaning it must be written and part of a law enforcement investigation. Captain 
Putthoff states that the department has yet to receive any request like that. 
Captain Putthoff notes that speeding has been a problem for as long as there have been 
vehicles, and that in his 15 years with the department, Mission Hills has been no exception. He 
describes a three-part approach. The first is education about the speed limits and traffic laws for 
residents and for those traveling through the City. The second is enforcement, including a more 
visible officer presence, writing tickets, and selective enforcement in areas known to have more 
speeders. The third is engineering, such as speed bumps, sidewalks, or narrowing of streets, 
which is a traffic calming program outside the scope of what police can provide, though the 
department can help collect data. Captain Putthoff states that the department hears concerns on 
63rd Street, State Line Road, and Tomahawk Road, which are the larger thoroughfares, as well 
as on the side streets of 65th Street, 67th Street, and 68th Street. 
Captain Putthoff states that education goes hand in hand with enforcement and that the 
department has a new system for tracking selective enforcement, with everything coming 
through the Traffic Sergeant Mieske. He encourages City Council to tell constituents to contact 
the sergeant directly, because that is where complaints are logged, adding that his contact 
information is on the website. Captain Putthoff reports that three selective enforcement areas are 
currently out and that the four officers assigned to Mission Hills will make it a priority to spend 
an hour or two in them during their day. He states that much of the evidence is anecdotal, that 
cars look like they are going fast and probably are, but that the department wants data to back it 
up so it can best use its resources. Captain Putthoff explains that the speed trailer displays a 
vehicle's speed, which is another visual, such as a driver seeing a limit of 25 while doing 35, and 
that it also registers speeds for data collection. He gives the example of finding that speeding on 
Tomahawk Road peaks between two and six, so that an officer can be directed there, since 
officers cannot spend an entire shift in one place and must remain available for calls for service. 
Mayor Weed shares that he has had questions about whether red light or speed cameras 
are an option, Captain Putthoff responds that they are not, given the hot topic of LPRs, cameras, 
and data collection, and that even if it were legal he does not think the department would go 
down that road. He notes that there is case law from other cities that if no one is there to visually 
observe the speeding, stop the driver, and positively identify them, this is not an option.

Mayor Weed asks whether the City should have more trailers that capture and register 
speed. Captain Putthoff responds that the City has a speed trailer and a data collector trailer, that 
both collect data, but one collects more, and that additional units are an option but it would be a 
matter for City staff and budgeting. He notes that the two trailers are shared with Prairie Village 
and can only be in one place at a time. Mr. McQuaid asks about the permanent sign by the school 
near Belinder Elementary and whether it registers information for the department. Captain 
Putthoff responds that he would assume so if it registers speeds but would have to check and get

Ms. McKenna states that they have gone from being the youngest couple on the block to

car coming. She states that the speed occurs along Tomahawk Road between Belinder Avenue 
and Ensley Lane, down the hill, and asks that the City put up more information for drivers, 
saying she loves the idea of several more trailers or something to slow traffic so that there is no 
tragedy on the street. Captain Putthoff responds that the permanent speed signs would be up to 
Council and staff to purchase and adds that the biggest thing residents can do is report 
complaints directly to the traffic sergeant. Captain Putthoff states that it is the police 
department's job to make officers visible in high-speed areas, and that it is a cooperative effort, 
because the speeding is by outsiders and also by residents, and it is on the officers to hold them 
accountable. 
Mayor Weed thanks Captain Putthoff for the presentation and for the Pizza with a Cop 
event the week before, where the officers were available to the public and some residents spoke 
with them about using their driveways for enforcement vehicles. Mayor Weed states that being 
available to the public is really important. Captain Putthoff responds that certain areas on 
Tomahawk Road are difficult for officers to hide or park and notes that the department does 
receive requests like this and that officers can use a resident’s driveway if one is offered.

D. Resolution No. 2026 – II: Design Guidelines 
Ms. Clifton states that there are two proposed changes. The first concerns the width and length of 
detached buildings as a recent proposal for a detached building sparked discussion of how long 
9 
  
 
such buildings should be. The guidelines currently recommend a footprint of no more than 720 
square feet for most building areas, and in reviewing that building the Architectural Review 
Board (ARB) suggested that the Planning Commission consider adding a recommendation for 
width and length. After discussing it for about three meetings and holding a public hearing, the 
Planning Commission proposes a maximum overall width or length of 30 feet when the building 
is located outside the primary building area, added on four pages of the Design Guidelines and 
highlighted in yellow in the packet. Councilperson Bruning notes that this concerns accessory 
buildings, which Ms. Clifton confirms.  
The second change concerns pool equipment, and Ms. Clifton explains that when pool 
projects are proposed, owners often want to place the equipment farther from the house, that 
responsible architects and owners, especially on a new house or large project, will put it in 
underground vaults or in a fenced or walled utility yard so it cannot be seen and the noise is 
suppressed. She adds the City hears complaints when owners want to place it along the property 
line rather than up against the house. The Planning Commission recommends that the equipment 
be placed underground, if possible, that it be located in the rear yard and fully screened with a 
solid fence or wall, and that if it is above ground it be closer to the primary building than to the 
rear and side property lines. Ms. Clifton adds that the Planning Commission added a caveat for 
large properties where the pool may be closer to the rear property line than the primary building 
area, which would make it impossible to place the equipment closer to the house because of the 
long distance between the house and the pool. In that case the equipment should be located along 
the side of the pool closest to the applicant's home. 
Councilperson Thorne asks whether the example in the packet is currently someone's 
situation and whether a neighbor is complaining, and Ms. Clifton responds that it is and that she 
has never seen one like it before, and that it now has shrubs in front of it. Councilperson Bruning 
asks whether it should specify below ground level, noting that underground means under the 
surface of the ground. Councilperson Davidson asks whether the guidelines describe a structure 
built like a vault, and Ms. Clifton responds that it is a concrete structure but is still placed below 
ground. Ms. Clifton agrees to change the wording to below ground level. Councilperson 
Davidson asks whether generators can be put underground in a vault. Mr. Nickles states that he 
thinks there is an exhaust issue. Ms. Lee states that she does not think the City has seen an 
underground generator come through.  
 
Councilperson Thorne moves to approve Resolution No. 2026 – II: Design Guidelines. 
Councilperson Sullivan seconds. Approved 4-0.
