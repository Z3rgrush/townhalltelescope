REVIEW OF JUNE 30, 2025 INVESTMENT REPORT 
 
Ms. McDonald presented a review of the June 30, 2025, Investment Report.  The City 
invests idle funds in accordance with strict Kansas investment laws and the City’s 
investment and cash management policy.  As of June 30th, the City held $300 million 
in investments.  The balance held includes City funds, which are the result of City 
activities, such as collection of property tax and sales, tax, but also includes funds the 
City is the custodian of, including escrow funds, trust funds, grant funds, and others.  
The balance also includes funds for encumbered items, and items which the City is 
legally obligated to pay for but have not yet done so.  The City’s total portfolio position 
as of the end of June is $353.4 million, which is about $45 million higher than the 
position the City held at the beginning of 2026.  The cash position accounts for the 
recent receipt of property taxes in June 2026.   
 
In accordance with state law and the City’s Investment Policy, the City’s primary 
concern with investing idle funds is ensuring the preservation of principal and accrued 
interest.  The City pursues only conservative investments and structures such as 
investments based on projected cashflow needs, rather than attempting to tie markets 
to maximize returns.  Due to the conservative nature of the City’s investing and 
practice of holding investments until maturity, the City’s interest yields usually lag the 
market by a substantial amount of time, which produces higher-than-average returns 
during periods of interest rate declines and lower-than-average returns during the 
periods of interest rate hikes.     
 
The first page of the report shows the breakdown of various types of investments at 
the end of the second quarter and the City’s overnight purchase agreements of      
$53.4 million, which earns about 281 basis points.  This investment usually closely 
mirrors the market, as they are short-term investments.  Other investments are long 
term and are expected to lag the market.  The City has $190 million invested in the 
U.S. Treasury and an average rate of 400 basis points, and $70 million invested in 
U.S. Agencies at 420 basis points.  Both of these investments have seen an increase of 
about 20 basis points of investment returns over the last year.  They also have $40 
million invested in CDs at an average rate of 370 basis points.  While investment 
amounts in CDs have doubled since the last year due to changes in state law and 
Governing investments, their investment returns have decreased based on market 
conditions.  Overall, the City’s weighted average yield is approximately 3.85 percent, 
which is slightly higher than the 3.79 percent, which is below the benchmark T-bill 
rate of 4.1 percent.  The City’s investment strategy is to buy investments and they

REVIEW OF MUNICIPAL COURT PRO TEM JUDGES 
 
Interim Presiding Judge Cynthia Cornwell stated according to Resolution No. 5148, 
the presiding judge is required to maintain a list of at least five individuals qualified to 
serve as pro tem judges and present that list to the FAED Committee annually for 
review.  She highlighted the importance of pro tem judge to the courts.  From an 
operational standpoint, pro tem judges ensure uninterrupted court operations.  They 
cover dockets during planned and unplanned leave, such as judicial education, 
vacation, illness, retirement and step in when a judge has a conflict or when they open 
additional dockets to manage high case volume.  Without their service, they would 
experience stagnant dockets and delays that could impact the court’s ability to provide 
timely access to justice.   
 
In 2025, their pro tem judges covered 137 dockets.  So far this year, they have 
handled 197 dockets.  That increase is directly attributable to Judge Dixon’s 
retirement this year.  This year’s list of pro tem judges includes five longstanding pro 
tem judges, specifically, David Bell, Keith Drill, John Duma, Jerry Merrill, and Karen 
Torline.  They also have three well qualified additions to this year’s bench, which 
include Ashley Repp, Sydney Paquette, and Nori Cooper.   
 
Interim Presiding Judge Cornwell presented an overview of the candidates’ 
qualifications.  Six of the attorneys on the list are in private practice, including two 
who also hold regular appointments as municipal judges in the main cities.  Of the 
remaining two attorneys on the list, one serves as a municipal prosecutor and a 
municipal judge, and the other serves as a municipal judge in four surrounding cities.  
She noted long-time pro tem Judge Andrew Talge is no longer on the list, because of 
his recent appointment to fill the vacancy created by Judge Dixon’s retirement.  She 
expressed her gratitude to all of the pro tem judges, as each stepped up to cover 
dockets this year when called upon, resulting in zero disruption to court operations.  
Judge Talge covered a large portion of the docket prior to his appointment, and she 
was grateful for his longstanding commitment to the bench. 
 
Judge Andrew Talge explained he has had the opportunity to serve in every court in 
the metropolitan area and some surrounding counties.  He felt Overland Park had one 
of the finest courts he has seen, as it begins with the leadership with Presiding Judge 
Cornwell, and Court Administrator Jaime Murphy, and flows down to the court staff.

APPOINTMENT OF PRESIDING JUDGE - Cynthia R. Cornwell as Presiding Judge of 
the Overland Park Municipal Court. 
 
Senior Assistant City Attorney Trevor Stiles presented the appointment of Cynthia R. 
Cornwell as the Presiding Judge of the Overland Park Municipal Court.  On July 6, 
2026, the Governing Body approved Cynthia Cornwell as Interim Presiding Judge for 
the Municipal Court.  The City has been without a permanent presiding judge since 
the retirement of former Presiding Judge Ryan Dixon last summer.  Resolution        
No. 5148 provides that the Governing Body designate a presiding judge responsible for 
the daily operation and administration of the judicial function of the Municipal Court.  
Duties of the presiding judge include being responsible for the daily operation of the 
administration of the judicial function, prescribing adoptive rules of practice and 
procedures for the Municipal Court and scheduling dockets for trial and disposition of 
matters before the court.  The presiding judge receives an additional 5 percent in 
compensation during that time for those additional duties. 
 
Judge Cornwell is currently serving as Interim Presiding Judge and was appointed to 
the Overland Park Municipal Court as a judge of Division 1 in May of 2012, and served 
continuously as a full-time judge for Overland Park since then. 
 
Mr. Payne moved to recommend to the Council approval of Judge Cynthia R. Cornwell 
as presiding judge of the Overland Park Municipal Court.  Mrs. Grummert seconded 
the motion, which carried by a vote of 6 to 0.

ORDINANCE NO. TGT-3530 – Levying a Transient Guest Tax of Nine Percent (9%) 
upon the gross receipts derived from or paid by transient guests for sleeping 
accommodations within the City; establishing funds and accounts to account for said 
tax; authorizing the apportionment and expenditures of said tax; and repealing 
Ordinance No. TGT-3295. 
 
Mrs. Gunja presented Ordinance No. TGT-3530, levying a Transient Guest Tax Rate of 
9 percent upon gross receipts derived from or paid by transient guests for sleeping 
accommodations within the City.  Those funds are collected by the state from hotels 
and short-term rentals and are remitted to the City on a quarterly basis.  There are 
currently three funds established to support tourism-related activities.  Ordinance   
No. TGT-3530 would modify the existing ordinance to reduce the number of bonds.  
Transient Guest Tax deposited in the newly-formed Tourism and Promotion Fund will 
provide greater flexibility and can be used to pay the costs related to promotion of 
tourism and convention activities authorized by law, including but not limited to 
funding of destination and marketing services, sports tourism activities and 
investment in strategic assets that generate tourism. 
 
The current City ordinance dictates the percentage of funds that must be dedicated for 
specific purposes each year.  It is unusual for a city ordinance to do this.  For other 
types of special revenue funds, those decisions are made on an annual basis as part of 
the budget process and based on the Council’s priorities at the time.  Providing 
additional flexibility by removing self-limiting language in the TGT ordinance enhances 
the Council’s ability to address their own priorities.  Council priorities are developed 
and evaluated on a regular basis.  Current Council priorities under the government 
accountability area related to this topic include maintaining a responsible and

sustainable fiscal position, maximizing the economic impact in the Convention Center 
and identification of long-range capital and maintenance needs of City facilities.  Some 
of those considerations include TGT, which is to currently support payments of debt 
service for the Soccer Complex and Convention Center.  Debt service for the Soccer 
Complex will be paid off in 2027, and debt service for the Convention Center will be 
paid off in 2030.  As these two facilities that generate tourism continue to age, capital 
investments are needed to stay competitive in the market.  The City is making some of

TERMINATION OF THE QUIVIRA 95 SHOPS COMMUNITY IMPROVEMENT 
DISTRICT 
 
Deputy City Manager Kristy Stallings stated staff was seeking direction from the 
Committee to prepare an ordinance terminating the Quivira 95 Shops Community 
Improvement District (CID).  The City issued bonds in 2014 related to that project, and 
it was the City’s first CID project created in 2011.  This project demolished existing 
retail development in that area and recreated what is at 95th Street and Quivira on 
the northwest corner, which is now a Designer Shoe Warehouse store.  Since there is 
not enough money accumulated to the fees on the bonds on the December 1st payment 
date, staff recommends to proceed with terminating the district so they can stop the 
sales tax effective January 1st.  The state requires one quarter advance notice so they 
can notify retailers.  If they notify the retailers before October 1st, the CID sales tax 
will end on January 1st, and will no longer be collected beginning in 2027.  Staff is 
seeking direction to prepare that ordinance and recommendation from the Committee 
to the Council to approve the ordinance. 
 
Mr. Mitrisin understood the CID did not pay to demolish any buildings.  Mrs. Stallings 
felt regarding demolition, a portion of the eligible costs was demolition.  CID’s have a 
more permissive set of expenditures and can include vertical construction that will be 
privately owned.  This was the first CID project approved in the City, and was before 
they had a CID policy.  The Council, at the time, felt this was a great first project, 
because it completely demolished the old, and is no longer viable retail development, 
which completely redeveloped that corner.  Mr. Mitrisin also noted the project was to 
include onsite infrastructure, pedestrian friendly corner feature, enhanced façade, 
decorative crosswalks, and pedestrian-friendly paths.  Mrs. Stallings stated without 
looking at the ordinance, they kind of did the catch-all process, which is eligible under 
the statute and is different than what they do now with their policy.  Mr. Mitrisin felt 
the corner needed some more life.  He understood they got the $1.7 million collected, 
and felt the timing was off.  Mrs. Stallings stated the CID became effective in 2012, 
and the bonds were issued in 2014.  The CID works by having the life of bonds issued 
that can go for 22 years, and they are not scheduled to mature for several years.  The 
$1.7 million was the principal, because they issued bonds, and the CID sales tax has 
gone toward payment of interest on outstanding principal, as well as them paying 
down the principal.  It will have been more than 20 years that the CID sales tax has 
been in effect, but it will be within the bounds of the law, because bonds were issued 
and the act permits continuation of the CID sales tax for 22 years for the repayment of 
the bonds.  She noted it was well within the life of the CID, and the project has 
performed very well, as it was a complete demolition and construction. 
 
Mrs. Grummert verified the sales tax would go away.  Mrs. Stallings agreed, stating 
there will be 1 percent less paid on sales within those shops. 
 
Mr. Payne moved to direct staff to prepare an ordinance terminating the Quivira 95 
Shops Community Improvement District (CID) effective January 1, 2027, and to also 
recommend approval to the Council of the same.  Mrs. Grummert seconded the 
motion, which carried by a vote of 6 to 0.
