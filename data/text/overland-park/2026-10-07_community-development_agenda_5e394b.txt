 2Overland Park Comprehensive Plan
In 2022, the City of Overland Park launched a process called FrameworkOP 
to create a major update to its comprehensive plan. This community-driven 
process was a detailed update of the City’s previous comprehensive plan to 
respond to current needs, shape future opportunities, integrate best practices, 
and build upon the City’s recent ForwardOP strategic plan. This comprehensive 
plan reflects the community’s intention for where and how Overland Park 
should grow through 2045. It serves as a guide for decision-makers and the 
community regarding future development and many aspects of our city’s quality 
of life.

What is a Comprehensive Plan?
A comprehensive plan sets forth the long-range vision that is used as a community considers issues 
linked to physical development, transportation, housing, parks, environmental resources, economic 
development, community facilities, infrastructure, and other related topics. It includes specific actions 
(additional plans, regulatory updates, programs, and funding) and identifies the responsibilities for 
undertaking those actions. It also contains map-based recommendations that indicate the City’s 
intent for where and how it will use land resources and design infrastructure improvements. The 
plan serves as the foundation for the City’s budgeting process, zoning ordinance, land development 
regulations, capital improvements program, and other ordinances. It is implemented over time 
through many distinct decisions including annual budgeting, departmental work programs, rezonings, 
and subdivisions of land.

Need for New Plan
The previous plan largely benefited the city, notably by generating a positive net fiscal impact from 
development, which has enabled Overland Park to offer exemplary public services and facilities. 
Although the previous plan underwent annual assessments and minor updates, the City recognized 
the necessity for an updated plan to address evolving land use patterns and other critical community 
issues. This revised plan underscores the interrelatedness of various topics and and adopts a more 
contemporary approach to long-range planning, emphasizing design and character over strict land 
use regulations. It also incorporates strategic actions alongside broad policy directives. Additionally, 
this plan presents an opportunity to articulate policies, projects, and programs that Overland Park can 
implement to align its physical environment with the vision outlined in ForwardOP.
As depicted in the planning cycle diagram below, the comprehensive plan should align with 
the strategic goals set by the City Council, offer guidance for implementation - including public 
investments made through the City’s Capital Improvement Plan (CIP) - and undergo regular 
assessment through benchmarking and performance metrics evaluation.

Review and Update Cycle
This comprehensive plan is the first step in the cycle of planning for Overland Park. Updating 
community-wide strategic plans or departmental work plans is the next step to help identify prioritized 
actions and ways to implement the policies and actions of the plan. Those priorities are given 
resources in the budgeting process, in the annual budget, or in a longer-term capital improvement 
plan. With resources in hand, Overland Park and any partners can implement plan priorities through 
the creation and maintenance of projects, policies, and programs. As those changes are made, 
progress can be evaluated and measured. Over time, communities change and the City will need to 
review the plan to check that it still reflects the aspirations of the community. 
The Kansas state enabling statute (12-747, L. 1991, ch. 56, § 7; L. 1997, ch. 147, § 4; May 1) for 
comprehensive plans requires the planning commission to “review or reconsider” a comprehensive 
plan (including any of its elements) at least once annually. This yearly requirement provides the 
opportunity for City staff to conduct an annual review of the plan’s implementation status and report 
the results of this review to the Planning Commission, City Council, and the public at large. 
Moving forward, this review will include a status update on the implementation of the plan, an update 
on the number of development projects that were approved or denied and whether they were 
deemed consistent with the comprehensive plan, and an opportunity to update the quality of life 
metrics dashboard – a recommended action of the plan. A full, in-depth review of the plan should 
take place every five years. At that time, the City will have the opportunity to consider making updates 
and modifications to the plan as needed, based on the plan’s functionality and any relevant market 
changes or new community conditions. If extenuating or unforeseen circumstances or conditions 
arise that require an earlier full review of the plan, the City could choose to exercise that option.

Plan Goals   
Successful community planning requires coordination and integration across planning topics. This 
plan considers the nexus with other planning topics in each chapter and in some instances uses 
cross-references to reinforce these connections. The following six goals describe the highest level of 
desired outcomes for Overland Park.
Quality of Life 
Cultivate an environment of life-long learning and excellent 
community amenities, including parks, gathering places, recreational 
facilities, City services, arts and cultural events, and venues for a 
diverse population. 
Land Use
Guide the development of land and infrastructure using flexible and 
predictable policies and regulations that support revitalization, foster 
strategic growth, enhance stewardship of the natural environment, 
create an appealing community character, and promote the City’s 
long-term fiscal health. 
Economic Well-Being 
Encourage growth in local businesses and job centers that help create 
places and spaces that are attractive to innovative companies and 
their employees, support the economic advancement of residents, 
increase entrepreneurship, and support the City’s fiscal vitality.
Housing
Foster development and maintenance of vibrant neighborhoods with 
a variety of housing types that offer attractive, attainable, and safe 
housing options for current and future residents.
Mobility
Create a safe, efficient, and accessible transportation system that 
serves people of all ages and abilities, with strong connectivity for 
biking and walking and support for public transportation. 
Environment 
Preserve, protect, and sustain natural resources (riparian areas, tree 
canopy, stream corridors, and wildlife habitat), adapt to changing 
climate conditions, and foster long-term resiliency from natural 
disasters and weather events.

Guiding Principles
Quality of Life Guiding Principles
A fundamental guiding principle of this plan is to enhance the quality of life provided in Overland Park. 
While the comprehensive plan is a policy guidance document focused on physical planning, it also 
supports and fosters the following quality of life elements:
 • Cultivating an environment of life-long learning that supports the education of youth, post high 
school education, and graduate studies, as well as later life learning for adults. 
 • Offering high-quality community amenities that serve a diverse population. This includes parks, 
gathering spaces, recreational facilities, City services, and arts and cultural events and venues.
Along with the natural and built environment found in Overland Park, these quality of life elements 
define the experience of Overland Park today and in the future. This comprehensive plan embraces 
these elements and, as applicable, supports their implementation through policies and actions that 
have a linkage to physical planning. 
Sustainable Development Guiding Principles
Overland Park’s vision plan – ForwardOP – charts a future for Overland Park to provide “a high-quality, 
sustainable and attractive built environment offering a variety of choices including neighborhoods 
that offer the opportunity to live, work, and play and establishes Overland Park as a destination 
for business development and family fun.” This plan integrates the ForwardOP sustainability vision 
throughout the plan topic areas. 
As outlined in the City Council’s 2023 Strategic Plan, the City of Overland Park views sustainability 
as reflected in a simple model that consists of three pillars: environmental sustainability, economic 
sustainability, and social sustainability. These three pillars are interrelated and key to the success of 
community well-being.

The City has a variety of plans to guide policy creation and implementation over the years. One of

understanding of important topics that must be pursued to achieve the unified vision for Overland 
Park.

Overview of the Planning Process
The comprehensive plan process fostered discussions on essential community topics. Understanding 
the importance of engagement, the process set out with a goal that everyone who cares about the 
future of Overland Park has the choice to participate. This meant developing a strategy to create 
awareness that people in the city were invited to share their insights and ideas. Using existing City 
communication networks, the process website, Steering Committee guidance, and a variety of in-
person options, FrameworkOP designed a robust plan inviting the community to contribute. These 
contributions were the foundations for developing the plan goals, policies, and actions in the plan. 
Stakeholder Interviews
To kick off the planning process, staff and the consultant team identified key stakeholders to engage. 
The purpose was to gain insight into the available opportunities and potential challenges the City 
should consider as we plan for future growth and development. 
Steering Committee
To guide the comprehensive plan update, the Mayor appointed a 31-member Steering Committee. 
The Steering Committee consisted of a diverse group of community participants, who provided 
guidance on the process and substance of the Plan, served as community advocates, and served as 
stewards of the completed Plan. Members were selected as part of a citywide application process, 
drawing in more than 180 applications. 
The Steering Committee worked closely with City staff, the City’s consultant team, and other 
community participants to bring insight and perspective to the planning process. They helped spread 
the word about the planning effort to encourage community participation. They also advised on the 
plan’s substance, helping staff and the consultant team to integrate the community’s intuition with 
technical analysis. The Steering Committee met ten times during the planning process.

Round 1 Engagement
The first round of engagement was called “Framing 
our Future.” This round was designed to generate 
excitement about the planning process, introduce 
the public to the process, and gather ideas about the 
direction of Overland Park. This round of engagement 
began with an in-person workshop on October 18, 
2022, where approximately 250 people shared their 
ideas. Following the workshop, staff promoted use 
of the tool, Meeting-in-a-Box, to a wide variety of 
community groups and committees. The tool allowed 
people to host FrameworkOP conversations at a 
time and location at their convenience. Additionally, 
the same materials were available online. These 
events gathered feedback from more than 400 people and provided the project team with a fuller 
understanding of residents’ thoughts, ideas, and concerns for the city’s future. The first round helped 
shape the plan’s structure and goals.
Advisory Teams
Four topic-based Advisory Teams were engaged for targeted input: Environmental Resources, Housing 
Choices, Land Use, and Mobility Options. Team members primarily consisted of those who had an 
interest in participating in the Steering Committee and subject matter experts. Approximately 10-14 
members participated on each team. The Advisory Teams worked closely with City staff, the consultant 
team, and other community participants to bring unique knowledge and expertise on specific topics of 
interest. 
The teams were initially tasked with meeting 
three times over the planning process. After 
the first Advisory Team meetings in October 
2022, team members expressed a desire to 
participate in the process more. In addition, 
staff felt a need to work with the teams to 
further identify issues and opportunities in 
the planning process. Therefore, City staff 
facilitated a staff-led engagement effort with 
the Advisory Teams every month from March 
2023 to August 2023 to dive further into 
existing policies, programs, and plans and 
to educate team members on the current

Land Use Alternatives
Following the first round of public engagement 
and the foundational work, the process set 
about “Exploring Land Use Alternatives for 
Future Growth” for two types of potential 
changes in the community: redevelopment of 
existing developed areas and new growth in 
southern Overland Park. 
Using computer modeling software, several 
areas of the city that represent opportunities 
for redeveloping aging commercial, office, and 
institutional developments were assessed. 
Alternative land use and development programs 
were modeled to determine how they would 
impact the city across several dimensions: 
fiscal impacts of development changes, mixing 
uses, open space created, new housing types, 
new jobs created, walkability, transportation 
connectivity, and others. In all cases, the 
modeled redevelopment alternatives provided 
a greater mix of uses, more opportunities for 
creating open spaces, and more support for 
multimodal accessibility (driving, walking, biking, 
taking transit) than currently provided at these 
sites. These modeled alternatives were shared 
and tested with the Overland Park community. 
Generally, the changes modeled to these sites 
were welcomed and supported, with some 
concerns expressed regarding how these 
changes would take place in real life. To address 
these concerns, more detailed policy guidance 
is provided in the character area descriptions 
listed in the Land Use chapter to articulate the 
community’s support for these changes and the 
expectations for how these changes should be 
implemented to meet the community’s vision. 
In addition, new growth alternatives in southern 
Overland Park were modeled in areas south of 
167th Street that are planned for public sanitary 
sewer within the planning horizon (2045). 
Alternatives modeled different character areas 
from rural communities located on very large 
lots, to smaller rural lot sizes, to more traditional 
suburban-scale lots that are found in much of 
Overland Park today. There was support for 
protecting areas that are not planned for sewer 
and allowing for suburban-scale development in 
areas that will have municipal sewer service in 
the future. See page 42 for more information on 
the land use alternatives.
The inputs from these modeling exercises along 
with other public inputs provided throughout 
the process provided the basis for developing 
the Future Development Character Framework 
in this plan.

How to Use the Plan
This comprehensive plan is a policy guide 
that will be used City of Overland Park staff to 
evaluate development proposals, annexation 
requests, and capital investment projects. The 
Planning Commission and City Council will also 
use this plan when making decisions related to 
physical planning in the community. The goals, 
development principles, and policies included 
within this plan will be assessed and referenced 
in staff reports. Elected and appointed officials 
will use this policy guidance to assess whether 
projects support the goals and policies of this plan 
and assess any related trade-offs when making 
important development and policy decisions. 
Using Goals and Development 
Principles 
Goal statements in the plan, listed in each 
topic area chapter, describe the highest level 
of community aspiration and articulate the 
intended outcomes of plan implementation. When 
interpreting additional levels of guidance such 
as policies and actions, the goal statements help 
steer a course toward the intent of the community. 
Development Principles are intended to guide the 
City on future land use planning choices related 
to infill, redevelopment, and new development. 
Like goals, they are meant to steer interpretation, 
however they are more specifically focused on 
building and development.
Using Policies
Policies, listed in each element chapter, express 
a commitment and direction toward achieving 
goals. They are meant to inform decision-making 
and can be assessed as part of staff reports on 
legislatively-approved development cases. The 
direction of the policies led to many of the plan 
actions. Over time, new actions not considered by 
this plan may become possible. In evaluating those 
actions, decision-makers can consider whether 
they advance listed policies. 
Using Actions
Actions are specific implementation tasks for 
the City and its partners. Actions come in the 
form of projects, policies, and programs. Where 
additional resources may be needed to pursue 
an action, it is up to the City’s leadership to 
prioritize actions described in this plan. Sources 
and expenditures of revenue for actions may 
come from City funds, bonds, government 
and private grant programs. The actions may 
be implemented through new public facilities 
or capital improvements. Additional direction 
from City leadership may come from specific 
ordinances or direction to staff that expand on 
the intent of the action. 
Using the Future Development 
Character Framework
The Future Development Character Framework 
is itself a statement of the aspirations of the 
future of the community. It is also reviewed as 
part of development and rezoning processes. 
If a proposal is consistent with the Future 
Development Character Framework, that is 
evidence that the proposal is aligned with the 
vision of the city’s future. 
Over time, conditions in the community will 
change and it may be necessary to consider 
alternative character type applications for 
certain areas. This is appropriate for sites that 
have experienced the following changes:
A. New or enhanced transit service provided 
nearby;
B. Land use changes or increases in 
development intensity on adjacent sites 
that share a property boundary;
C. New public investments or changes in 
transportation or utility infrastructure, 
parks, schools, religious facilities, or other 
public facilities; 
D. Changes in market demand; or
E. Changes that align with the City’s 
Development Principles on page 37.

Policy Guidance
These policies will guide Overland Park in pursuing the Quality of Life goal.
P-QOL.1. Maintain Safe and Healthy Neighborhoods.
Sustain the protection and security of life and property that promotes tranquility in the 
community and peace of mind for the residents.
Overland Park is known for its responsive, professional, and excellent emergency services, and should 
continue this legacy through focused investments. This includes:
A. Ensure the continued safety of the community by providing coordinated and comprehensive all-
hazards response capabilities.
B. Continue to collaborate through balanced regional partnerships while remaining focused on our 
locally established goals and priorities.
C. Leverage incentives to extend City services in a manner that supports established goals and 
development principles.
D. Provide the community a level of police services which ensures all current specialized capabilities 
are maintained. Services provided should demonstrate the Police Department’s commitment to 
the pillars of Procedural Justice; treating all with dignity and respect, giving all a voice, remaining 
neutral and transparent in our actions, and demonstrating trustworthiness.
E. Continue long-established countywide, Kansas City metro-wide, regional and national 
partnerships aimed at providing leading-edge police services.
P-QOL.2. Continue Support for Neighborhoods. 
Leverage and build on existing neighborhood programs to sustain safe and healthy 
neighborhoods. 
The Neighborhood Conservation Program (NCP) and Neighborhood Executive Committee (NEC) 
help maintain the city’s existing neighborhoods. Strong neighborhoods foster connections among 
neighbors to strengthen community bonds and mutual trust with organization and grant-based 
support from the city. These programs meet community needs and support the health and 
sustainability of both people and neighborhoods.

LAND USE
Land use planning establishes the vision for all lands in the city and how they will 
be protected, enhanced, or transformed over time. Like many American cities 
in the previous decades, Overland Park employed a future land use planning 
approach where land is assigned a single use (residential, commercial, industrial, 
etc.) and then largely kept separate based on these uses. This plan introduces 
an approach to land use planning that recognizes the benefits of guiding change 
based on character and design that results in quality places and spaces and 
allows more people to live in closer proximity to services, schools, jobs, and 
entertainment. 
This chapter lays out a character-based approach—the Future Development 
Character Framework. Character types are used to define guidance for distinct 
areas in the city. These character types, along with the policies and actions 
that follow, establish the vision for how the city will develop. This approach will 
help create more walkable, green, sustainable, and resilient developments in 
Overland Park. The implementation of these policies requires an update to the 
City’s Unified Development Ordinance.

Future Development Character Framework
Historically, land development in Overland Park was guided by the Future Development Plan Map and 
corresponding comprehensive plan goals. The previously adopted Future Development Plan map 
guided growth using an approach that designated land uses and densities to different developments 
in Overland Park. This approach was the most common way that suburban communities guided 
development for the last 30+ years.
Today, many communities are shifting from a focus on new development to infill/redevelopment 
and are experiencing changing real estate market trends. They have found the previous approach to 
not be supportive of creating walkable, connected, sustainable, and vibrant places - largely because 
the previous approach does not guide the way places are designed or allow mixed-use. It can result 
in developments that are not connected, do not support active modes of travel such as biking 
and walking, do not maximize development potential or public infrastructure capacity, and reduce 
opportunities for social interaction. 
As a result, the planning profession has evolved to consider land uses as part of a larger framework 
of places in communities with similar block patterns, building forms, street networks, and land uses. 
Called “character types,” this approach provides the following benefits:
 • Focus on design – The experience and function of a place is largely determined by its design. 
A rural single-family home does not have the same character as one located in a downtown 
neighborhood. This approach focuses more on the design of places and manifests the types of 
character that is intended for different parts of the community. 
 • Flexibility for uses – Market dynamics have changed over time, requiring greater flexibility for 
designating land uses, particularly for commercial and employment areas. Setting out a planning 
framework that supports flexibility of uses increases opportunities for redevelopment of aging 
developments and provides more opportunities to leverage existing infrastructure capacity.
 • Foster mixed-use development – Demand for walkable and vibrant places has resulted in 
more support for mixing uses within a single site. Mixed-use development is generally defined as 
having residential and non-residential uses within a common development. With an intentional 
design focus, addressing concerns about co-locating different land uses can be successfully 
achieved. The Future Development Character Framework guides where mixed-use development is 
appropriate.
 • Framework for coordinating design of development and transportation facilities – 
This approach addresses not only the design of new or redeveloped buildings, but also general 
guidance for the design and location of transportation facilities that may support those structures, 
such as sidewalks, bike or multi-use paths, greenways, transit stops, and streets.

land use in each alternative, this exercise also 
considered and evaluated the transportation 
impacts and fiscal impacts of each alternative. 
Generally, the findings from this engagement 
showed that the community was supportive 
of having more connected, walkable, mixed-
use places in Overland Park that include green 
spaces and are designed with sustainability 
in mind. This feedback was used to evolve 
the character types from describing current 
conditions to describing the vision for how 
different areas of the city will transform over 
time. 
This plan’s set of character types includes a 
hierarchy of areas from the most intensive 
developments that should be targets for 
future improved transit service and the highest 
intensities of development (e.g., Regional Activity 
District), to lower order centers that are likely to 
continue to be primarily served by automobiles 
within the foreseeable future (e.g., Commercial 
Hub). Within this hierarchy, there are many 
areas with potential for infill and redevelopment. 
The character types allow flexibility for mixed-
use to support redevelopment efforts, and the 
building blocks articulate the general character 
of these sites to guide future changes. 
For new growth areas in southern Overland 
Park, the Suburban Neighborhood category 
has been assigned to existing subdivisions 
with lot sizes under 10 acres and to areas that 
are projected to have sanitary sewer service 
within the plan horizon - 2045. All other areas 
south of 167th Street are designated as Rural 
Transition Zone. The intention is for these areas 
to maintain a rural development pattern with a 
minimum of 10 acres or more, facilitating their 
transition from Rural Transition to Suburban 
Neighborhood once public sanitary sewer 
service becomes available. The larger 10-acre 
lot size offers flexibility to subdivide into smaller 
lots in the future.

The updated Future Development Character

Future Development Character Framework Map and Character 
Types
The Future Development Character Framework map, included in the Appendix, and the following 
character types, comprise the Future Development Character Framework. The character types include 
a broad list of uses along with associated definitions that are referenced within each of the character 
types. Each character type contains a list of appropriate uses that may currently exist or could exist in 
the future. They are organized into two categories: preferred and supported uses.
Preferred and Supported Uses
 • Preferred Uses - The dominant land uses that serve as the main focus 
of the development and inform the building design, site layout, and overall 
character of an area.
 • Supported Uses - The supporting and complementary land uses that are 
compatible with the preferred use but are not the main focus of the area. 
Setting out preferred and supported uses for each character type provides flexibility for new 
construction and redevelopment to be guided by market factors, while also achieving a level of 
certainty for the character of the area as it relates to selected land uses. When a project is proposed 
to include a supported use(s), City Staff, the Planning Commission, and City Council can evaluate 
whether the project aligns with the character intent for the area. Criteria for evaluating proposed 
supported uses includes: 
 • Considering the overall character type area where the project is proposed (not just the project 
site), would the supported use’s character and design complement existing or potential future 
preferred uses, or would it overly dominate and define the area’s character?
 • Does the proposed design of the supported use align with the character type, and will it adhere 
to building blocks of that character type?
For instance, a single hotel proposed in a Regional Activity District could be deemed an appropriate 
supported use if the area isn’t already dominated by this type of use, and if the hotel is designed to 
prioritize pedestrian access and integrates with street frontages, thereby supporting the aim for a 
more vibrant and walkable mixed-use center. Conversely, a hotel constructed using a suburban design 
approach with permanent surface parking and lacking pedestrian connections to adjacent uses would 
not be suitable.
