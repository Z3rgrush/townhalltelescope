Beginning in 2026, waste haulers became eligible to receive reimbursement for the costs of 
providing recycling services to residents, providing an opportunity to ensure residents 
receive free curbside recycling. 
Currently, the City regulates waste haulers through licensing requirements in the 
Westminster Municipal Code (W.M.C) Title V (Licensing and Regulations), Chapter 7 (Solid 
Waste Collection). These requirements stipulate that haulers must offer trash and recycling 
services to residents. Curbside compost collection is currently not required to be offered. 
The City has an opportunity to better align the W.M.C. and our waste hauler licensing 
requirements with PRP to ensure free recycling services are provided to residents and to 
increase Westminster’s community-wide waste diversion. 
Three updates to the W.M.C have been identified for City Council consideration and 
feedback. Each option builds upon the prior. These options are:
1. Amend the hauler licensing code to: 
Require haulers to provide resident recycling services free of charge, and 
Provide documentation of free recycling services provided, and
Clarify reporting requirements to better attain waste diversion data and 
assess progress of the program  
2. Require waste haulers to offer compost services, potentially through a phased 
approach, providing services to additional entities over time. 
3. Require a volume-based pricing or “Pay-As-You-Choose” (PAYC) pricing structure 
for residential curbside waste collection services.
Two additional potential updates to the W.M.C. include further alignment with the Plastic 
Pollution Reduction Act (PPRA) by explicitly adding the fine schedule outlined in the PPRA, 
and the implementation of a “Skip the Stuff” ordinance to require businesses to have an 
opt-in model for throw-away utensils and service items in take-out food orders. 
These proposed code updates build upon the results of a capstone project completed in 
2025 by the University of Colorado (CU) Boulder’s Master’s of the Environment (MENV) 
program. The results of this project were provided to City Council in the Information Only 
memo “Options for Increased Waste Diversion in Westminster” on March 9, 2026.

Background Information:
Westminster has an open-market waste collection system with nine licensed residential waste 
haulers. The W.M.C. regulates waste haulers through licensing requirements. Haulers’ licenses are 
renewed on a yearly basis. This process offers the most direct method by which the City regulates the 
local waste collection and hauling system. Currently the W.M.C. requires haulers to offer both 
recycling and waste curbside collection services. Recycling collection is not required to be provided 
by default with trash collection services or subscribed to by residents. Instead, recycling is offered as 
an additional service at a cost. In addition, compost collection is not required to be offered or provided 
by haulers to residents.
In 2025, the City partnered CU Boulder’s MENV program to identify opportunities to improve 
residential recycling access and affordability and community-wide waste diversion. To do so, MENV 
researchers analyzed surrounding municipalities’ waste management systems, identified best 
practices, completed expert interviews, and conducted community outreach with input from over 200 
residents. The graduate students identified several high-impact, easy-to-implement opportunities to 
improve residents’ access to recycling and that bolster community-wide waste diversion. These 
opportunities include:
1. Increase community education and outreach
2. Update the W.M.C. hauler licensing section to require: 
a. Free recycling for residents
b. Offer of compost/organic recycling collection services 
c. Volume-based waste management pricing
3. A construction and deconstruction ordinance and program for new development and 
redevelopment
4. Increase staffing to support these waste reduction and diversion initiatives 
These results were provided to City Council in the Information Only memo “Options for Increased 
Waste Diversion in Westminster” on March 9, 2026. These recommendations are underpinned by the 
funding and resources available to the City and haulers through the PRP. 
The PRP, created through House Bill 22-1355, increases access to and affordability of recycling 
services. PRP shifts the cost of recycling from residents to the companies that produce and sell 
packaging. Projected PRP benefits include an increase in Colorado’s paper and packaging recycling 
rate from 25 percent to 58 percent. It also projects 7,900 new direct and indirect green jobs, realizing 
an additional $31 million in annual wages while avoiding greenhouse gas emissions equal to taking 
278,000 cars off the road each year. PRP reimburses waste haulers for the costs of recycling 
collection services, reimburses the operators of recycling drop-off centers for operational costs, and 
provides cities funding for education and outreach.
Most importantly, PRP’s reimbursements allow haulers to provide curbside recycling collection 
services for free. Haulers’ residential services are reimbursed beginning in 2026 and reimbursements 
for non-residential entities begin in 2028. (See Attachment 1 and Attachment 2 for more details on

PRP). Of note, waste haulers opt into PRP, electing to receive reimbursement for providing recycling 
services. Haulers that do not participate in PRP may continue to charge residents for recycling 
services and not receive reimbursement through the program. Failing to ensure all haulers provide 
recycling services for free may advance cost inequities for Westminster residents. 
Activating the opportunities identified by the MENV researchers and utilizing PRP resources, the City 
has an opportunity to modernize Westminster’s waste collection system and ensure free recycling 
services for residents. This effort will achieve the City’s Strategic Plan outcome 3.1. by exploring the 
implementation of a citywide recycling and composting program and advance progress on the City’s 
Sustainability Plan target to improve the community-wide waste diversion rate. The key action the 
City can take to achieve these goals is to update the W.M.C.’s waste hauler licensing requirements. 
Three options have been identified for City Council’s consideration and direction. 
1.Require haulers to provide resident recycling services free of charge to residents. This 
requirement would be achieved through amending W.M.C. Title V (Licensing and Regulations), 
Chapter 7. Haulers would provide documentation of free recycling services delivered to 
residents during the licensing renewal and approval process. The amendment would also 
clarify reporting requirements to better attain waste diversion data and assess impact.
2.Require haulers to offer curbside compost services. Curbside compost collection could be 
done through a phased approach. For example, waste haulers could be required to provide 
compost pickup services to single-family units within a year, then require that compost services 
be offered to multi-family properties and eventually offered to food-based businesses. Similar 
requirements are already live in Denver, Boulder, and Longmont. Increasing the utilization of 
composting is imperative, as organic materials including wasted food, food scraps, and yard 
trimmings account for roughly 37 percent of landfilled material in Colorado. In addition to 
compostable materials taking up precious space in landfills with capacity constraints, organics 
decomposing in a landfill without oxygen release methane, a greenhouse gas that is 80 times 
more potent than carbon dioxide. Moreover, Colorado’s agricultural lands are rapidly losing 
topsoil, which is reducing agricultural productivity, degrading water quality and the soil’s ability 
to hold and filter water, which increases erosion and damages ecosystems. High-quality 
compost can reduce soil degradation, thereby addressing these climate and agricultural 
issues, creating an end market for the finished compost. 
3.Require a volume-based pricing system, also known as a Pay-As-You-Choose (PAYC) 
pricing structure for residential curbside trash collection services. In a PAYC system, 
waste haulers charge households for trash collection based on the amount they throw away, 
changing trash disposal from a flat fee into one respective to the size of their trash bin. The 
direct financial savings that can be achieved creates a strong incentive for residents to 
produce less trash, recycle more, and compost. Typical PAYT pricing systems offer recycling 
and compost services bundled for free with no cost differential for the size of the selected 
recycling and/or composting bins. Such systems are common and already in place in Arvada, 
Boulder, Erie, Fort Collins, Golden, Lafayette, Longmont, and Loveland. See Attachment 2: 
Residential Waste Diversion Report (CU Boulder) and the Staff Presentation for a complete 
analysis of how the City’s waste collection system compares to our neighboring jurisdictions. 
To understand the potential impact and feasibility of the proposed updates to the W.M.C., Staff 
surveyed Westminster’s licensed waste haulers. The four haulers most utilized by residents have 
already begun the contracting process to receive reimbursement for residential recycling services

In addition to updating waste hauler licensing, two additional waste diversion opportunities can be 
realized by adding clear support of the PPRA in the W.M.C. The first opportunity is to bolster current 
enforcement provisions of the PPRA. The PPRA, House Bill 21-1162, phased out single-use plastic 
carryout bags, created a 10-cent fee for recycled paper bags, and banned polystyrene (Styrofoam) 
food containers across the state. Current code exhibits City support of business compliance with this 
State law. Staff cannot enforce fines outlined in the PPRA until they are explicitly added to the 
W.M.C.
While the City has leveraged the City's Save. Act. Grow. Earn (SAGE) Sustainable Business Program 
to educate businesses on compliance requirements, the City cannot utilize the fine structure detailed 
in the PPRA to motivate those businesses that are aware of the requirements but have chosen not to 
comply. The PPRA authorizes local government to enforce against a violation of the act by imposing 
a civil penalty against a store or retail food establishment of up to $500 for a second violation or up to 
$1,000 for a third or subsequent violation. As of July 1, 2024, a local government may enact, 
implement, or enforce an ordinance, resolution, rule, or charter provision that is as stringent as or 
more stringent than the requirements set forth in the act. Through adding this language on 
enforcement and fines, the City has an opportunity to actively enforce the PPRA locally. This 
enforcement would only result in fines after repeated communication, education, and support from the 
City’s SAGE program. 
The second additional waste diversion opportunity focuses on reducing take-out food waste. "Skip the 
Stuff,” a State amendment to the PPRA proposed earlier this year, serves as a legal blueprint to 
reduce single-use waste in food establishments. A local “Skip the Stuff” ordinance would ban food 
establishments from automatically providing utensils and condiment packets in take-out orders. 
Instead, customers would have to ask for these items. This benefits small businesses through 
monetary savings on takeout cutlery and condiments and supports responsible resource usage, 
reducing unnecessary single-use plastic waste. The SAGE program would liaise with the business 
community, supporting communication, education, and implementation. 
To support in the education, outreach, and implementation of the potential opportunities outlined, the 
City has partnered with Eco-Cycle. Eco-Cycle is a 50-year-old national leader in recycling and waste 
diversion programs, education, and engagement. Eco-Cycle may support the roll out of code changes 
through creating communication materials for residents and waste haulers, managing a school 
education program, and running a community recycling ambassador program. Eco-Cycle can also 
support work with the implementation of the PPRA enforcement and a “Skip the Stuff” ordinance. 
PRP provides reimbursement to the City for the education and outreach activities conducted by Eco-
Cycle, augmenting staff capacity for no additional cost.
The presented options involve no financial cost to the City or residents, while creating systematic 
change and guaranteeing, at a minimum, free recycling for residents. Updates to the W.M.C. bring 
alignment with the City’s Strategic Plan outcome and tasks, specifically 3.1, which is to "explore 
implementation of a citywide recycling and composting program." In total, updating the W.M.C offers 
a simple, low-lift way to align the City with State priorities, reduce resident waste disposal costs, and 
increase access to PRP resources, all while increasing responsible environmental resource usage.

PROJECT BACKGROUND 
The City of Westminster (‘City’) is exploring waste diversion initiatives, specifically
initiatives that increase the residential waste diversion rate. Westminster’s
Sustainability Office partnered with three graduate student consultants from the
Masters of the Environment (MENV) program at the University of Colorado
Boulder (‘Team’). Tara Sullivan and Kate Williams are specializing in Urban
Resilience and Sustainability while Lindsay Knight is specializing in
Environmental and Natural Resources Policy. The City benefits from the
additional capacity to take on waste systems research and stakeholder
engagement through this collaboration to explore waste diversion within
Westminster.  
The consultants generated recommendations following ten months of project
development and research. Background research focused on state-level policy as
well as waste initiatives and strategies from surrounding jurisdictions.
Informational interviews with waste experts and sustainability staff were
conducted during June and July 2025. Community engagement was conducted
throughout the life of the project via a resident survey. 
This report details Westminster’s current residential waste profile, explains the
different waste systems in Colorado, covers waste system lessons and successes
of Front Range municipalities, highlights four Front Range municipalities as case
studies, and shows the importance of community engagement. Near-term
recommendations for improved waste diversion in the residential sector include
an additional full-time staff member to specialize in waste, a data tracking
software to develop a comprehensive waste profile, updated hauler licensing,
ordinance updates including mandated free recycling and a volume-based waste
structure, improved compost accessibility, and utilization of state funding for
improved waste systems.

CURRENT RESIDENTIAL WASTE PROFILE IN COLORADO
AND WESTMINSTER 
Colorado  
Colorado has set statewide municipal solid waste diversion goals of 35% by 2026
and 45% by 2036.  However, in 2024, Colorado only diverted 15.7% of its waste
from landfills, less than half the national average of 32.2%.  
1
2
Colorado residents generate 6.4 pounds of waste per person per day, which is
comparable to what other state residents generate.  However, Colorado recycles
less material than other states in the nation with 1.0 pounds per person per day.
Oregon and Washington residents recycle 3.1 pounds per person per day and 1.9
pounds per person per day, respectively.  Waste diversion and reduction are
important as municipalities, companies, and people who do not recycle or
compost send their waste to landfills where the materials are unable to be
revitalized and reused. Landfills operate on land that could be used for housing,
open space, or wildlife habitat.  Landfills contribute to the release of methane
gas, which occurs when organic matter breaks down with little access to oxygen
in the air.  Municipalities can adopt more sustainable practices to protect their
communities and the environment by diverting waste from the landfill through
recycling and composting. Composting facilities can convert organic material
into usable compost, and recycling facilities process multiple material types
before sending them out to end markets.

Westminster has an extensive waste management portfolio. As of late 2025,
there are 10 residential waste haulers that operate within the City. Westminster
has an open market system for waste hauling, meaning any licensed hauler can
operate in the City. The City attempted to reduce the number of haulers with an
ordinance for a municipally contracted waste system in 2017, but this measure
failed before going to a vote with City Council. 
To gain a better understanding of the effort in 2017 to move towards a
municipally contracted hauler, the Team spoke with Jen Prehn, the current
Business Operations Manager for the City. In 2017, Prehn worked closely on the
contracted hauling attempt.  
Prehn noted many reasons why this measure failed. Several waste hauling
companies organized a misinformation campaign as they were concerned about
losing customers if Westminster moved to a municipally contracted system. The
misinformation campaign swayed the City Council to believe the ordinance was
not a viable solution for waste services in Westminster. There were political
concerns regarding how support or opposition of this measure would impact
Councilors’ future campaigns for office. Proponents of municipally contracted
hauling were less vocal and present at the public meetings in contrast to the
opponents of the measure who were extremely vocal. This historical context will
influence future changes to Westminster’s waste system. Please see page 26 for
more information on how this background influences the Team’s
recommendations and how the City can address waste management through
various avenues. 
The Westminster Municipal Code Title V (Licensing and Regulations), Chapter 7
(Solid Waste Collection) outlines the existing requirements for waste hauler
service providers (‘haulers’). Per Westminster Municipal Code § 5.7.8 (Recycling
Requirement), “haulers shall offer [curbside] recycling as an option to residents
and commercial entities who desire such services”.  Since the ordinance only
requires recycling be provided as an option, residents are not required to have
recycling and instead have to opt in to the service. This can pose an issue if
recycling is not feasible given physical space constraints (like in multi-family
housing), recycling services are not well marketed by the hauler meaning
residents are not aware of the option, or the additional cost of recycling services
deter residents.

The ordinance does not outline requirements for curbside organics. Composting
services are not offered by any trash and recycling providers licensed within the

compost companies.

(C&D) sectors currently. There are no C&D recycling requirements within
Westminster as of late 2025.

Westminster must accurately track the waste generated across the three sectors
(residential, commercial, and C&D) to achieve any diversion target. All haulers are
currently required to report annual waste data to the City by March 31 each year.
The report requests haulers provide the following information: (1) what types of
customers the hauler provides services for; (2) where the hauler brought the
recycled materials; (3) confirmation that the hauler offered a list of materials the
City determines for recycling such as aluminum, paper, cardboard, etc.; (4)
description of how the hauler notified customers of the materials they collect;
and (5) details of collection services including the days of the week and
frequency of pick-up. For 2025, six out of 10 haulers provided the above
information. Some haulers provided additional information including vehicles,
pricing, and tons of material collected (trash and recycling). While all 10 haulers
provide aspects of the requested documentation to the City, the reports range in
completeness from a full report with all information included to reports with
entire blank sections or even no data submitted. The discrepancy in data
submitted by haulers causes confusion as to what haulers need to provide and
creates gaps in the data. There is currently no data verification process beyond
the hauler’s signature on the submitted report to the City Clerk’s office. The
deputy City Clerk confirmed there is a lack of enforcement for the submission
deadlines. These discrepancies call for a reexamination of waste data tracking in
Westminster. Without accurate data providing a baseline for total waste
generated, the City cannot set realistic and achievable goals. 
WASTE SYSTEMS 
Colorado has several different residential waste management systems. The Team
focused on 12 Front Range municipalities based on their population and
geographic similarities to Westminster as well as the efforts each municipality
has taken to improve waste management. The selection process enabled the
Team to accurately understand how waste systems operate and create
recommendations that fit Westminster’s needs. The Team defines the different
waste systems, includes how the systems operate, and provides examples of
Colorado jurisdictions that use each waste system. 
Open Market 
This is a voluntary, opt-in, and subscription-based waste system with commercial
haulers running the waste services.  Often this system has no requirements for
recycling, which puts the responsibility onto the residents to opt into recycling.

Westminster is comprised of 49.4% Homeowners Associations (HOA) as of 2025,

consideration, which requires a connection with the HOAs ahead of time.

CASE STUDIES 
The Team interviewed 12 municipalities as models for waste improvements for
Westminster, with four communities standing out as particularly pertinent
examples. These case studies provide valuable insight into how Westminster can
improve their waste system and address the needs of residents. 
Arvada
Arvada moved from open market hauling to municipally contracted hauling for
residential waste of both single and multi-family homes in 2021. They
experienced an exponential rise in calls and complaints with residents asking
about the impacts of the waste system change during this process. Residents
were confused by the division of responsibility between the city and the
contracted hauler. Residents called the City of Arvada to document complaints
that their trash was not picked up even though Republic Services was in control
of the hauling operations. Arvada’s Utility Services staff of seven pulled 20 people
from different City of Arvada departments to assist in handling the influx of calls
and resident feedback. Westminster’s Sustainability Office contrasts Arvada’s full-
time staff of seven with their full-time staff of three. Careful planning and conflict
management strategies will set Westminster up for success when dealing with a
waste management change at this scale. 
Arvada has operated under this waste system for four years and the City of
Arvada staff can now measure the impact. Opt-out fees are crucial for the RFP
process in a municipally contracted system as haulers will not want to bid on a
city program that cannot offer a minimum profit margin. Opt-out fees are
typically the price of the lowest service the hauler offers. The fee protects the
resident’s freedom to choose an alternative hauler, satisfying some residents’
concerns about a municipally contracted system. Arvada set their opt-out fee low
at $6.24 a month. Comparatively, other Front Range municipalities set their opt
out fee higher like Fort Collins’ $11.00 per month opt-out fee, which is equivalent
to the fee for the lowest volume cart size offered through their waste system.
The low opt-out fee is convenient enough for the average Arvada resident to pay,
in addition to paying for another hauler of the resident’s choosing for waste
services. This undermined the waste system Arvada worked so hard to create and
landed the City of Arvada with an opt-out rate of 30.23%.  Westminster will need
to set a higher fee to lower the opt-out rate if pursuing a municipally contracted
hauling system.

Only 52% of households reported having curbside recycling pickup (single-family
home) or a communal recycling receptacle (multi-family complex). Licensed
haulers are required to offer recycling, but are not required to advertise the
service or offer it at an accessible rate to their customers. The responsibility is on
the resident to opt-in to the service since recycling is not mandated. One
household noted their hauler does not offer recycling in the ‘other’ option for
question 5. The lack of advertising for haulers recycling services comes at the
detriment of residents believing the option does not exist for them.  
Question 6 - How aware are you of the guidelines on materials that are
accepted for recycling by your hauler?  
This question aimed to determine the awareness and understanding of recycling
requirements prior to the implementation of PRP. Funding for recycling
education from PRP will increase access and availability, leading more residents
learn about recycling requirements. More information about PRP can be found in
Appendix A. 
Question 7 - Do you currently compost your kitchen food scraps and/or yard
waste? 
Organics recycling services are difficult to source in Westminster. Small boutique
composting companies operate in Westminster, however, residents have to add
food scraps and yard waste collection on as a service in addition to the hauler
they select for the general waste stream. Of the households surveyed, 61% do not
have any way to compost at home. Some noted they use Westminster-sponsored
free yard waste events, which effectively engage residents in organic recycling. Of
the 27% of respondents that reported recycling organics at home, 22% of
respondents compost in their backyard and only 5% use organic curbside
recycling.

Question 8 - Do you support the City of Westminster exploring options that may
increase the residential recycling rate? Examples of options include free curbside
recycling, preferred haulers or a single hauler that meet quality service
requirements.  
Out of 201 respondents, 78% selected ‘Yes’ when asked if they would support the
City of Westminster exploring initiatives to increase residential recycling rates. Of
the 9% of households that selected ‘other’, responses included, ‘Yes except for
single hauler’ and ‘Only if it doesn’t mean there will be no choice in trash
companies’, while others mentioned they would support depending on the
selected business. Residents are interested in increased recycling access in
Westminster, but are concerned about the City imposing on their freedom in the
process.  
Question 9 - Do you support the City of Westminster looking into options to
support residents’ access to curbside pickup services for compostable materials?
(These may include leaves, lawn clippings, food scraps, and more) 
The majority of respondents do not compost as mentioned in the analysis of
question seven. However, 84% noted they would support initiatives to increase
residents access to curbside organic recycling. This presents an opportunity for
Westminster to divert a significant amount of material from the landfill with
great interest from residents to participate in the effort.  
Question 10 - OPTIONAL: If you could design your dream waste system, what is
one thing it would entail? For example - fewer trash trucks in my neighborhood,
smaller trash bins and larger recycling bins, community competitions that
reward those who recycle the most without contamination, or robots that sort
my waste for me.  
The optional open-ended question received 109 responses from households
requesting a wide variety of support from the City. Many responses included a
desire for smaller trash bins and larger recycling bins. One respondent wrote,
‘Different size trash and recycling bins. Price of service less for using smaller sized
bins’, which accurately describes a volume-based waste system (PAYT). Many
noted a preference for fewer trash trucks while others mentioned they want their
waste system to stay the same. Please see Appendix B.3 for the full list of
responses to question 10. 
Conclusion 
The residential survey provided key insight into residents’ attitudes towards their
current waste system and highlighted areas of opportunity for Westminster to
provide support such as expanded recycling access and added organics
recycling. Residents tend to value the freedom they have in selecting their
hauler, but would like the opportunity to pay less for generating less waste. Most
respondents do not have curbside composting and about half have curbside
recycling services, but are interested in the City exploring initiatives to increase
their access to these services.

RECOMMENDATIONS AND TOOLKIT
The Team identified recommended actions for the City to implement to improve
waste diversion. These pathways are organized based on the amount of effort
required to implement the given action, from lowest to most effort required by
City staff. 
Additional Staff  
The Team recommends the City hire additional full-time staff to support in waste
diversion and reduction activities, specifically to implement residential waste
system changes that achieve diversion targets. All 15 interviewed municipalities
mentioned the large workload required for current staff to work with City
leadership, engage with the community and haulers, and update the waste
system. The municipalities spoke about utilizing other departments or hiring
consultants in order to manage the work. Westminster should use additional
staff to implement the recommended actions such as organics recycling,
education and outreach, etc. 
Education and Outreach 
Continued education and outreach will help the City increase community
understanding and support for recycling and composting. Residents are
interested in waste initiatives based on residents’ experiences the Team gathered
from the community engagement survey. The City should continue tabling at
public events and interacting with Westminster residents to understand how the
City can make positive changes for the community. Additionally, the Producer
Responsibility Program rolling out in January 2026 will provide residents with
free recycling, giving the City a way to educate and connect with residents on
how the program impacts the community. PRP creates a unique opportunity for
the City to increase support for and understanding of recycling. Education and
outreach can lead to a better understanding of residents’ wants and improved
outcomes for sustainability initiatives. 
Data Tracking 
It is recommended the City partner with Re-TRAC, a waste tracking software, to
accurately capture trash and recycling tonnage in Westminster. This
recommendation is based on research and conversations with waste tracking
software companies as well as interviews with municipalities across the Front
Range who use Re-TRAC in their everyday work. See the Waste Data Tracking
Report for more details.

Licensing Updates 
The current Westminster ordinance allows for a software like Re-TRAC to be
used. An update to the ordinance should enforce strict data tracking within the
licensing and reporting process and require haulers to report the trash and
recycling they collect curbside from residents. In 2025, five of the 10 haulers in
Westminster did not report their trash tonnage, despite ordinance requirements.
Requiring the reporting of trash and recycling tonnage will enable Westminster
to have a comprehensive diversion baseline and accurately capture trends in the
waste system. Submission of hauler’s reporting can be required on a monthly,
quarterly, or annual basis and would be verified by City staff. Clarifying the timing
of data submission would also be beneficial to prevent any hauler confusion. See
the Waste Data Tracking Report for more details. 
 
Ordinance Updates 
The City should update its current ordinance to include mandated free recycling
and volume-based waste management to expand access to recycling and
increase cost effectiveness for residents. 
Mandated free recycling
Ensuring equity and price transparency is a strong justification for waste
management improvements, and cost transparency resonates with residents as
a public benefit. A mandated free recycling system requires haulers to provide
free curbside recycling to all residents, removing the burden from residents to
opt in to a recycling service. Residents noted they cannot afford recycling, or their
hauler does not properly advertise recycling services. A mandated free recycling
system could be initiated through the update of the Recycling Requirement in
the City of Westminster ordinance. A change to the City ordinance would require
a vote from City Council. Mandated free recycling in Westminster would help the
City prepare for the Producer Responsibility Program. Mandated free recycling
would incentivize the haulers operating in Westminster to opt-in since the PRP is
an opt-in system for haulers rather than a requirement. 
 
Volume-based waste management
Implementing PAYT increases the cost effectiveness of waste management
services and increases residents’ access to recycling and other waste services.
Residents have the freedom to choose the size trash bin that best fits their needs
with recycling included in the cost. A PAYT system could be initiated by updating
the Recycling Requirement in the City of Westminster City ordinance. A change
to the City ordinance would require a vote from City Council.  
One consideration when moving to a PAYT system is to include compost within
PAYT fees as it could satisfy the desire for residents to have increased access to
organics recycling without moving to more intensive waste systems like
municipally contracted hauling.

Compost  
Westminster residents are interested in the City exploring initiatives to increase
resident access to curbside organic recycling (compost). Compost is an important
way to increase diversion from the landfill and engage residents in their waste
systems.
The Team recommends the City explore developing a pilot program with
businesses or residential homes in a set block radius to provide them access to
composting. City staff could work with an organics recycling facility and haulers
such as A1, Western Disposal, Compost Colorado, or Wompost to haul the
compost.
 
Westminster Open Space Office currently offers multiple seasonal drop-off
events for Christmas trees, tree leaves, tree limbs, and yard waste. Given the high
demand for these events, the City should look into expanding the hours and days
of the existing yard waste events to increase diversion of organics from the
landfill. 
Funding Opportunities
 
Circular Action Alliance
Circular Action Alliance (CAA) will support municipalities as the Producer
Responsibility Program rolls out in 2026. Westminster can secure funding from
CAA, which will pay cities on a money per household basis to provide education
and outreach. Below is a draft list provided by CAA of the covered education and
outreach : 62
1.Direct mailers or monthly email updates to households. 
2.Presentations at public meetings and community events. 
3.Delivery of K-12 lessons on waste and recycling in local schools. 
4.Train-the-trainer sessions to equip individuals with the knowledge, tools and
resources necessary to train others to correctly recycle and compost. 
5.One-on-one outreach to households. This may include door hanger
campaigns, cart tagging, or other initiatives approved by CAA Colorado. 
6.Paid media campaigns. This may include print, streaming, broadcast, digital
or social media. 
7.Use of community-based social marketing (CBSM) by engaging with local
groups to encourage positive recycling behaviors. CBSM emphasizes
understanding the barriers and motivations that influence people's actions
and works to overcome those obstacles through targeted, community-
specific interventions. Sample projects could be technical assistance for Multi-
Family Premises with distinct contamination challenges or a targeted door-
to-door initiative in a neighborhood with low recycling participation. 
8.Engaging local and regional community-based organizations to develop and
share relevant and relatable messaging via dynamic storytelling. 
9.Other educational initiatives that align with Colorado’s EPR Program Plan
and are approved by CAA Colorado.

Colorado Circular Communities (C3) 
Colorado Circular Communities (C3) Enterprise is a statewide program run by the
Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment to support
communities, businesses, nonprofits, schools, and tribes transition to a circular
economy. The program is funded by user fees (tipping fees) at Colorado landfills.
C3 offers funding and assistance for local governments through the Strategic
Technical Expertise for the Public Sector (STEPS) program. The STEPS program
provides financial and technical assistance for a variety of projects related to the
circular economy with several types of support depending on the project scale.
Westminster is pursuing STEPS project funding for the expansion of multi-family
housing waste collection space and enclosures to accommodate trash and
recycling. This expansion project will aid Westminster in increasing recycling
access for residents and prepare for the PRP. See C3’s STEPS program webpage
for more information on C3 and the STEPS program. 
Municipally Contracted Hauling 
Westminster can achieve the long-term goal of municipally contracted hauling if
the political climate presents itself and the City Council has made waste
initiatives a priority. The Team recommends the City pursue municipally
contracted hauling once there is support from City Council for a waste system
change of this scale. Westminster can successfully make progress on its waste
diversion by taking the incremental steps outlined in the recommendations
above to support this long-term goal of municipally contracted hauling. 
It became clear throughout the municipal interviews that moving from an open
market to municipally contracted system is labor and time intensive. The most
recent municipally contracted hauling examples on the Front Range are Arvada
(2021), Broomfield (2025), and Fort Collins (2024), with all three detailing the
importance of gaining community support, a knowledge of waste systems, and
understanding the immediate and prolonged impacts on residents. The City
must acquire strong City Council support, have residents who are informed and
involved in the process, and have a prepared City staff if and when Westminster
pursues municipally contracted hauling.
