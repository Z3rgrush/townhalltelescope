Background Information:
In Spring 2026 the City commissioned a Childcare Market Study to gain better understanding of the 
state of the childcare industry sector, the current needs and services available to Westminster 
caregivers and local workforce, and to help plan for future needs that align with the city’s future 
population growth. The Metro Denver Economic Development Corporation was selected through a 
competitive process to conduct a study to evaluate Westminster’s current condition of childcare, and 
the future outlook of childcare with a focus on availability and affordability for children under the age 
of five. Three groups were surveyed for the study including childcare providers, residents, and 
businesses.   
The study shows that there continues to be a significant gap in childcare availability. In Westminster 
there is a need for licensed childcare spaces for 3,695 children. The study identified a gap not to 
exceed 949 spaces, which highlights the limited availability of childcare throughout the city. Infant 
care is the most severe with 11 providers reporting typical waitlists of five to seven months. The 
survey also highlights the difference between licensed capacity, and the capacity providers can 
realistically use with current operating conditions.

Affordability remains a major barrier for families. The annual childcare cost in the city is $19,512, 
which is 19.5 percent of the median household income. Survey responses reinforce this concern, 
70.7 percent of the respondents identified affordable price as one of the most important 
considerations when selecting childcare. Affordability significantly constrains families’ access to 
childcare. 
For families with young children, the cost and availability of childcare can directly affect whether 
parents can enter or remain in the workforce, work full time, or maintain their hours. Strengthening

decade, demand for childcare is expected to remain substantial. The population under five is 
forecasted to decrease by about 7.2 percent between 2025 and 2035, but more than 5,000 children 
will continue to live in the city and the existing gap between available care and demand indicates that 
demographic change will not resolve current shortages. The study also provides insight into 
Westminster’s provider landscape. There are 62 childcare providers operating in the city including 
both home-based and facility-based models. Providers face ongoing staffing challenges, including low 
wages and high turnover rates, which limit their ability to operate at full licensed capacity. 
To respond to childcare challenges, other communities have adopted strategies such as streamlining 
zoning and permitting, offering financial support to providers and staff, assisting families directly with 
tuition costs, and implementing dedicated funding sources like lodging taxes or special districts. 
These approaches demonstrate the types of policy options to consider addressing both current gaps 
and long-term childcare needs.

Executive Summary 
The Westminster Child Care Study evaluates the current condition and future outlook 
of the City’s child care market, with particular attention to the availability and 
affordability of care for children under age five. The study combines demographic and 
economic data, child care licensing and workforce information, family  and provider 
surveys, a forecast of Westminster’s young -child population, and an economic 
analysis of the relationship between child care and labor force participation. 
The analysis finds that Westminster has a child care market gap despite a declining 
population of young children. An estimated 3,695 children had potential 
employment-related child care need , compared with 3,537 licensed spaces and an 
estimated 2,746 effective spaces. Depending on whether supply is measured by 
maximum licensed capacity or by the capacity providers can realistically use under 
current operating conditions, the estimated gap ranges from approximately 158 to 
949 spaces. Provider outreach provides additional evidence of limited availability, 
particularly for infants: eleven facilities reported infant waitlists , and three reported 
toddler waitlists, with reported waits generally ranging from five to seven months. 
Affordability further limits families’ ability to access child care. Center-based infant 
child care cost averages approximately $2,026 per month, while estimated annual 
child care expenditure in Westminster represents approximately 19. 5 percent of 
median household income. Results from the 2026 Westminster Family Child Care 
Needs Survey similarly indicate that affordability is a primary consideration for families, 
with 70.7 percent of respondents with young children identifying affordable price as 
one of the most important characteristics when selecting a provider. 
Child care availability and affordability also have implications for Westminster’s 
workforce. The survey also found that nearly 91.0 percent of responding Westminster 
households with children under age five reported at least one employment, education, 
or training impact associated with child care challenges during the previous twelve  
months. The study’s economic analysis also identifies a positive association between 
greater licensed child care coverage and labor force participation, reinforcing the role 
that child care can play in supporting Westminster residents’ ability to work or seek 
employment. 
Local and neighboring communities have responded to similar child care challenges 
through a range of approaches, including land -use and regulatory reforms, provider 
and workforce support, direct assistance to families, and dedicated local funding 
mechanisms. These strategies illustrate the different ways communities are 
addressing both the supply and affordability sides of the child care market, from 
reducing barriers to provider expansion and supporting the child care workforce to 
helping families offset the cost of child care.

Section 1: Introduction 
1.1: Overview  
This report evaluates Westminster's child care market, comparing local data with 
national trends and peer communities. It provides city leaders and community 
stakeholders with the evidence base and best practices needed to understand local 
child care challenges and guide future municipal action. 
Child care is closely connected to broader demographic and economic trends. 
Americans are aging and having fewer children: data from the National Vital Statistics 
System (NVSS), the federal system that compiles birth, death, and other vital records 
from st ates and U.S. territories, show that the U.S. fertility rate has declined by  23 
percent since 2007. While many factors shape decisions about whether and when to 
have children, child care costs are a notable consideration. In a 2020 national survey, 
Bohanan and Cohen found that 87 percent of Millennial and Gen Z adults identified 
child care costs as an important factor in deciding whether to have children. 
Emerging research has also found an association betwe en higher child care prices 
and lower birth rates, estimating that a 10 percent increase in child care prices is 
associated with a 5.7 percent decrease in birth rates (Dow, 2026). 
For families with young children, the cost and availability of child care can directly 
affect whether parents can enter or remain in the workforce, work full time, or 
maintain their hours. Many families still face significant affordability challenges: U.S. 
Department of Labor data indicate that child care for one child can represent about  
8.9 percent to 16.0 percent of median family income, with annual prices ranging from 
about $6,552 to $15,600 depending on the type and location of child care. These 
pressures are also increasing, with preschool and child care prices rising 5.1 percent in 
2025, compared with a 2.6 percent increase for consumer prices overall. 
These affordability pressures are occurring alongside significant demographic 
change. Nationally, the population under age five declined by 5.4  percent between 
2017 and 2024, while the population age 65 and older increased by 21 .0 percent. 
Colorado is experiencing many of the same pressures, including high child care costs, 
a declining population of young children, and a comparatively high cost of living.  
Although Westminster’s broader child care system serves children across a range of 
ages, the primary market analysis focuses on children under age five. Children in this 
age group are generally not yet enrolled in full-time elementary school and therefore 
have the greatest potential need for full -day child care while parents work, attend 
school, or participate in training. Infant, toddler, and preschool child care also tend to 
face some of the strongest capacity and affordability pressures because younger 
children require smaller child-to-staff ratios and more intensive child care.

Beginning around age five, many children transition into kindergarten, and the nature 
of child care demand changes toward before-school, after-school, summer, and other 
school-age programs. Focusing the core supply -and-demand analysis on children 
under five therefore creates a more comparable relationship between Westminster’s 
young-child population and licensed infant, toddler, and preschool capacity. School -
age providers remain part of the broader provider inventory where relevant, but they 
are not the primary focus of the market-gap analysis. 
To assess how these broader trends are affecting Westminster specifically, this report 
combines data analysis with input from the community. Surveys of families, child care 
providers, and local businesses provide insight into the day -to-day challenges 
surrounding access, affordability, and workforce needs. A forecast of Westminster’s 
population under age five helps estimate how demand for child care may change in 
the coming years, while an analysis was also conducted to examine how child care 
prices and available capacity are related to parents’ ability to participate in the 
workforce. 
1.2: Primary Research and Community Input 
In addition to secondary demographic, economic, and licensing data, the study 
incorporated primary research to better understand how Westminster’s child care 
market operates from the perspectives of families, providers, and employers. Three 
surveys were de veloped to capture different components of the market that are 
difficult to observe through administrative data alone. 
The 2026 Westminster Family Child Care Needs Survey  collected information from 
families regarding current child care arrangements, affordability, provider 
preferences, unmet needs, work location, and the effects of child care challenges on 
employment, education, and training. These results are used throughout the report 
to highlight the struggles that families experience in the child care market and to 
refine selected measures of potential need.  
The 2026  Westminster Child Care Facility Survey gathered information from local 
providers regarding licensed and operational capacity, age groups served, enrollment, 
openings, waitlists, staffing, and operating conditions. Since responses to the formal 
facility survey were  limited, survey information was supplemented with direct 
provider outreach. These findings inform the study’s effective-capacity estimate and 
provide additional context on waitlists, staffing, enrollment, and operating conditions.

Section 2: Community Profile  
Understanding Westminster’s child care market begins with understanding the 
families and workers who generate demand for child care. The number and age of 
children establish the demographic foundation of potential demand, while family 
structure, household income, poverty, and parental workforce participation influence 
whether and how families use formal child care. The following section examines 
Westminster’s demographic and economic characteristics before narrowing the 
analysis to the population of young children most directly associated with child care 
demand. 
2.1: Population and Demographic Context 
Westminster is one of the larger communities among the peer cities examined. In 
2024, Westminster had a total population of 115,484, making it the third- largest peer 
community behind Thornton (144,187) and Arvada (122,634). Westminster’s population 
was considerably larger than Broomfield (76,304), Commerce City (66,445), and 
Northglenn (38,014). Westminst er has maintained a similar position relative to its 
peers over the past decade, remaining one of the larger cities among its peers despite 
differences in population growth across communities.

Household Income and Economic Vulnerability 
Family structure influences how households may coordinate caregiving, while 
financial resources influence whether families can afford the child care available to 
them. Household income therefore provides an important measure of families’ overall 
capacity to absorb child care expenses, while poverty rates help identify households 
facing the greatest financial constraints.  
Westminster’s median household income reached $100,272  in 2024, up 4.3 percent 
from $96,415 in 2023. Between 2015 and 2024, median household income increased at 
an average annual rate of approximately 4.6 percent. Among the peer communities 
examined, Westminster had the second-lowest median household income, ahead of 
Northglenn at $84,030. Broomfield reported the highest median household income 
at $123,874, followed by Arvada at $117,348. 
 
Median household income describes the financial position of the typical household, 
but it can obscure the circumstances of families at the lower end of the income 
distribution. This distinction is especially important for families with young children 
because child care re presents a large recurring expense. Poverty rates by age of 
children provide another measure of the financial vulnerability facing households 
most likely to need early childhood care. 
For this analysis, the poverty rate refers to the share of families whose income falls 
below the U.S. Census Bureau’s official poverty threshold, which varies by family size 
and composition. F or example, the poverty  threshold for a family of four with two 
related children was $31,812. The federal thresholds are applied nationally and are not 
adjusted for differences in local cost of living.

Westminster families with young children face a higher poverty rate than the City’s

Results from th e Westminster Family Child Care Needs Survey provide a different 
perspective on the families participating in this study. More than half of respondents

Why Are Poverty Rates Often Higher Among Families With Young Children? 
Higher poverty rates among families with young children are not unique to Westminster. 
Families with very young children are often in an earlier stage of the household earnings cycle, 
when parents tend to be younger and have had less time to advance in their careers and 
increase their earnings. The arrival of young children can also temporarily reduce household 
labor supply when a parent reduces working hours, takes leave, or exits the workforce to 
provide child care. 
 
As children age, parents generally have greater opportunity to return to full-time employment 
and continue progressing through their careers. For this reason, poverty rates among families 
with young children are often higher than among families with older children or no children

2.4: Young Child Population and Future Child Care Demand 
The preceding analysis establishes the broader household and labor -market 
conditions that influence whether families require and can afford child care. The 
analysis now narrows to the demographic group that most directly drives early -
childhood care demand. Birth trends and the population under age five provide the 
foundation for estimating the current and future scale of Westminster’s child care 
market. 
Current Births 
Births provide one of the most direct indicators of future child care demand because 
each annual birth group progresses through infant, toddler, preschool, and eventually 
school-age care. Westminster recorded 1,606 births in 2007, compared with 1,226 
births in 2025, representing a decline of 380 births, or approximately 23.7 percent. This 
declining trend was not uniform over the study period: births declined through 2011, 
partially recovered between 2012 and 2015, and then resumed a generally downward 
trend through 2025. Since 2020, annual births have remained within a narrower range 
of approximately 1,200 to 1,265, including modest increases in 2022 and 2024 that were 
not sustained in the following years.

The long -term decline in births suggests that Westminster is generating smaller 
classes of young children than during the late 2000s and mid-2010s. All else equal, this 
trend places downward pressure on the total number of children requiring child care 
and may moderate the pace of future growth in citywide child care demand. However, 
these effects occur gradually over time. Births in a given year create immediate 
demand for infant child care, followed by demand for toddler and preschool child care 
as children progress through early childhood. As a result, the relatively stable number 
of births since 2020 suggests that Westminster will continue to experience substantial 
and recurring demand across early childhood age groups, even as overall dem and 
remains below historical peaks. 
Birth counts should not be interpreted as a direct measure of licensed child care 
enrollment. Not every child uses formal child care, while household migration can add 
or remove children from the community after birth. Westminster’s recent increase in 
single-family permitting could also attract additional families with young children and 
partially offset the effects of declining lo cal births. Future child care demand will 
therefore depend on the interaction of births, residential development, family 
migration, parental workforce participation, affordability, and families’ preferences for 
licensed, informal, or family-provided child care. Overall, the birth trend points toward 
declining demographic pressure rather than a broad reduction in the need for child 
care, particularly given that more than 1,200 children continue to be born to 
Westminster families each year. 
Children Under Five and Forecasted Children Under Five 
Westminster’s population under age five has generally trended downward over the 
past decade. Based on the American Community Survey’s five-year estimates, the City 
had approximately 7,315 children under age five in 2015, compared with 5,717 in 2024, 
a decline of nearly 1,600 children, or 21.8  percent. This direction al movement  is 
consistent with Westminster’s broader birth trend, as annual births fell from more 
than 1,600 in 2007 to approximately 1,200 in recent years.  
To provide a reliable basis for long -term child care planning, a forecast of 
Westminster’s population under age five through 2035 was developed. Rather than 
simply extending the historical population trend, the forecast incorporates the 
primary demographic and housing factors that influence how many young children 
are likely to reside in the City. These include recent and projected births, the historical 
relationship between birth groups  and the resident under -five population, and the 
potential effects of future residential development.

Section 3: Child Care Market 
The demographic analysis indicates that Westminster’s population of young 
children is set to continue declining and will not reach the level it was a decade prior. 
Demographics alone, however, do not determine whether families can obtain child 
care. The next step is to evaluate the supply and demand sides of the market by 
observing current licensed capacities, the ages these capacities serve, demand for 
child care, the cost of child care, and whether providers have the workforce and 
operating environment needed to sustain or expand services. 
3.1: Provider Inventory 
Westminster currently has 62 child care providers operating within the City. Home-
based child care providers and facility-based child care centers and preschools each 
accounted for 26 providers, representing approximately 41.9 percent of the inventory

source information.

the importance of both models within Westminster’s child care market. Facility-based 
centers and preschools generally account for a larger share of licensed capacity 
because they can serve more children at a single location, while home -based 
providers offer smaller-scale child care distributed throughout residential areas. The 
presence of both provider types therefore contributes differently to the overall 
availability and geographic distribution of child care within the City.

3.2: Age-Aligned Capacity and Child Care Gaps 
Total Capacity  
To align licensed supply with the under-five population examined in the gap 
analysis, capacity explicitly designated for school-age children and youth programs 
was excluded. The resulting measure includes infant, toddler, preschool, home child 
care, and combined preschool/school-age capacity and therefore represents the 
maximum licensed capacity potentially available to children under five. 
Licensed capacity reflects how many children providers are legally authorized to 
serve. It does not necessarily indicate how many spaces are available for enrollment 
at a given time. Staffing levels, classroom configuration, age-specific enrollment, 
schedules, and other operating conditions can prevent providers from using all of 
the capacity allowed under their licenses. For this reason, the study also estimates 
effective capacity, which reflects the number of spaces providers may realistically be 
able to use under current operating conditions. The effective-capacity estimate is 
based on responses from the Westminster Child Care Facility Survey regarding the 
share of their licensed capacity they are actually able to achieve. 
Under the licensed-capacity measure, potential under-five capacity increased from 
3,411 spaces in 2023 to 3,537 in 2024, before declining to 3,490 in 2025 and 3,425 in 
2026. Estimated effective capacity followed a similar pattern, increasing from 2,648 
spaces in 2023 to 2,746 in 2024, before declining to 2,710 in 2025 and 2,659 in 2026.

Demand 
Consistent with the Bipartisan Policy Center’s Child Care Gaps Assessment, this study 
begins by defining potential child care need as the number of children for whom all 
available parents are in the labor force. The Bipartisan Policy Center is a Washington, 
D.C.-based nonprofit policy organization that conducts research and develops policy 
recommendations on a range of national issues, including child care and family 
economic security. “Available parents” includes both parents in a two-parent family or 
the resident parent in a single -parent family. This measure identifies children whose 
household employment circumstances are most likely to create a need for 
nonparental child care. 
The estimate was further adjusted using the Westminster Family Child Care Needs 
Survey. Of the 212 children represented in the survey, 17, or 8.0  percent, lived in 
households using private nanny or unpaid family child care that reported being 
completely satisfied with their current arrangement. This share was removed from 
estimated potential demand. Children in alternative child care arrangements that did 
not fully meet household needs remained in the estimate.  
Using this methodology, Westminster had an estimated 3,695 children with potential 
child care demand in 2024, down approximately 10.7 percent from 4,137 in 2014. After 
reaching a low of 3,464 in 2022, estimated demand increased approximately 6.7

Gap Analysis 
To evaluate whether Westminster’s existing child care supply is sufficient to meet 
potential need, the study compares estimated employment- related child care need 
with two measures of supply: licensed capacity and effective capacity. The analysis 
focuses on 2024, the most recent year for which the demand estimate overlaps with 
sufficiently detailed child care licensing data. 
The two measures describe different aspects of the child care market. Licensed 
capacity represents the maximum number of children providers are legally 
authorized to serve. Effective capacity reflects the number of children providers may 
realistically be able to serve under current staffing and operating conditions. In simple 
terms, licensed capacity shows the space that exists on paper, while effective capacity 
provides an estimate of the space that can actually be used. 
Westminster had an estimated 3,695 children with potential child care need. This 
compares with 3,537 licensed spaces, equivalent to 95.7 percent of potential need, and 
an estimated 2,746 effective spaces, equivalent to 74.3 percent of potential need. 
Depending on which measure of supply is used, Westminster’s estimated child care 
gap therefore ranges from approximately 158 to 949 spaces.

child care that is available. The following analysis examines these constraints.

could spend about $17,568 to $24,312 annually for a single child . On the other hand, 
family child care provides a comparatively lower -cost option, with average monthly 
costs of $1,288 for children ages 0 to 23 months and $1,182 for children ages 24 to 53

3.4: Child Care Services Industry Workforce 
High child care costs place significant pressure on families, but efforts to reduce prices 
are constrained by the high and rising costs of providing care. Because child care is 
especially labor intensive, personnel costs make up a substantial share of prov ider 
expenses, while staffing levels directly determine how many children providers can 
safely and legally serve. The size, compensation, and stability of the child care 
workforce are therefore central to understanding both the cost of care and the extent 
to which Westminster’s licensed child care capacity can actually be used.  
Employment and Wages 
The Quarterly Census of Employment and Wages (QCEW) provides detailed 
information on employment, establishments, and wages based primarily on records 
from employers covered by unemployment insurance programs. QCEW data offer a 
useful picture of Westminster’s employment base and provide context for 
understanding the size and compensation of the city’s child care workforce. 
In Q3 2025, Westminster had 46,097 jobs, an increase of 119 jobs, or 0.3 percent, from 
Q3 2024. Employment was concentrated in several large sectors: Professional and 
Business Services, Wholesale and Retail Trade, and Education and Health Services 
together accounted for 25,095 jobs, or 54.4 percent of total employment. Employment 
trends across sectors were mixed, with six of the city’s twelve supersectors gaining 
jobs and six experiencing declines over the year. Westminster also had 3,868 business 
establishments in Q3 2025, up 0.7 percent from the prior year. Overall, these figures 
indicate a relatively stable employment base, with modest grow th in both jobs and 
establishments.

3.5: Business Start-Up Environment for Child Care Providers 
Wages for child care workers that are close to the State minimum wage and high 
labor turnover rates highlight the difficulty child care providers face in attracting and 
retaining a skilled workforce. Beyond workforce challenges, providers must 
complete several steps to operate a licensed child care business in the State of 
Colorado, and specifically in the City of Westminster. 
Licensing 
Once the general requirements for starting a business are met, such as registering 
with the Secretary of State, the first child care-specific step is obtaining a license from 
the Colorado Department of Early Childhood.  This applies to both home -based and 
facility-based providers. Both the licensing process and the application fee depend on 
the license type: home- based child care, non- home-based facilities, outdoor and 
nature-based preschools, and substitute placement agencies. As of July 1, 2020, 
Colorado licensing fees for home -based providers depend on licensed capacity: $65 
for providers serving one to six children and $100 for large family providers serving 
seven to twelve. For facility-based providers, the base fee is $200 for facilities serving 
five to 15 children and increases to $300 for those serving more than 31.  For facilities 
serving 16 or more children, an additional licensing fee of $3 applies for each child 
beyond 15. Licenses for both at -home providers and facility -based providers remain 
valid for one year.  
At the city level, the first step is to obtain a business license from the City of 
Westminster, which charges no application or renewal fee . The City states that 
licenses typically take four to six weeks to obtain and must be renewed annually. After 
a provider obtai ns the Westminster business license, the next step is  to sign 
agreements for funding programs, such as the Colorado Child Care Assistance 
Program (CCCAP), which is an assistance program that helps low-income families find 
affordable child care.

Funding and Barriers to Entry 
Beyond licensing and permitting considerations, child care providers  typically face 
funding shortfalls related to low profit margins. However, there are various financing 
and funding options available to them. Unlike most goods and services, child care 
does not tend to benefit from the innovation or efficiency gains that lower production 
costs, and ultimately, lower prices over time. Children -to-teacher ratios are fixed by 
regulation, with infants and toddlers requiring more intensive child care, resulting in 
more staff per child and higher labor costs for the youngest age groups. Innovation or 
technological improvement cannot increase this ratio, as one caregiver can only safely 
monitor a few children at one time, regardless of the tools and resources available to 
them.  
Operating at scale does not fix this issue, as the number of staff must increase 
proportionally with the number of children enrolled. Thus, providers must maximize 
enrollment and staff retention to remain profitable. Smaller facilities and home-based 
providers may have fewer resources than larger facilities for advertising open 
enrollment slots or for training and recruiting staff. These difficulties pose significant 
barriers to entry for small and startup providers, which may partly explain why home-
based providers in Westminster have declined. 
One of the largest sources of child care financing in Colorado is the Child  Care Loan 
Program offered by the Colorado Enterprise Fund. The program is available to both 
home-based and facility -based providers.  Program requirements include that 
applicants must be located in Colorado, be unable to obtain a traditional bank loan, 
be at least 80 percent owner-operated, and hold a valid license from CDEC. According 
to the Colorado Enterprise Fund, loan amounts range from $10,000 to $1,000,000, and 
terms depend on the loan’s size and use.  Additionally, the program offers Business 
Navigation Coaching, which is designed to equip providers with mentorship and 
guidance for operating a successful child care business.  
An additional source of financing is the Colorado Loans to Increase Mainstreet 
Business Economic Resiliency (CLIMBER)  loan fund, which provides loans from 
$10,000 to $500,000 to small businesses with 99 or fewer employees, a threshold that 
covers most Westminster child care facilities. One primary source of federal financing 
is the Small Business Administration (SBA), which offers loans up to $50,000 to 
qualifying small businesses. These loans can provide targeted support for child care 
providers aiming to expand operations or improve quality of child care.

Section 4: Economic Analysis 
The previous sections helped identify the gaps and showed how licensing capacity 
may fall short of the potential need, with affordability constraints further reducing a 
family’s access to child care. This section presents an economic analysis that models 
potential positive economic effects if these issues are addressed. 
A 2021 study by the U.S. Department of the Treasury found that individuals who do not 
participate in the labor force lack access to employment -related benefits such as 
health and dental insurance and retirement plans.  Additionally, labor force 
participation increases state income and sales tax revenue as well as municipal sales 
tax revenue. The following analysis examines Westminster’s child care industry from 
two perspectives: capacity and price, and draws on a sample of cities across Colorado 
to examine whether increasing child care capacity or decreasing child care costs leads 
to more people either finding jobs or choosing to look for work.  
The capacity analysis draws on a Colorado Information Marketplace dataset of all 
licensed child care providers in Colorado. The price analysis combines the 2017 -2018 
Colorado Child Care Market Survey, the National Database of Child Care Prices from 
the U.S. Department of Labor Women’s Bureau, and the child care component of the 
Consumer Price Index  (CPI) to produce a city-level price estimate for Westminster.  
The model’s two variables of interest are the price of child care and licensed child care 
coverage, which is calculated as a city’s licensed child care capacity in a given period 
divided by its population under five.  The model’s estimates are then applied to 
Westminster’s current values to examine how changes in either capacity or price 
would affect the City’s labor force participation rate. 
4.1: Results 
The model estimates the relationship between child care coverage and labor force 
participation, which includes both employed residents and unemployed residents 
actively seeking work. For purposes of estimating earnings and fiscal impacts, the 
modeled increase in labor force participation was converted to an estimated increase 
in employment using Westminster’s prevailing employment rate. As a result, the 
employment estimates presented below are slightly lower than the corresponding 
increase in labor force participants and provide the basis for calculating additional 
earnings and tax revenue. 
The analysis finds a positive relationship between child care coverage and 
employment across the comparison cities. In general, cities with more licensed child 
care capacity relative to their population under age five also tended to have higher 
employment levels. This relationship remained even after accounting for differences

The effect is modest  but consistent: more child care  coverage is linked to more

meaning cities with more child care coverage tend to have more people participating 
in the labor force . In other words, it does not indicate that increase s in labor force

labor force participation may also naturally have more child care providers in the area.  
The model does not identify a clear relationship between child care prices and 
employment at the city level. This may reflect limitations in the available local price 
data rather than evidence that cost does not matter to families. For some households, 
child care may remain unaffordable even when licensed spaces are available. As  a 
result, increasing capacity alone may not address every barrier families face in 
accessing child care.

Section 5: Local Government Initiatives in 
Colorado 
Communities across Colorado are increasingly treating child care as a local workforce 
and economic development issue. In response to shortages in available child care, 
high family costs, and workforce challenges within the child care sector, cities and 
counties have adopted a range  of policies intended to combat these issues.  These 
approaches generally fall into four categories: land -use and regulatory reforms, 
provider and workforce support, direct assistance to families, and dedicated local 
funding mechanisms. 
Policy and Zoning Support 
Local governments can influence child care supply by reducing regulatory and real 
estate barriers that make it difficult for providers to open or expand facilities. 
Strategies being used or considered across Colorado include streamlining permitting, 
expanding child care as a permitted use across additional zoning districts, reducing 
parking requirements, waiving development or building fees, and aligning local 
requirements more closely with state licensing standards. These approaches can 
increase the number  of properties available for child care use and reduce the time 
and expense associated with opening a facility. 
Several Colorado communities have taken steps in this direction. Denver eliminated 
its separate local child care business -license requirement in 2026, while providers 
remain subject to applicable state licensing and local health, fire, planning, and 
building requirements. Colorado Springs, Longmont, and Thornton have also updated 
land use regulations to allow child care facilities in a broader range of locations. 
Westminster has pursued a similar approach through its Comprehensive Plan, 
allowing child care facilities throughout most of the City outside heavy industrial areas. 
These approaches are consistent with research from Executives Partnering to Invest 
in Children (EPIC), which has identified real estate costs, financing constraints, and 
lengthy approval processes as barriers to expanding Colorado’s child care supply. EPIC 
has recommended reducing regulatory barriers and improving access to suitable real 
estate and financing for new and expanding providers.

Provider Expansion and Workforce Support 
Local governments are also using financial and operational support to strengthen 
existing providers and create additional capacity. Potential strategies include leasing 
underutilized public facilities to child care operators at reduced rates, providing low - 
or no -interest start- up capital, offering grants for facility improvements, and 
subsidizing training or compensation for the child care workforce. These approaches 
recognize that licensed capacity depends on providers’ ability to finance facilities and 
recruit and retain qualified workers. 
Eagle County, for example, operates an Early Childhood Workforce Stipend Program 
that provides eligible child care employees with a $500 monthly stipend. The County 
has also considered additional supports for facility improvements, housing costs, and 
creating infant and toddler capacity. In Estes Valley, local lodging tax revenue 
supports several components of child care, with some examples including facility 
improvements, workforce compensation, and family tuition assistance. 
Similar approaches have also emerged through regional planning efforts. The La Plata 
County Economic Development Alliance identified a broader child care investment 
framework that included workforce wage and benefit support, capital and 
infrastructure funding for providers, and assistance designed to strengthen the child 
care workforce. 
Supporting Families and Caregivers 
Local governments have also developed programs that address the demand side of 
the child care market by helping families afford available child care. These programs 
are often designed to supplement state and federal assistance , particularly for 
households whose incomes are too high to qualify for traditional subsidies but remain 
insufficient to comfortably absorb the cost of licensed child care.  Other strategies 
include family navigation services that help households identify available providers 
and understand existing financial-assistance programs. 
The Estes Valley Child Care  Fund was created specifically to help bridge this gap for 
middle-income households. Larimer County’s Kids Thrive Fund similarly uses local 
revenue to provide assistance through a tiered eligibility system, while the City of 
Boulder extends locally funded child care assistance to working households with 
incomes up to 400 percent of the Federal Poverty Level.

Dedicated Funding and Tax Initiatives 
A recurring challenge for local child care initiatives is identifying a sustainable funding 
source. Colorado communities have increasingly used several methods to finance 
child care programs, as recent state legislation has expanded local governments' 
ability to use these mechanisms for child care-related purposes.  
Over a dozen Colorado communities are using lodging-tax revenue to fund child care 
efforts. For instance, Estes Park raised its lodging tax from 2.0 percent to 5.5 percent, 
allocating part of the extra revenue to support child care programs.  Property-tax 
policy provides another tool, where Colorado law has allowed local governments to 
establish property -tax credit or rebate programs for licensed child care providers. 
Adams and Douglas Counties have adopted programs providing rebates ranging 
from 50 percent to 100 percent for qualifying properties with child care operations. 
Other communities, including Larimer, Garfield, and Pitkin Counties, have established 
voter-approved special districts supported by dedicated sales -tax revenue to fund 
child care initiatives.

Section 6: Conclusion 
Overall, the analysis finds that Westminster faces a constrained child care market 
characterized by limited availability, high costs, and workforce -related impacts for 
families. In 2024, Westminster had an estimated 3,695 children with potential 
employment-related child care need, compared with 3,537 licensed spaces potentially 
capable of serving children under age five and an estimated 2,746 effective spaces 
under current operating conditions. Depending on the measure of supply used, 
Westminster’s estimated child care gap therefore ranges from approximately 158 to 
949 spaces. Licensed capacity is equivalent to 95.7 percent of estimated potential 
need, while estimated effective capacity is equivalent to 74.3 percent. 
Affordability represents an additional constraint. Center -based infant child care in 
Westminster averages approximately $2,026 per month, while the estimated annual 
cost of licensed facility child care is approximately $19,512, equivalent to about 19.5 
percent of Westminster’s median household income. Results from the Westminster 
Family Child Care Needs Survey  reinforce the importance of affordability, with 70.7 
percent of responding families with children under age five identifying affordable 
price as one of their most important considerations when selecting a child care 
provider. 
Child care challenges also have implications for Westminster’s workforce. The survey 
also found that 90.8 percent of responding resident households with children under 
age five experienced at least one employment, education, or training impact 
associated with child care challenges during the previous 12 months. The study’s 
economic analysis further identified a positive association between licensed child care 
coverage and labor force participation, although the relationship should not be 
interpreted as strictly causal. 
Westminster’s child care challenges are occurring despite a long-term decline in the 
number of young children. The City’s population under age five is projected to decline 
from approximately 5,695 children in 2025 to 5,285 in 2035, a decrease of about 7.2 
percent. These findings indicate that Westminster’s current child care constraints are 
driven less by expected demographic growth than by the relationship between 
existing need, licensed supply, the ability of providers to fully utilize authorized 
capacity, affordability, and age-specific availability.

Study Overview
• The Metro Denver Economic Development Corporation (MDEDC) was selected 
through a competitive process in early 2026 to complete a Study that evaluates the 
current condition and future outlook of the City’s childcare market, with particular 
attention to the availability and affordability of care for children under age five.
• MDEDC staff will share key findings around childcare availability gaps; affordability 
constraints; and workforce participation implications.
• Study findings will guide near‑term actions and long‑range planning to expand 
childcare capacity and support economic growth.

Key Findings
CENTRAL FINDING
Westminster’s young-child population is declining, but the market remains constrained. Licensed spaces nearly match 
potential need on paper; the much larger issue is whether those spaces can actually be staffed and used, and whether families 
can afford them.
3
• 158–949 spaces: Westminster’s estimated child care gap, depending on 
available capacity. 
• 19.5% of income: Westminster’s estimated annual child care cost burden. 
• 90.8% of households: Reported employment, education, or training 
impacts from child care challenges. 
• 5,285 children: Projected under-five population in 2035, indicating 
substantial future child care need.
