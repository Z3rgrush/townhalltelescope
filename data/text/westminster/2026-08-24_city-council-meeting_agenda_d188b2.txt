11. PUBLIC HEARINGS 
A. Public Hearing and Consideration of an Appeal of the Denial of an Official 
Development Plan for the Mountain Range High School Nutrition Center  
Carmelia opened the public hearing at 7:41 p.m.  
 
Carmelia, Nurmela, Johnson, and Ireland disclosed that they had received 
communications regarding the matter but stated that they remained impartial and 
could decide the matter based upon the evidence presented at the hearing. Barajas 
disclosed receipt of an email sent to the full City Council and stated that he had 
submitted the communication into the public record. 
 
Senior Planner Reb Batzel entered the agenda memorandum and attachments, S taff 
presentation, mailed notices, and posted notices into the public record and stated that 
the hearing had been properly noticed.

Page 7 of 163

Staff explained that Adams 12 Five Star Schools proposed an ODP for a nutrition center 
at 12450 Delaware Street on the Mountain Range High School campus. The Planning 
Commission denied the ODP and three requested exceptions on July 14, 2026, by a 5 -2 
vote, after which the applicant timely appealed the decision to City Council.  
 
The three requested exceptions were to allow textured architectural metal panels and 
textured, integrally colored masonry block veneer as predominant building materials;  
reduce the minimum number of required trees and shrubs in landscaped areas; and 
allow approximately 62 percent of the retaining walls to exceed the maximum four -
foot height. 
 
Staff recommended approval of the ODP with the three requested exceptions. 
 
Council questioned Staff regarding infrastructure and utility review, landscaping 
requirements, retaining wall height and safety, site design alternatives, and the 
potential precedent associated with approving exceptions to the City’s architectural 
and landscaping standards. Staff stated that 83 trees and 249 shrubs would otherwise 
be required, while the project proposed 23 trees and 23 shrubs. Staff also explained 
that portions of the retaining walls would reach approximately 10 feet in height and 
discussed the structural, safety, and aesthetic purposes of the City’s four-foot standard. 
 
Representatives of Adams 12 Five Star Schools presented the District’s proposal and 
described the nutrition center as a centralized facility for storing, preparing, and 
distributing meals to approximately 29,000 students at 47 schools. The District stated 
that the facility would support scratch cooking, farm-to-table opportunities, 
accommodation of students’ dietary needs, and more efficient purchasing and 
distribution of food. 
 
The applicant explained that the requested building-material exception was necessary 
because a pre-engineered metal building was more appropriate and cost-effective for 
the facility’s warehouse and food-production functions. The District described 
architectural modifications made in response to City concerns, including masonry 
elements, varied textures, articulated rooflines, and other design features.  
Regarding landscaping, the applicant stated that the proposed native landscape would 
reduce water consumption and that security needs limited landscaping within the 
secured portion of the site. Regarding the retaining walls, the applicant stated that the 
taller walls were necessary because of site grading and required maneuvering areas for 
semi-trucks and emergency vehicles. The walls would be professionally engineered and 
located within the secured area of the property. 
 
The District also discussed project costs and delays, stating that compliance with the 
requested design changes would create approximately $1.5 million in additional costs 
and that delays had already resulted in increased construction costs. 
 
Council questioned the applicant regarding future expansion of the site, opportunities 
for additional landscaping and tree canopy, the original design’s compliance with City 
standards, retaining wall construction and safety, project costs, food distributio n 
operations, and the District’s interpretation of its authority under state law.  
 
Council and the City Attorney also discussed the City’s authority over the development 
review process. The City Attorney stated that Staff considered the matter to be properly 
submitted before Council and recommended that Council evaluate the application 
under the criteria contained in the Westminster Municipal Code. The District 
acknowledged that it and the City had differing interpretations of applicable state law.  
 
Seeing that no members of the public signed up to provide testimony, Carmelia closed 
the public hearing at 8:47 p.m.

Summary Statement:
The City of Westminster has been awarded the Government Finance Officers Association (GFOA) 
Triple Crown Award, a national recognition granted to governments that receive all three of the 
following distinctions within the same award period:
Certificate of Achievement for Excellence in Financial Reporting
Distinguished Budget Presentation Award
Popular Annual Financial Reporting Award
The Triple Crown Award represents the highest level of recognition in governmental finance and 
affirms Westminster’s adherence to nationally recognized standards for financial transparency, 
accountability, and reporting excellence.

Background Information:
The GFOA administers nationally recognized programs that evaluate government financial 
documents for clarity, accuracy, and compliance with best practices. To qualify for the Triple Crown 
Award, a government must receive recognition in all three of the following categories during the same 
year: 
1. Annual Comprehensive Financial Report (ACFR) 
2. Popular Annual Financial Report (PAFR) 
3. Adopted Budget 
For the 42nd consecutive year, the City of Westminster has received the Certificate of Achievement 
for Excellence in Financial Reporting from the Government Finance Officers Association for its 2024 
Annual Comprehensive Financial Report (ACFR). This distinction represents the highest form of 
recognition in governmental accounting and financial reporting and affirms that the City’s ACFR is 
clearly presented, comprehensive, and prepared in accordance with a defined reporting framework 
incorporating Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP) and GFOA program standards. The 
report is supported by an unmodified opinion from the City’s independent auditor, providing 
assurance that the financial statements are fairly presented in all material respects. Following a 
rigorous peer review conducted by a national committee of financial reporting professionals, the 
GFOA determined that Westminster’s report fulfills the “spirit of full disclosure,” demonstrating the 
City’s sustained commitment to transparency and accountability. 
 
In addition, Westminster received the Award for Outstanding Achievement in Popular Annual 
Financial Reporting for its 2024 Popular Annual Financial Report (PAFR), marking the fourth 
consecutive year of recognition. While the ACFR follows a prescribed GAAP-based framework, the 
PAFR relies on professional judgment to distill key financial information into a concise, accessible 
format for readers seeking a high-level overview. Westminster’s PAFR presents financial results 
through an analytical and visually engaging format, incorporating graphics and charts to enhance

Recommended City Council Action:

Summary Statement:
City Council action is requested to pass the attached Councillor’s Bill on second reading that 
amends various sections of the Westminster Municipal Code concerning police regulations, 
offense classifications, and penalties to align with Colorado statutes and the Colorado Supreme 
Court's decision in People v. Camp.
The amended motion made during the August 10 meeting included the deletion of the phrase “an 
unclassified” prior to criminal violation in 1-8-1(A) and is reflected in the attached Councillor’s Bill.
In People v. Camp (Camp), the Colorado Supreme Court held that home-rule municipalities may 
not impose criminal penalties exceeding the state’s sentencing caps when the municipal violation 
is identical to conduct that is prohibited under the state’s criminal code. As a result, the City of 
Westminster must adhere to the following maximum penalties for municipal offenses that are 
Page 30 of 163 
Page 30 of 163 

Page 2 of 2
identical to state criminal conduct:
oClass 1 misdemeanor: Up to 364 days jail; $1,000 fine
oClass 2 misdemeanor: Up to 120 days jail; $750 fine
oPetty offense: Up to 10 days jail; $300 fine
As part of the broader Westminster Municipal Code (W.M.C.) Title VI update effort, Staff is 
presenting the first 11 amendments for City Council consideration. In addition to penalty 
compliance, this phase of updates refines several code sections which have broadly drafted 
language that must now be narrowed, clarified, or reorganized to reflect the differentiated penalty 
structure required by Camp. Those changes at times include adding subsections to specific 
offenses, but Staff does not anticipate a meaningful increase in case volume because most 
amended sections were already covered by the W.M.C.’s pre-existing broad language.
In addition, Staff is proposing an update to Section 1-8-1, W.M.C., to adopt uniform municipal 
offense classifications aimed at improving transparency, clarity, and administrative efficiency 
across the Police Department and Municipal Court for the benefit of the public.
City Council unanimously passed Councillor’s Bill No. 26 on first reading by a vote of (6 to 0) on 
August 10, 2026.

Background Information:
The City requires highly sophisticated banking and lockbox service due to the volume, diversity, and 
complexity of its financial operations. The City processes substantial daily transaction activity across 
multiple revenue streams, including utility billing, sales tax collections, licensing, recreation program 
payments, and development-related fees. These operations require secure high-speed remittance 
processing, automated data capture, same-day imaging, and robust exception-item handling. The 
City also requires advanced fraud-prevention tools, enhanced treasury analytics, automated 
reconciliation capabilities, consolidated payables processing, reliable ACH and wire-transmission 
services, and future-ready, seamless integration with the City’s enterprise financial management 
system.
Banking service costs are typically offset through an ECR generated on non-interest-bearing 
compensating balances maintained at the bank and interest earned on balances exceeding the 
threshold. The ECR is an internal rate banks use to calculate credits on business account 
compensating balances, which are then applied to monthly banking service charges and transactional 
fees. Balances above the compensating balance level earn interest income that further offset any 
remaining fees. 
Example of Earnings Credit Rate for August:
Average Monthly Balance: $250,000
Compensating Balance: $100,000
ECR: 2.00% (approximately $170)
Interest Rate: 1.90% (approximately $242)
Service Fees: $165
In this example, the ECR of 2.00% applied to the $100,000 compensating balance generates 
approximately $170 in credits to offset service fees. The 1.90% interest rate applied to the $150,000 
excess balance, above the compensating balance, earns approximately $242. The ECR credits fully 
cover the monthly service fees, and the City earns $242 in interest income. ECR credits have no cash 
value. Staff regularly evaluates the City's cash position to determine an appropriate compensating 
balance level that minimizes banking costs while maintaining the City's investment priorities of safety 
and liquidity.

capabilities, customer support, implementation strategy, and pricing.
