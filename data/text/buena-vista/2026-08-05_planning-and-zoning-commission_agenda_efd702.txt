1. Public Hearing – Rustic Haven Site Plan
Staff - Kopp presented the Rustic Haven Major Site Plan, outlining the hearing process, site
location, zoning, and proposal for the 2.2-acre property that previously received a Special Use
Permit for co-housing in May 2025. The plan retains the existing 9 cabin structures (11-units) and
a common house. The applicant is proposing to add six new cabin units (located outside the
floodplain and floodway), with no expansion of existing buildings. Staff worked closely with the
applicant to complete a water audit, confirming existing infrastructure can support the
development by reducing irrigation and confirming the updated fixture count can be supported
by the existing infrastructure. Any future addition of water fixtures would require Town water
department review. Staff found the proposal met the applicable site plan review criteria, and in
response to Commissioner Kosley's question, Kopp explained that restrictions on additional
water fixtures will be included in the condominium plat, the project's bylaws, and the
development agreement.

Applicant -Robin Mesaric-King provided an overview of the Vista Court Cabins proposal, 
explaining the project's evolution from a lodging property to a cohousing community designed 
to address Buena Vista's need for attainable local housing. She described the cohousing model 
as emphasizing individually owned homes with shared amenities, a central courtyard, reduced 
maintenance, and a smaller environmental footprint. The proposal preserves the existing historic 
cabins by converting them into deeded condominium homes without significant structural 
modifications, while incorporating water conservation measures such as drought-tolerant 
landscaping, reduced irrigation, and backflow devices. The development is intended to provide 
homeownership opportunities at a range of price points. Existing infrastructure and parking will 
be retained, and each unit will include dedicated covered bicycle storage. In response to Chair 
LaGreca’s inquiry about the number of units owners versus units rented, the applicant clarified 
that all units will be privately owned; with further clarification that none of the units would be 
eligible for short-term rental use.

Transportation Master Plan – Project Introduction 
Summary: The Town is continuing work on the 2026 Transportation Master Plan update in partnership 
with KLJ Planning and Engineering. The consultant team is led by Ben Manibog, Transportation 
Planning Team Leader, who will be presenting the existing conditions analysis and summarizing the 
community engagement completed to date. 
The purpose of this discussion is to introduce the Commission to the project, review the work 
completed during the existing conditions phase, and receive early policy-level feedback before the 
project advances into identifying and prioritizing transportation improvements. No formal 
recommendation or action is requested from the Planning & Zoning Commission at this time.

Purpose: Staff is underway facilitating the town’s update of the Transportation Master Plan. 
The project began in March 6, 2026 in recognition of the need to consolidate 
transportation-related recommendations from past policy documents and update the 
previous master plan with the recent comprehensive plan. 
At this stage, staff are not seeking formal recommendations or action from the Planning & 
Zoning Commission. Rather, staff would like to begin a broader policy conversation and 
receive early feedback on the direction of the master plan as it takes shape. 
Discussion: As staff moves to next phase of the Transportation Master Plan, involving 
future conditions and project identiﬁcation, staff is seeking feedback from the Commission 
on: 
1. Do the existing conditions match your understanding of Buena Vista? Why or why 
not? 
2. Does the initial community engagement match your understanding of Buena Vista? 
Why or why not? 
3. Does the draft project goals match your understanding of Buena Vista’s needs? Why 
or why not? 
Background: The purpose of the Buena Vista Transportation Master Plan (TMP) is to 
establish a strategic, implementation-focused roadmap for improving the safety, 
connectivity, and operational performance of the transportation system over time. The plan 
is intended to move from an evaluation of existing and projected conditions, set new policy 
direction and develop a prioritized set of projects and coordinated actions that can be 
realistically implemented. 
Key objectives of the Transportation Master Plan include: 
• Evaluating existing and future transportation conditions using data-driven analysis 
• Identifying critical system needs, including capacity, safety, and connectivity gaps 
• Aligning transportation investments 
The project team has completed the ﬁrst round of community engagement. Online 
opportunities to provide feedback on transportation conditions included an online survey 
and a mapping activity on My BV . In-person opportunities to provide feedback on the 
project’s direction and transportation conditions included a visioning meeting with Town 
 
and Chaffee County staff, a stakeholder meeting, and a public open house held at the 
community center. 
Project goals identiﬁed through Town/County staff and stakeholder meetings, ranked from 
highest to lowest priority are: 
• Safe transportation access for all ages, abilities, and modes of travel 
• Attainable solution-oriented projects 
• Sustainable maintenance 
• Connectivity and completing missing links/transportation gaps 
• Managing seasonality – Reducing congestion, mitigating impacts from tourism and 
reducing impacts to local BV travel 
• Sense of place and arrival when entering BV 
• Agency coordination and collaboration 
• Transparency – Creating a transparent process for project prioritization 
Conclusion: This discussion represents the ﬁrst step in the Town’s development of the 
Transportation Master Plan. At this stage, staff are seeking broad policy guidance, 
community considerations, and initial direction from the Planning & Zoning Commission 
before shaping the second phase of the project consisting of project identiﬁcation, project 
prioritization, and implementation. 
Any feedback, concerns, desired outcomes, or initial questions the Commission would like 
staff to research further are welcomed and appreciated as this process moves forward.

Introduction | 3
 Introduction
The Buena Vista Transportation Master Plan (TMP) is a data-driven 
planning effort intended to guide future mobility, infrastructure 
investment, and system performance both within the Town and 
across the broader study area. The TMP builds directly on the 
policy direction established in the 2025 Comprehensive Plan and 
the Three-Mile Plan, translating long-range community goals and 
growth expectations into a coordinated transportation strategy. 
A key component of this effort is recognizing that Buena Vista’s 
future transportation needs will be shaped not only by conditions 
within the current Town limits, but also by growth occurring 
within the surrounding three-mile planning area. The Areas of 
Desired Growth (ADGs) identified in the Three-Mile Plan establish a 
corridor-oriented and intersection-based growth framework that 
will influence how and where travel demand increases over time. As 
these areas develop, they will generate new trips that rely heavily 
on existing corridors particularly US 24, Main Street and connecting 
collectors reinforcing the need to evaluate system performance at a 
broader, regional scale. The TMP serves as the critical link between 
land use planning and transportation system planning, ensuring 
that development decisions and infrastructure investments are 
coordinated and mutually supportive. 
The current transportation network is generally operating at 
acceptable levels, with most intersections functioning at LOS C 
and traffic volumes concentrated along Main Street, which carries 
approximately 8,000 to 10,000 vehicles per day. While these 
conditions indicate stable and efficient operations, they also reflect a 
system that is moderately utilized with limited excess capacity.  
Previous plans including the Comprehensive Plan, Trails Plan, and 
Parks and Trails Inventory consistently emphasize the importance 
of expanding pedestrian and bicycle connectivity, improving safety, 
and enhancing access to recreational assets such as the Arkansas 
River corridor. These plans identify barriers such as high-speed 
roadways, limited crossing opportunities, and incomplete facilities 
that can discourage non-motorized travel, particularly for everyday 
trips. Relatively high bicycle usage and a notable share of residents 
working from home indicate an opportunity to support a more 
diverse set of travel behaviors beyond traditional vehicle use.
Current trends combined with Buena Vista’s steady population 
growth, increasing daytime activity, and strong tourism influence, 
point to the likelihood of incremental but meaningful increases in 
travel demand over time. Growth is not expected to occur as rapid 
expansion, but rather as gradual intensification within the Town 
and outward development in the ADGs. This type of growth pattern 
can incrementally erode system performance, particularly along 
key corridors and at critical intersections, if not proactively planned 
for. Developing a long-range traffic forecast that incorporates both 
background growth and ADG-driven development is essential to 
understanding how the system will perform in the future and where 
improvements will be needed. 
The TMP is structured to move toward a forward-looking, 
implementation-oriented strategy. By integrating Comprehensive 
Plan policy direction, Three-Mile Plan growth assumptions, and 
observed transportation trends, the TMP establishes a foundation 
for forecasting future conditions, identifying system needs, and 
prioritizing investments. This approach ensures that Buena Vista can 
maintain a reliable, efficient, and context-sensitive transportation 
system that supports both current mobility needs and the 
community’s long-term vision for growth and development.

Study Area | 6
 Town Boundary and Jurisdiction
The mapped study area extends beyond the incorporated town 
boundary, clearly including land under both municipal and county 
jurisdiction. The central and more developed portions of the study 
area particularly along Main Street and Colorado Avenue, are 
indicative of areas within the town limits, where urban services, 
zoning regulations, and higher-density land uses are typically 
concentrated. 
Conversely, the outer extents of the study area transition into 
unincorporated areas covered by the 3-Mile Plan. These areas are 
generally characterized by lower-density development patterns 
and are governed by county-level land use regulations rather than 
municipal zoning. This dual jurisdictional condition is significant, 
as it introduces differing standards for land use, infrastructure, and 
future development, necessitating coordination between town and 
county agencies for planning and implementation.
 County Elements
The inclusion of unincorporated areas of Chaffee County within 
the study area highlights the importance of regional connectivity 
and rural-to-urban transition. The numbered routes shown on 
the map serve as critical connectors between the town and 
surrounding county areas, supporting commuter travel, goods 
movement, and access to regional destinations.  County lands 
within the study area may also accommodate uses that differ 
from those within town limits, such as larger parcels, agricultural 
or industrial activities, and lower service levels for utilities and 
infrastructure. These differences reinforce the role of the study 
area as an interface between municipal development and broader 
county land use patterns.

 Floodplain 
Flood risk in Buena Vista is closely tied to several low-
lying areas, where mapped flood hazard zones extend 
through portions of the study area and intersect with key 
transportation corridors. Buena Vista has a documented 
history of flooding, with flash flooding and localized 
overland flow representing the most common and 
impactful hazard. While the Arkansas River creates defined 
regulatory floodplain constraints, the more frequent risk 
arises from intense rainfall events that overwhelm local 
drainage systems, resulting in sheet flow, split-channel 
flooding, and ponding outside mapped floodplains. These 
conditions are typical of mountain-valley communities 
with multiple tributary systems and variable topography. 
In addition to the Arkansas River, several smaller drainage 
systems contribute to flood risk, including: Cottonwood 
Creek, Chalk Creek and Trout Creek (historic flooding 
influence). Chaffee County, Colorado has recorded 22 
flood-related events since 1996 according to NOAA’s 
Storm Events Database, including 20 flash floods and 2 
river or area floods. The county has received 5 federal 
disaster declarations, 1 of which involved flooding. 
The floodplain acts as both a constraint and a planning 
consideration, influencing where expansions or new 
roadway connections are feasible. This reinforces the 
need to coordinate transportation planning with land 
use, environmental constraints, and hazard mitigation 
strategies, ensuring that future improvements avoid high-
risk areas where possible or incorporate design measures 
to minimize long-term exposure and maintenance 
challenges.

The Town of Buena Vista includes a range of key community 
destinations that strongly influence how people travel throughout

destinations are not only widely used, but they also serve

access to a car who are more dependent on a safe and connected 
multimodal system. Prioritizing these linkages helps ensure 
equitable access, improves safety, and supports a more active and 
connected community.

 Freight Corridors and Facilities
Freight movement in Buena Vista is closely tied to the community’s 
functional classification network, which defines how different 
roadways serve regional, through, and local traffic. Freight systems 
are anchored by principal arterials, including US 24 and US 285, 
which provide regional connectivity and carry the highest levels of 
through traffic. These highways connect directly to minor arterials 
and major collectors such as Main Street and Arizona Street that 
distribute freight traffic into the community and provide access 
to commercial and light industrial land uses. This hierarchy allows 
freight to move efficiently from regional routes onto local-serving 
corridors without relying heavily on neighborhood streets. 
US 24 and US 285 carry some of the highest volumes in the study 
area, with segments reaching approximately 8,800 vehicles per 
day, reflecting their role as primary regional corridors. Main Street, 
functioning as a key arterial/collector through downtown, carries 
moderate volumes generally ranging from roughly 4,300 to 9,900 
vehicles per day depending on the segment, indicating a mix of 
local, visitor, and freight traffic. The presence of freight vehicles on 
corridors like Main Street introduces operational considerations, 
including turning movements, reduced speeds, and increased 
interaction with pedestrians in more active areas.
When developing a Transportation Master Plan (TMP), it is critical 
to align freight movement with both the functional classification 
system and observed traffic volumes. Principal arterials such as US 
24 and US 285 should continue to serve as primary freight corridors, 
given their higher capacity and regional function, while corridors 
like Main Street require a more balanced approach that considers 
freight access alongside downtown livability and pedestrian safety. 
Land use planning should support this framework by concentrating 
freight-intensive uses near higher-class, higher-volume roadways 
and limiting heavy truck activity in lower-volume, pedestrian-
oriented areas. This integrated approach ensures 
Buena Vista can support freight mobility while 
maintaining safety and community character.

Traffic operations within the Town of Buena Vista are currently 
stable and generally efficient, with the broader roadway network 
and the downtown core exhibiting consistent and balanced 
performance during peak travel periods. Level of service (LOS) 
analysis indicates that most study intersections across the Town 
operate at LOS C or better during both the AM and PM peaks, 
reflecting moderate traffic volumes, stable flow conditions, and 
acceptable levels of delay. 
Level of Service (LOS) analysis indicates that most study 
intersections across the Town operate at LOS C or better during 
both the AM and PM peaks, reflecting moderate traffic volumes, 
stable flow conditions, and acceptable levels of delay.  Along the 
Town’s primary corridors including US 24 and key cross streets 
such as Main Street, Crossman Avenue, Arizona Street, Colorado 
Avenue, Gregg Drive, and Rodeo Road traffic is well distributed and 
no individual intersection stands out as a significant bottleneck. 
Both the morning and evening peak periods demonstrate similar 
operational characteristics, with no meaningful degradation in the 
PM peak.

intersections along US 24 are beginning to show early signs of 
operational stress. Intersections currently operating at LOS C that 
may require future treatment include: US 24 & Baylor Drive; US 24 
& Steele Drive; US 24 & Main Street; US 24 & Arizona Street.

operational character than in outlying areas. Moderate delays 
are influenced not only by vehicle volumes but also by increased 
pedestrian activity, on-street parking movements, and closely 
spaced intersections, all of which contribute to friction within the 
roadway system. These conditions are typical and often desirable 
in a downtown setting, where slower speeds and multimodal 
interactions support accessibility and economic activity. As a result, 
some observed delay does not indicate a deficiency, but rather a 
balanced urban operating environment. In summary, Buena Vista’s 
roadway network, including its downtown core, is performing well 
under existing conditions.

 Crash Overview
Five years of crash data was evaluated as a part of the planning  
process, cover the years 2021-2025. Crash severity data shows that 
most crashes are non-injury events (239 incidents), representing 
approximately 78% of total crashes. Injury crashes (minor, serious, and 
possible) collectively account for a smaller but still significant share, 
indicating areas where safety improvements may be necessary.  There 
was one fatal crash recorded, highlighting that while fatalities are rare, 
severe incidents do occur. The distribution suggests a typical rural/
small-town safety profile where high-frequency, low-severity crashes 
dominate but targeted interventions may reduce injury severity. 
 Summary 
Crash type analysis indicates that wild animal collisions (63) and 
rear-end crashes (52) are the most common, reflecting a mix of rural 
environmental hazards and typical traffic conflicts. Broadside (41) 
and sideswipe crashes also show moderate frequency, suggesting 
intersection and lane-change risks.  Lower-frequency crash types 
include pedestrian (6), bicycle (3), and fixed-object collisions, which, 
while less frequent, are often associated with higher severity outcomes.

 Policy and Plan Review
The Town of Buena Vista’s has completed or been involved in the development of no less than ten related planning processes over the past 
several years. Existing plans and policy documents collectively establish a consistent vision centered on connectivity, multimodal access, and 
community character, but they vary in their level of detail, implementation focus, and geographic scale.
 Key Areas of Alignment
Across nearly all plans several foundational themes emerge:
Multimodal Connectivity
The Comprehensive Plan, Trails Plan, and Parks & Trails Inventory all emphasize 
expanding pedestrian and bicycle infrastructure and improving connectivity 
across the community.  Attention is consistently given to addressing barriers 
such as US 24 and improving crossings and linkages between neighborhoods, 
downtown, and recreational assets. 
Recreation and Tourism Integration
Multiple plans—including the Recreation Master Plan, Trails Plan, and Scenic 
Byways strategy—highlight the importance of trails, open space, and access to 
the Arkansas River in shaping travel patterns and supporting the local economy. 
Regional and Corridor-Level Coordination
Regional plans (e.g., the San Luis Valley RTP) and local efforts both identify US 24 
and US 285 as critical corridors, emphasizing improvements to safety, capacity, 
freight movement, and multimodal accommodations. 
Desire for Improved Safety
Safety particularly for non-motorized users is a recurring priority across plans, 
with consistent identification of high-risk areas such as major corridors, 
intersections, and trail crossings.
 Key Gaps Identified
Despite strong alignment,  
several consistent gaps emerge:
Lack of Implementation Frameworks
Few plans define funding strategies, phasing, or 
clear project prioritization, limiting their ability to 
guide near-term decisions.
Limited Integration Across  
Modes and Jurisdictions
While coordination is frequently cited as important, 
there is limited detail on how to align improvements 
across state, county, and local systems.
Transit and Mobility Options
Public transportation is identified as a need in 
multiple documents, particularly for economic 
development and aging populations, but remains 
largely undeveloped in terms of specific strategies.
Downtown and Parking Strategy Integration
The Parking Analysis provides targeted insight on 
downtown conditions, but broader integration with 
multimodal goals and land use strategies is limited.

 Parks & Trails Inventory (2022)
The Parks and Trails Inventory documents existing facilities and 
highlights opportunities to strengthen connections between parks, 
trails, and the broader transportation system. It identifies US 24 
as a barrier between different parts of Town and underscores the 
need for improved crossings and connectivity. The document 
provides a strong foundation of existing conditions, but does not 
establish clear priorities for implementation, leaving that step to 
future planning efforts.
 Signage & Wayfinding Plan (2018)
The Signage and Wayfinding Plan was created with goals to design 
a system that helps direct residents and visitors to destinations 
within the town; to identify the style quantity and type of signage 
needed; and to develop an implementation plan to outline the 
costs and resources needed.  The plan identified destinations and 
amenities in six distinct regions: East Main, River Park, South Main,

not have a distinct category, signage was intended for on-street 
signage and was shown in the refined final designs. Bicycle-related 
signage would be used in the Secondary Gateway and vehicle-
focused signage within major roadway corridors.

 Community Trails Plan (2015)
The Community Trails Plan’s purpose was to develop a system 
of connected non-motorized, multiuse pathways to increase 
recreational, educational, and alternative transportation 
opportunities for residents and visitors in the town’s area. The

the town prioritized avoiding busy intersections and hazardous 
conditions such as unstable slopes and inadequate lines of sight. 
On streets within town limits, wherever possible, the plan called for 
a 4 – 6 foot wide extension lane or shoulder added to both sides of 
existing or planned streets.

The parking analysis was conducted to inventory and understand 
the parking needs of Main Street from US 24 to South Main Street

public shuttle that runs along Main Street to encourage people to 
park farther away as well as make use of nearby vacant lots and 
partner with the Elementary School to allow visitor parking on
