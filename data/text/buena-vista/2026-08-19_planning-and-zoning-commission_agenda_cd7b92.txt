1. Business Item: Transportation Master Plan Introduction
Kopp introduced the Transportation Master Plan (TMP), informing the commission the project
kicked off in Spring 2026, focusing on community input. Kopp then introduced Ben Manibog, the
project manager from KLJ (the Town’s engineering consultant).
DRAFT
 
Ben Manibog began by providing an overview, covering existing conditions, community 
feedback, and comparing previous plan’s goals with new proposed goals. The TMP , last updated 
in 2004, is meant to align with recent existing plans, uplift multimodal transportation, and to 
provide a roadmap for the next 10-20 years. The new TMP will include town limits, 3 mile growth 
areas and major roads, but does not include roads and trails east of the river. When taking into 
consideration the existing conditions, it was noted that Buena Vista expects steady and 
manageable growth, increasing activity in tourist season, and housing affordability pressures. 
Additionally, on the topic of existing conditions, Manibog pointed out that the highest 
concentration of multimodal infrastructure is primarily in downtown and along the river and that 
the highest number of crashes occur along US 24. Following the existing conditions, the variety 
of public engagement events (online, in-person, pop-up events, surveys,etc.) were discussed, 
pointing out the highest priority areas identified are additional railroad crossing(s), US 24, W. 
Main, and Arizona; the lowest priority areas being along Marquette Dr and Greg Dr. The overall 
concerns varied between the focus groups, the top three priorities identified being safe crossings 
of major roads and railroad, addition of paved multimodal trails, and development of parking 
strategy. Goals identified by staff and County are safe transportation access, connectivity, 
managing seasonality, sense of place, sustainable maintenance, transparency, agency 
coordination, and attainable solution-oriented projects. 
3 BIG questions for commissioners to consider 
1. Do the existing conditions match your understanding of BV? 
The discussion generally confirmed the existing conditions analysis, while noting that some 
bicycle-related information may not fully reflect current conditions in Buena Vista. Kosley and 
Lane highlighted the significant increase in local e-bike use and questioned whether the bicycle 
data accurately represents current usage, while Ben clarified that the analysis relies primarily on 
available historic and existing data, which can be difficult to obtain. Kosley also noted a 
difference between staff and community perceptions of bicycle use and identified several 
mapping updates needed, including designated bicycle lanes on Rodeo, Arizona, and West Main, 
as well as an outdated depiction of the diagonal crosswalk at Gregg, which is now a four-way 
stop. 
2. Does the initial community engagement match your understanding of BV? 
The initial community engagement was generally viewed as reflective of Buena Vista, with the 
online mapping tool providing a valuable way for residents to identify concerns at specific 
locations; the engagement generated 129 survey responses and 75 map pins. Discussion noted 
that while residents often identified highly specific street or location-based issues, the 
Transportation Master Plan should synthesize these concerns into broader themes and strategies 
that address community needs more comprehensively. Differences between staff, stakeholder, 
and community feedback were attributed to varying perspectives and priorities, with staff and 
the Town/County considering broader system-wide needs and community members focusing on 
areas that directly affect their daily lives. Budget constraints and differing perceptions of issues 
such as E. Main Street and downtown parking were also noted as factors influencing these 
perspectives.

RE:   R-2 Old Town – Two-Family and Small Multifamily Building Forms 
 
Summary: Staff is seeking Planning Commission direction on whether two-family dwellings on 
adjoining lots in the R-2 Old Town Overlay (R-2 OT) should be allowed to share a party wall, and 
whether the Commission would like to consider small multifamily development more broadly in the 
district. 
 
On August 5, 2026, the Crossing Development Team requested consideration of a Code amendment to 
allow small multifamily in R-2 OT. The team described two stacked (“over-under”) duplexes on 
adjoining lots, attached along the shared lot line, for four units total. They believe this form could 
support attainable housing and is appropriate in a medium-density district. 
 
During Commission and staff discussion, it was identified that simply allowing small multifamily would 
not accommodate that configuration. Small multifamily is currently defined as three or four dwelling 
units in one building on one lot or parcel under single legal ownership. The proposed form would 
instead remain two separate two-family dwellings, each on its own lot, connected at a shared property 
line. 
 
Each qualifying lot can already accommodate a two-family dwelling, so the request does not increase 
allowable density. It does, however, change building form and massing by replacing the separation 
between two buildings with one continuous attached form. Small multifamily is currently allowed in 
the base R-2 district and in portions of R-1 OT, but was not included as an allowed use when the R-2 OT 
standards were established. 
 
Conceptually, the proposed configuration would be: 
 

     
 
The proposal would not increase the number of dwelling units otherwise permitted. Each lot (2500 sf 
or larger) can already accommodate a two-family dwelling. The primary difference is building form: 
instead of two buildings separated by side yards, the two duplex buildings would be joined along the 
common lot line, creating a larger continuous building mass that could visually resemble a four-unit 
building. 
 
The following options are presented for Planning Commission consideration. 
 
Option 1: Maintain Existing Standards 
Under this option, no substantive change would be made to accommodate the proposed building 
form. Each lot could contain a stacked two-family dwelling, but each building would continue to meet 
the applicable three-foot side setback. This maintains physical separation between principal buildings, 
but may result in two narrow three-foot yards between the duplexes. 
Conceptually: 
 
 
Option 2: Allow Party-wall Connections Between Stacked Two-family Dwellings 
The Code could allow stacked two-family dwellings on adjoining lots to use a zero-foot setback where 
the buildings are intentionally joined by a party wall. The two-family use would remain unchanged; the 
amendment would clarify how two otherwise-permitted duplexes may be arranged. This option would 
not require the Town to reconsider use permissions in R-2 OT or other zoning districts. 
 
Conceptually: 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

     
 
The Code could establish general party-wall standards addressing: 
• location of the party wall along the common lot line; 
• compliance with applicable building and fire codes; 
• independent compliance of each lot with applicable dimensional and development standards; 
• a recorded agreement establishing responsibility for maintenance, repair, access, replacement, 
and reconstruction of the party wall. 
 
Exterior Side Setback: If the shared-lot-line setback is reduced from three feet to zero, the Commission 
could require the non-party-wall side setback to increase from three feet to six feet. This would 
preserve the six feet of combined separation that would otherwise occur between detached duplexes, 
but consolidate that area into more functional exterior side yards. 
 
For example, two detached stacked duplexes in R-2 OT would each ordinarily provide a three-foot side 
setback, resulting in six feet of combined separation: 
 
 
Rather than eliminating that open area entirely, a party-wall standard could effectively reallocate it to 
the outside of the buildings. 
 
 
Under this approach, a stacked duplex that ordinarily requires a three-foot side setback would have a 
zero-foot setback at the party wall and a minimum six-foot setback on the opposite side. 
 
6’ total 
separation 
     
This could help ensure that the party-wall provision does not simply result in increased site coverage or 
reduced open area. Instead, the space that would otherwise consist of two narrow three-foot strips 
between buildings could be consolidated into more functional side yards. 
 
Building Appearance and Massing: 
The existing use-specific standard for two-family dwellings provides that: 
“Two-family dwellings shall be designed in a manner so that the façade is uniform and 
consistent as to appear as one (1) larger structure, not two (2) different units.” 
 
That standard makes sense when applied to an individual duplex. With two stacked duplexes 
connected across a party wall, however, the Town would need to determine how that principle should 
apply to the resulting four-unit building mass. 
 
One approach would be for each duplex to continue to read as its own building. Under that approach, 
the upper and lower units on Lot A would have a unified appearance as one duplex, while the upper 
and lower units on Lot B would similarly appear as a second duplex. 
 
Architectural articulation at or near the party wall could reinforce the appearance of two house-scale 
buildings joined together rather than one larger four-unit building. 
 
This would be conceptually similar to the existing use standard for single-family attached development: 
“Each single-family attached building shall exhibit the characteristics of a series of single-family 
detached dwellings that are arranged in an attached side-by-side fashion and shall be designed 
to protect the character of single-family detached residences.” 
 
Alternatively, the Commission could allow the entire four-unit structure to have a single unified 
architectural expression. 
 
Under that approach, the distinction between the two duplexes would primarily be a zoning, lot, and 
ownership distinction rather than a visual one. The development could appear to be one larger four-
unit residential building. That approach provides greater architectural flexibility but also further blurs 
the distinction between two attached two-family dwellings and a small multifamily/fourplex building, 
which is not currently an allowed use in R-2 OT. 
 

     
 
Option 3: Allow Small Multifamily in R-2 OT 
The Commission could instead, or additionally, allow three- and four-unit small multifamily buildings in 
R-2 OT. This is a broader policy change because it would add a residential use rather than only 
modifying the form of an already-permitted duplex. 
 
If pursued, small multifamily could be subject to additional form and dimensional standards such as 
minimum lot dimensions, building width or footprint, usable open space, parking location, and house-
scale façade treatment.  
 
A hybrid approach could also allow a qualifying small multifamily building by right while requiring 
Special Use review for larger or more concentrated development. 
 
This option would not, by itself, accommodate the current two-lot concept because the existing 
definition requires all three or four units to be in one building on one lot or parcel under single legal 
ownership. 
 
Considering by-right vs special use 
If the Commission is interested in allowing small multifamily in R-2 OT but does not want it broadly 
permitted without additional review, a hybrid approach could also be considered. 
 
For example, one small multifamily building could be permitted by right when all applicable 
dimensional and form standards are satisfied, while a Special Use Permit could be required when a 
project exceeds specified thresholds. Potential triggers could include multiple small multifamily 
buildings under common development, a building exceeding a specified footprint, or a defined 
concentration along a block face. 
 
A simple “one fourplex per block” limitation is possible but may create administrative challenges 
regarding how a block is defined, how existing developments are counted, and how simultaneous 
applications are handled. Form- and impact-based standards may provide a more direct means of 
protecting neighborhood character.

Option 2 is the most targeted way to accommodate the requested form without requiring 
consideration of new residential uses in additional zoning districts. If the Commission is comfortable 
with the underlying density but concerned about massing, it could pair the party-wall allowance with 
an increased exterior side setback and architectural articulation. If the Commission is comfortable with

required if the Commission prefers the existing standards.  
 
Additional Cleanup 
This review also identified inconsistencies in minimum lot area and lot width. Two-family dwellings 
have specific lot-area requirements in certain districts, while small and large multifamily generally rely 
on the underlying district standards together with setbacks, coverage, open space/landscaping, 
parking, and height. This can result in a two-unit dwelling having a more restrictive lot requirement 
than a three- or four-unit building. 
 
The Commission may wish to determine whether minimum lot area and width should continue to 
manage residential intensity. If so, duplex standards should be applied consistently and corresponding 
standards established for small multifamily. Alternatively, open space, coverage, setbacks, parking, 
height, and other form standards may provide sufficient controls. 
 
The minimum lot width for single-family attached/townhome development should also be reviewed. 
The use-specific standard requires at least 15 feet of street frontage per dwelling; where units are on 
individual lots, the minimum lot width could be reduced to align with that 15-foot frontage 
requirement. 
 
Direction Requested: Staff is seeking Planning Commission direction on the following questions: 
1. Preferred approach: Does the Commission prefer (1) maintaining the existing standards, (2) 
allowing adjoining stacked duplexes to share a party wall, or (3) allowing small multifamily 
within R-2 OT? 
2. Party-wall standards: If Option 2 is preferred, should staff develop standards that (a) 
increase the opposite exterior side setback to preserve the otherwise-required separation and 
(b) require each duplex to retain an identifiable house-scale architectural form? 
3. Related dimensional cleanup: Does the Commission support staff addressing the identified 
inconsistencies in minimum lot area/width for duplex, small multifamily, and single-family 
attached development as part of the dimensional-table amendments?
