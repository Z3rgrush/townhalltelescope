10. Discussion and Review of 2026 Amended Budget 
During the 2026 amended budget review, the Board examined key financial adjustments ahead of formal 
action set for November 12th, while year-end department spending remains strictly controlled. Trustees 
focused on resolving administrative and accounting discrepancies, notably verifying inter-fund water loan 
repayments to avoid high interest penalties, correcting line-item projection errors, and ensuring proper 
reporting of eliminated temporary positions. The discussion also covered statutory and operational cost 
pressures moving into 2027, such as mandated pension contribution increases, rising utility costs, the minor 
financial impact of recent staff departures, and managing delayed invoices for major capital initiatives like 
the Spruce Mountain project. 
11. 
Discussion and Review of the Draft 2027 Budget 
 
During discussions regarding the 2027 budget, municipal staff identified approximately $200,000 in potential 
cost reductions to help address a projected $500,000 annual deficit, which the Board declared unsustainable 
given that long-term relief options like PPRTA funding, mill levies, or regional service transitions provide no 
immediate financial certainty. To address major cost drivers since 2022, specifically budget growth across 
police, fire, and administration, alongside fixed burdens such as legal claims, contracted development 
reviews, and local matching funds for the Spruce Mountain project, a proposal was presented to cut 
proportional percentages across departments to prioritize a balanced budget over existing staffing levels. 
Acknowledging that right-sizing municipal operations will lead to a noticeable decline in public service levels 
like street plowing and infrastructure maintenance, the Board reached a consensus to take a stringent 
approach to fiscal management and scheduled a policy workshop to review staff scenarios and finalize cost-
cutting directives. 
12. Discussion, Review and Possible Action Regarding Resolution 40-2026 Authorizing a Shade 
Structure  
The Board considered a resolution authorizing Awake Palmer Lake to construct an open-timber shelter at the 
pickleball courts at no cost to the town, targeting completion by Thanksgiving. Situated south of the 
restrooms to preserve emergency and railroad access, the new public rental facility will require only minimal 
town maintenance while adhering to standard park rules and dark-sky lighting guidelines.

1. Discussion and Direction regarding the 2027 Town Budget 
Trustee Moseley raised a point of order regarding a requested agenda item regarding Eco Spa. Town 
Administrator Patrick Davidson responded that as it was a non-emergency item request, it was not scheduled 
based on availability of legal counsel and time to prepare a presentation.The Board continued discussion 
on financial strategy for the upcoming fiscal year while resolving current year-end deficits. To prevent further 
depletion of town reserves, Town Administrator Davidson introduced a scaled-back operational plan 
centered strictly on maintaining staff compensation and essential public safety functions. Through 
widespread spending freezes, service cutbacks, and staffing realignments, the town bridged the majority of 
its initial budget deficit. The remaining balance will be absorbed through minor, one-time internal fund 
transfers that are projected to be replenished over time by future municipal parking revenues. Revenue 
streams remain constrained by modest commercial development, local business turnover, and regional 
competition affecting sales and excise tax collections. Municipal departments are adjusting to reduced 
operating budgets and leaner personnel structures. Legal and financial management functions are being 
realigned or brought in-house to attempt to curb professional fees, while emergency services and public 
works will operate at minimum core staffing levels, limiting non-essential maintenance and out-of-area 
deployments. Major capital endeavors, such as key infrastructure and roadway improvements, are being 
carefully phased to maximize grant coverage while meeting mandatory project deadlines. The Board will 
review the remaining enterprise budgets and present the final budget to meet the statory deadlines.

BOARD OF TRUSTEES – ADMINISTRATOR MEMO 
 
DATE: October 16, 2026 ITEM NO.  SUBJECT:  Weekly Update 10-16-
2026 Presented by:  Patrick Davidson, Town Administrator 
 
MEMORANDUM 
 
Good morning, Mayor, Mayor Pro Tem, and Members of the Board of Trustees.  Here is your newest weekly 
update: 
 
Agenda Packet for October 22nd.  Unfortunately, this is going to be another large packet of actions items and 
information.  Please plan on spending a little extra time on the packet in preparation of the meeting, and as 
always, let me know if you have any questions. 
 
Fiber Installation on the Bridge.  I have been approached several times to allow the installation of fiber optic 
cables across the bridge at the lake.  While the idea is great, it is not without substantial limitations.  A review 
of the terms of the Pedestrian Bridge Crossing Agreement, it identifies the UP as “railroad” and the Town of 
Palmer Lake as “Licensee”.  These are legal terms of art and can have profound consequences.  In essence the 
Town has on-going permission to make use of the airspace for a pedestrian bridge and those items directly 
related to the operations of a pedestrian bridge.  It is not an open-ended easement that allows for unfettered 
use of the airspace above the railroad track.  I support the idea of fiber and believe it is necessary, and perhaps 
we can gain authorization by the UPRR, but for now I would recommend not proceeding.  As you may have 
been, or will become informed on this matter, I wanted to provide the information to you in advance. 
 
2026 Budget Discussions.  I have been asked by both members of the Board and the public regarding the 2026 
budget.  The key questions center on the anticipated draw from reserves and the proposed sale of property.  In 
December 2025, when the Board approved the FY 2026 budget, the Board authorized the draw of $503,000 
from savings to meet any budget shortfall.  In addition, the Board implied the sale of property in the amount 
of $385,000 when it authorized it to be included as “revenue” for purposes of planning the FY 2026 
budget.  Without the completion of the sale, the deficiency automatically increased from $503,000 to $888,000 
without further action being necessary by the Board.  To be clear, the Town cannot continue to cover expenses 
in the amount of $500,000 to $1M per year from savings and plan on having reserves in the future.  
 
Sales Tax Scale.  I have also been asked several questions about economic development, and the promotion 
of small businesses within the community to bridge the sales tax deficiency.  In this context, the size and scope 
of sales tax must be taken into consideration.  The current sales tax for Palmer Lake is 3%.  To have covered 
the $503,000 deficiency for 2026, the Town would have needed a business, or businesses, capable of 
generating an additional $16.77M in taxable retail sales in 2026.  Or, looking to the 2026 total deficiency of

HISTORICAL BACKGROUND 
The voter-initiated annexation measure arose while the Town was considering the Monument 
Ridge West/Buc-ee’s annexation. The initiative petition was approved as to form on April 11, 
2025, submitted to the Town Clerk on May 6, 2025, and determined sufficient on May 27, 2025, 
with no protests received. At its June 30, 2025, meeting, the Board considered whether to adopt 
the initiated ordinance unchanged or refer it to the electorate, with authority to submit an 
alternative ordinance. The Board directed that the initiated measure appears on the September 9, 
2025, special municipal election ballot.1 
 
BALLOT AND ELECTION 
The ballot asked whether the proposed initiated ordinance should be adopted. Its Section 1 required 
approval of “any proposal to annex land” by a majority of Palmer Lake registered electors at a 
regular or special municipal election and withheld annexation effectiveness until that approval. 
Section 2 needed compliance with C.R.S. §§ 31-12-101 et seq. and other applicable laws; Section 
4 applied the requirement to certain previously approved but unfinished annexations. Voters 
approved the measure on September 9, 2025, by 765 votes to 375, approximately 67% in favor.2 
 
IMPLEMENTING ORDINANCE AND BOARD DISCUSSION. 
At the September 25, 2025, meeting, the Board adopted Ordinance No. 05-2025, amending former 
Code § 17.14.040 to implement the approved initiative; the annexation provision is now codified 
at § 17.05.150. The signed ordinance reproduces the approved ballot language in its recitals and 
directs incorporation into the Town Code. The September 25, 2025, agenda identifies Ordinance 
05-2025 as business item 13. Contemporary reporting of that meeting records Town Attorney Scott 
Krob acknowledging that the initiative did not specify when the annexation election should occur, 
discussing more than one possible sequence, and recommending a vote after an eligibility

PRINCIPAL ISSUES 
The review identifies the following concerns: 
 
1. Electorate: The separate Town vote is not expressly reconciled with the landowners and 
electors recognized under state annexation law. 
 
2. Election timing: The ordinance specifies neither the required stage of voting nor the effect 
of an early or late election. 
 
3. Governmental responsibilities: The roles of the Planning Commission, Board of Trustees, 
and electorate are not integrated. 
 
4. Scope of approval: The ordinance does not identify precisely what voters approve or when 
later project changes matter. 
 
5. Development feasibility: Uncertainty over costs, sequencing, and finality may prevent 
otherwise permissible proposals from advancing. 
 
6. Special circumstances: Small parcels, public lands, rights-of-way, and previously approved 
annexations create application questions. 
 
7. Declaratory relief: Judicial interpretation cannot be assumed to provide substantive 
procedures omitted from adopted legislation.

1.  THE ELECTORATE.  
 
The ordinance assigns its municipal vote to existing Palmer Lake registered electors, while Article 
II, § 30 of the Colorado Constitution and C.R.S. § 31-12-112 recognize landowners and electors 
of the territory proposed for annexation in specified statutory circumstances. This creates 
differently constituted electorates for decisions concerning the same property.5 
 
Analysis and Conclusion. In City of Aspen v. Howell, 459 P.2d 764 (Colo. 1969), the Supreme 
Court recognized participation by nonresident landowners in an Annexation Act election and noted 
the equal-protection concern that a contrary interpretation could create. Minch v. Town of Mead, 
957 P.2d 1054 (Colo. App. 1998), upheld an added municipal vote in a statutory town against the 
statutory and initiative-power challenges presented. Minch did not establish that the Town may 
displace voting rights secured in a required territorial election. The adopted ordinance does not 
explain the relationship between the two electorates. Accordingly, any administration that 
substitutes the municipal electorate for an electorate required by state law presents a serious 
Constitutional concern. A genuinely separate municipal approval vote poses a different 
constitutional question; exclusion from that additional vote is not, by itself, a settled equal-
protection violation.6 
 
2.  UNDEFINED ELECTION SEQUENCE.  
 
The ordinance does not identify the point at which the Town must hold the municipal election. 
The absence is substantive, not simply a clerical scheduling matter. 
 
Analysis and Conclusion. The Annexation Act specifies petitions, notices, hearings, findings, and 
annexation by ordinance, but it does not expressly sequence the separate election established by 
Palmer Lake’s initiative. An early election could precede Planning Commission review, the 
Board’s eligibility findings, agreement negotiations, and analysis of land uses and infrastructure. 
A late election could follow extensive applicant expenditures and governmental review, with no 
assurance that voters will approve the annexation. The Town’s December 11, 2025, Board minutes 
record four competing approaches to the election sequence, illustrating that officials could not 
identify one procedure from the existing text. The ordinance supplies no clear choice among them.7 
 
3.  PLANNING COMMISSION AND BOARD RESPONSIBILITIES.  
 
The ordinance does not integrate the Planning Commission’s role in land-use and zoning review 
with the Board’s separate statutory annexation powers and the municipal vote. 
 
Analysis and Conclusion. Planning Commission recommendations, where required by the Town 
Code, aid the Board in its decisions concerning land use and zoning; they do not replace the

Board’s statutory annexation findings. Under C.R.S. §§ 31-12-110 and 31-12-111, the Board 
decides eligibility and may, where allowed, adopt an annexation ordinance. A finding of eligibility 
does not compel discretionary approval. Nor does the local ordinance say whether a Board policy 
rejection requires an election on a proposal that the Board has declined to adopt. The omission 
creates uncertainty over the responsibilities and effect of decisions by each body. It must not be 
solved by assuming that the Planning Commission, Board, and voters have interchangeable roles.8 
 
4.  WHAT THE VOTERS APPROVE.  
 
The ordinance does not define whether the ballot concerns only a territorial boundary change, a 
negotiated annexation agreement, an associated zoning proposal, or a completed development 
plan. 
 
Analysis and Conclusion. This silence affects the information needed for a meaningful vote and 
the legal durability of that vote. A conceptual proposal may materially change during normal 
review; a fully detailed design cannot realistically be frozen against later revisions. Changes in 
building layout, utility design, access, or intensity ordinarily proceed through applicable land-use 
and building approval processes. The ordinance states no threshold for when a change to the 
proposal voters saw would require a new annexation vote. We cannot infer that routine building 
revisions require elections; equally, we cannot supply an omitted material-change standard from 
assumptions about voter intent.9 
 
5.  CUMULATIVE COST AND UNCERTAINTY.  
 
The absence of a defined sequence, ballot subject, and finality standard exposes applicants and the 
Town to uncertainty that can independently discourage annexation even when annexation is not 
expressly prohibited. 
 
Analysis and Conclusion. A developer may be unwilling to fund engineering, traffic, utilities, 
and development review without some indication that annexation is attainable. Yet an early 
favorable election may offer little protection if the Planning Commission or Board later cannot 
approve the proposed use or necessary conditions. Requiring completed review before any election 
transfers substantial sunk-cost risk to an applicant. Neither consequence, standing alone, proves 
unlawful regulation; together they show that the ordinance lacks a predictable administrative path 
from application to effective annexation. That is an operational deficiency regardless of the 
motives behind adoption. 
 
6.  SPECIAL ANNEXATIONS AND PRIOR ACTIONS.  
 
The broad reference to “any proposal” offers no detailed treatment of minor parcels, municipal 
land, public rights-of-way, qualifying enclaves or surrounded land, boundary adjustments, or 
proposals previously approved but not completed.

Analysis and Conclusion. The ordinance references statutory compliance and contains provisions 
concerning certain statutory annexations and a lookback for previously approved transactions. 
Those provisions must be read as written and against applicable statutory exceptions; the existence 
of an exception cannot be assumed merely because a project appears routine. The lookback also 
requires close examination of effective dates and the status of prior approvals before the Town can 
administer it consistently. The ordinance gives no proportional distinction between small 
administrative annexations and large development-driven annexations.10 
 
7.  LIMITS OF DECLARATORY RELIEF.  
 
A judicial declaration of the ordinance’s meaning is not the same as legislative adoption of 
procedures the ordinance does not contain. 
 
Analysis and Conclusion. C.R.S. § 13-51-106 allows courts to decide questions of construction 
or validity involving municipal ordinances. Minch determined the effect and validity of Mead’s 
adopted requirements; it did not authorize the judiciary to write a full implementation scheme. A 
court may construe existing language against controlling law, but it cannot be presumed to 
establish the election calendar, Planning Commission coordination, ballot content, treatment of 
later project changes, and other substantive decisions omitted from a voter-approved measure. 
Moreover, the availability and scope of relief depend on a genuine, justiciable controversy. A 
declaratory action should not be treated as an administrative substitute for clear legislation.11 
 
OVERALL CONCLUSION 
Ordinance 05-2025 sets up a mandatory voter-approval condition without establishing the 
complete process required to administer it reliably. The most significant issues are the relationship 
between the municipal and territorial electorates; election timing; Planning Commission and Board 
review; the precise subject and finality of voter approval; and the cumulative uncertainty imposed 
on applicants and Town operations. The Town can acknowledge the electorate’s adopted policy 
while candidly showing the resulting deficiencies. Some questions may be resolved through 
existing law and ordinary interpretation; others involve substantive choices not stated in the 
ordinance. The next step is to evaluate those questions through the established municipal process, 
without assuming a particular legislative or electoral outcome.

CMI in the amount of $3,900 as has been previously identified by Trustee Moseley.  That as corrected 
shortly thereafter (3/3/2026), without additional concerns, and this was confirmed with the Board of 
Trustees (3/13/2026).  This matter has been brought up again via email and public comments on 
September 4, 2026, September 8, 2026, and again on October 22, 2026.  This, at least in part, was a point 
for the non-approval of the Buc-ee’s payment.  To be clear, this matter of the $3,900 was corrected when 
identified, and remains resolved, and should be put to rest. 
  
Second, there were concerns regarding how long this matter has taken to get resolved.  The Board sought 
to have some kind of “confirmation” that Buc-ee’s was doing something more than just withdrawing its 
annexation application.  From my perspective, and as I have shared with most of you, this is sufficient to 
end the process.  Several members of the Board have continual sought something further, in writing, to 
presumably act as a defense to any action by Buc-ee’s in the future.  There have been on-going concerns 
that Buc-ee’s does not respond to correspondence, is ignoring the Town’s attorneys, and that it is either 
the Staff’s fault or the fault of the attorneys.  This is not the case.  Members of the Town Board have 
sought something from the applicant that is not required and is out of the normal course of business.  
  
There have also been concerns that the Board has not been kept abreast of what the attorneys are doing 
and that there are on-going attorneys fees for non-action.  When there is nothing to report, Staff and 
counsel have informed you nonetheless.  The Board was aware of the amount of the check prior to the 
meeting, and direction was given that while Staff could simply approve the payment because it was 
returning funds to Buc-ee’s, there was a consensus that the amount discussed should be placed on the 
agenda as is the case with any other larger payment.

On October 22, 2026, the Board denied the final payment to Buc-ee’s to close out this matter.  More 
specifically, this was because the Board did not feel comfortable with the amount stated, and that it did 
not conform to a Trustee’s internal recordkeeping.  The Board must rely on the actions of staff in making 
these determinations.  Members of the public, political action committees, and outside attorneys cannot 
and should not influence Board’s recommended courses of action.  In this instance, the check to Buc-
ee’s was an amount determined several months ago, was confirmed with the Town’s legal staff, was 
known to the Board, was reviewed and approved by Town Staff, and by all measures appears 
correct.  Recall that this is not the Town’s money, this is returning Buc-ees money to them.  
  
I know there is a history of what has transpired.  It is important that as a board and as a community that 
we move forward.  Placing this item in the rearview mirror seems the appropriate way to proceed.
