PROCLAMATION RECOGNIZING “CREEK WEEK 2026” 
 
 
WHEREAS,  Town of Palmer Lake is fortunate to have extensive and diverse natural resources, such 
as forests, grasslands, riparian areas, lakes, creeks and a wide variety of open spaces; and 
 
WHEREAS,  the Fountain Creek watershed, including Fountain Creek, related wetlands,   
  existing trails, and recreational facilities, is a unique watershed that is an important  
  resource and asset to the residents and visitors of Fountain and the Pikes Peak  
  Region; and 
 
WHEREAS,   the Fountain Creek Watershed District is partnering with the City of Colorado Springs, 
Colorado Springs Utilities, El Paso County, Pueblo County, the Cities of Manitou 
Springs, Fountain, and towns of Monument and Green Mountain Falls, along with 
numerous community organizations to coordinate the 13th Annual “Creek Week” 
cleanup, which will encourage the protection, restoration and maintenance of the 
Fountain Creek watershed; and 
 
WHEREAS,   this 9-day litter clean-up effort kicks off in Fountain Creek Regional Park on September 
26th and runs through October 4th, 2026 throughout the watershed; and 
 
WHEREAS,  “Creek Week” programs and activities are designed to raise awareness about the littering 
issue within our watershed, to encourage organizations and individuals to collect litter 
and debris to make Fountain Creek and the watershed cleaner and safer; and 
 
WHEREAS,  businesses, churches, schools, non-profits, neighborhood associations, youth groups, 
service clubs and individuals are encouraged to form a "Creek Crew” to clean up at a 
“Creek Week” project site; and  
 
WHEREAS, “Creek Week” litter removal activities will reduce pollution in our creeks and clogs in 
our waterways that can lead to flooding, provide for a safer drinking water supply, and 
enhance wildlife habitat and property values.  
 
NOW, THEREFORE, the Board of Trustees of the Town of Palmer Lake, Colorado hereby proclaim 
September 26 through October 4, 2026, as “Creek Week” in the Town of Palmer Lake and encourage our 
citizens to help protect, restore, and maintain our waterways by participating in “Creek Week” activities. 
 
DONE THIS 10th day of September 2026 in the Town of Palmer Lake, Colorado.

8. Discussion and Possible Action Regarding a Moratorium on Data Centers 
The Board discussed initiating a minimum six-month moratorium on all data centers to allow the planning 
commission time to evaluate municipal code, zoning, and environmental impacts. The primary concerns 
associated with potential data center development include high electricity demand, significant water 
consumption from local aquifers, and potential conflicts with the Town’s Master Plan, including 
considerations related to noise, light pollution, and the overall scale of such facilities. A six-month 
moratorium would provide the Town and appropriate boards and commissions with an opportunity to 
become better informed about data center development, evaluate potential impacts, and review the Town 
Code and applicable regulations. This review period would allow the Town to identify and make any 
necessary code or policy adjustments before considering future data center applications. 
Public Comment: 
Kent Hutson — Requested a six-month moratorium on data centers so the commission can draft regulating 
ordinances. 
  
9. Discussion and Update Regarding Stormwater Drainage 
The Board discussed town-wide stormwater drainage concerns and directed staff to research potential grant 
opportunities and consult with engineering professionals regarding funding for a comprehensive stormwater 
study. The study may require a local match and would help identify and prioritize long-term infrastructure 
improvements. In the interim, Public Works was tasked with addressing immediate culvert mitigation needs. 
The Town Administrator reported that he had met with consultants who had previously provided 
stormwater-related guidance to the Town. He is evaluating potential costs and determining how the project 
could be appropriately prioritized through the budget process, should the Board identify a comprehensive 
stormwater study as a priority.  
10. Discussion, Direction, and Possible Action Regarding Bear Activity and Related Trash 
Management 
Trustee Beeson initiated a discussion regarding increased bear activity and related trash concerns, including 
concerns that unsecured garbage associated with some short-term rentals (STRs) may be contributing to the 
issue. The Board discussed the potential for transient occupants and property owners or operators who are 
not responsive to complaints to leave garbage unsecured, creating potential public safety and nuisance 
concerns.The Board discussed potential strategies to strengthen bear-mitigation and trash enforcement 
throughout the Town. Staff noted that bear activity is a community-wide issue affecting both short-term 
rental properties and full-time residences and emphasized the importance of applying enforcement 
Page 19 of 72
measures consistently. 
While recognizing that bear activity and unsecured trash are significant concerns, the Board determined that 
additional discussion and review are needed before making changes to the Town Code or adopting additional 
regulations.The Town Attorney and Town Administrator reviewed the Town’s existing code requirements and 
discussed recently enacted state legislation that provides additional tools for addressing trash storage and 
securing waste. The Board discussed using the existing Town Code, along with the new state requirements, to 
establish consistent expectations for all residents and property owners and to strengthen the Town’s ability 
to address unsecured trash that may contribute to bear activity. 
The discussion focused on continued education, consistent enforcement, and further evaluation of potential 
code or policy updates to address the Town’s bear and trash concerns. 
Public Comment: 
Katherine Pietsch — Emphasized that bear-mitigation efforts must apply to all residents rather than singling 
out one group, urging the community to officially report trash violations so the town can maintain the 
necessary documentation to deny business licenses to repeat offenders. 
Paul Olivier — Informed the board of a new state law requiring trash cans to remain secured in enclosures 
except on collection days, recommending that the town review the statute to update local codes and enable 
police officers to issue citations for non-compliance. 
Kent Hutson — Clarified the technical standards for wildlife-resistant containers, and highlighted an updated 
state law imposing direct, escalating fines for leaving food or trash unsecured in bear-populated areas.

BACKGROUND  
Considering current budget expenditures, and in a view towards creating the 2027 annual budget 
for Palmer Lake, the Board has sought information related to legal expenses for 2026. These 
expenditures fall within the following categories and amounts: 
 
CORA 20,865.00 
General 48,884.00 
Investigations 43,356.00 
Land Use 18,699.18 
Litigation 34,328.42 
Municipal Court Services 3,715.00 
Water General 4,757.00 
Grand Total 174,604.60 
 
This encompasses the legal services provided by the ten (10) attorneys/law firms used by the Town 
of Palmer Lake in the prior eight (7) months. In general, a Town the approximate size of Palmer 
Lake should predict legal fees in the range of $50,000-$60,000 per year for general operations. 
Consequently, Palmer Lake’s legal expenses for the past eight (7) months are three times what one 
would estimate as a regular operating legal budget for a community. 
 
ANALYSIS 
I have had the opportunity to review the billing summaries and invoices for the law firms kept by 
the Town since the start of the 2026 calendar year. While the overall expenses for the Town are 
large, in reviewing the individual totals spent rendering legal services, they do not appear 
disproportional for the complexity of the work involved, the time involved on behalf of the 
attorneys, the billing rates within the State of Colorado, and the difficulty in the overall level of 
legal work.  
 
 
 
 
Page 48 of 72
Retention of Attorneys and Firms 
 
One concern is which attorney within a firm will perform the requested work. Although 
engagement agreements often name primary and secondary attorneys, other firm personnel may 
manage portions of a matter at lower rates. Associates and junior attorneys may be qualified for 
specific tasks but not necessarily prepared to attend Board meetings, advise clients, or assume 
broader responsibilities. This division of work is standard in legal practice. Firms appropriately 
assign tasks based on knowledge, ability, and cost; using associates and paralegals can reduce 
client expenses. However, attorneys within a firm are not always interchangeable for Board 
meetings, consultations, or responses to client questions. 
 
A second concern is the assumption that work stops at once when a client ends the representation. 
In practice, winding down a matter and transferring it to another firm may take days or weeks. The 
client bears these customary transition costs. Although many firms try to minimize them, necessary 
post-termination work may remain billable. Such work is not performed to generate added fees, 
but to ensure the smoothest possible transition between attorneys and firms. 
 
It should also be noted that in the case of certain types of cases, negotiations, and action that 
completing a legal action requires action and cooperation by the opposing party or attorney. Legal 
actions do not occur in a vacuum. In some instances, opposing parties and attorneys are less than 
cooperative in taking the final steps to resolve matters. This may be based on caseload, resentment 
towards the settlement/resolution, or other matters. Regardless of what the outcome litigation and 
legal issues are complex, emotional, and cannot simply be considered business as usual. 
 
One would expect a small community to seek legal advice from several sources. A firm that works 
within the realm of general municipal law may not have the experience to deal with complex water 
issues, employment law, civil litigation, and related matters. While there is a baseline smallest 
standard of education to practice law, it is just that, the minimum, and does not encompass all the 
areas of law. Like the medical profession, there are “experts” within the law and those should be 
sought out, as necessary. 
 
Litigation defense also poses a considerable challenge in finding just one firm to aid with all 
litigation. Not only are there specialties within the practice of law, but there may also be underlying 
conflicts of interest which likewise allow an attorney to aid the Town in one context while not 
authorizing representation in another legal matter. Attorneys are bound to the Rules of Professional 
Conduct, state law, federal law, and other regulations that may help or prohibit their role in 
representation.  
 
The Role of CIRSA in Legal Fees and Defense 
 
CIRSA is not traditional insurance; it is a municipal self-insurance pool in which government 
entities combine funds to provide coverage and services. As a member-driven, nonprofit 
organization, CIRSA emphasizes proactive training and education rather than merely responding 
to claims. 
 
Page 49 of 72
CIRSA, however, can be analogous to insurance policy in several ways. For example, there are 
deductibles, premiums, scopes of coverage, caps on coverage, and other items found in more 
modern insurance agreements. CIRSA covers not only general liability and property, but also 
errors and omissions coverage, as well as coverage associated with law enforcement and fire 
operations. However, to restate, there are limits on the coverage, and once exhausted, the Town 
may oversee a part of the costs including legal defenses. 
 
Another insurance-like aspect of CIRSA warrants consideration. Skilled plaintiffs’ attorneys may 
draft pleadings to include or avoid claims that CIRSA will defend. For example, after a motor 
vehicle accident, an attorney would assert all claims that could provide recovery through CIRSA. 
Conversely, an attorney looking to impose liability directly on the Town may frame the claims to 
fall outside CIRSA’s coverage. This possibility should always be considered when relying on 
CIRSA to defend the Town.
