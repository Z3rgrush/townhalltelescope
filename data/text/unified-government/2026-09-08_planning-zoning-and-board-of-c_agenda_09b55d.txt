Staff Recommendation starts at 47:14: Alyssa Marcy, Long Range Planner, stated 
that the library is within the environs of multiple local historic districts and sites, which is 
why a Certificate of Appropriateness is needed for demolition. The library is also within 
the Downtown Commercial Historic District, which was approved by the State Historic 
Preservation Office in 2024, which is why a State Law Review is required. Based on the 
Unified Government’s zoning code, there are only two (2) exemptions for demolitions. 
The demolition cannot be visible from the street and the demolition needs to include 
rehabilitation following the Secretary of Interior’s guidelines. Neither exemption would fit 
this project. As well, state law does not allow for demolition of historic sites. With that, 
staff recommends denial.  
 
Marcy also added that according to the State Historic Preservation Office, demolishing 
the library, which is currently a contributing factor to the Downtown Historic District, 
would not affect the listing of the entire district. However, if more buildings were 
eliminated, that would lead to losing the state-listed status.  
 
Commissioner Welcome-Hill directed a question to Laura Pellegrino, Director of the 
Kansas City, Kansas Public Library, on how building the new library would affect the 
reserves for the Wyandotte County Library Board in coordination with taxes that have 
already been collected. Pellegrino responded that that fund is collected by taxes paid by 
Piper, Edwardsville, and Turner. The demolition and construction of the new library is 
funded by the Kansas City Kansas Public Schools mill levy, which doesn’t impact the 
reserves for the Unified Governments Wyandotte County Library Board.

Summary  
 
The Cross -Lines Retirement Center (1970, 1976) is located at 3030 -3100 Powell Avenue in Kansas City, 
Wyandotte County, Kansas. This one building, constructed in two phases (1970 and 1976) consists of two 
rectangular apartment towers connected with a one -story lobby. The Modern Movement building has a 
reinforced concrete and steel structural system, a mix of brick and concrete cladding, and flat roofs. It is a good 
local example of the Residential property subtype of the Urban Renewal Development property type as outlined 
in the Urban Renewal-Resources in the United States Multiple Property Documentation Form (the MPDF). 
 
Phase 1, constructed in 1970, is a five-story apartment building with brick cladding with a flat roof. A one-story 
brick-clad projection from the west wall historically served as the main entrance, and two historic concrete stair 
towers project out from the east and west ends. The interior of Phase 1 contains reside ntial apartment units 
arranged along a double -loaded corridor. Phase 2, constructed in 1976, is a nine -story apartment building with 
distinctive concrete fins and aluminum panels along the exterior walls, and a flat roof. A historic one -story 
connection at the east wall serves as the main entrance for both sections. On the interior, Phase 2 contains a mix 
of tenant amenity spaces, offices, and a communal kitchen on the first floor. The upper floors contain residential 
apartment units arranged along a centra l double -loaded corridor. The open interior internally connects both 
towers (phases) of the development. In addition to the Cross -Lines Retirement Center, the site includes two 
contributing parking lots to the north and south. In all t he Cross-Lines Retirement Center retains integrity. The 
building has seen limited exterior alterations that mainly include replacement windows in Phase 1. A  recent 
project inside Phase 1 only included updating unit finishes. Some units were combined at an unknown date prior 
to that work.

SETTING AND SITE 
 
The Cross-Lines Retirement Center is located in the north part of the Argentine neighborhood, in Kansas City, 
Wyandotte County, Kansas (Figure 1). A large industrial area and railroad switchyard are located immediately 
north of the boundary, and the Kansas River lines the north part of that area. Strong Avenue, located a block 
south, is a corridor with a mix of one - to two-story commercial buildings that date to the turn of the twentieth 
century, non-historic commercial infill, and paved parking lots. A residential neighborhood is to the north and 
contains modest single-family houses on narrow lots. An open park known as Clopper Field is to the northwest, 
and a branch of the Kansas City, Kansas Public Library and a church are immediately south of the property.

Summary  
 
Built in two phases between 1970 and 1976, the Cross -Lines Retirement Center (Cross -Lines) is locally 
significant under Criterion A in the areas of COMMUNITY PLANNING AND DEVELOPMENT and SOCIAL 
HISTORY. Built by the Cross-Lines Co-operative Council, a faith-based organization that provided community 
services such as housing, food, and education assistance, in the Silver City Urban Renewal Area  (KS R-12), the 
Cross-Lines Retirement Center provided seniors in the Argentine neighborhood in Kansas City, Wyandotte 
County, Kansas, with access to affordable housing. The building, which contains 226 units of low -income 
housing, provided a significant percentage of senior housing in Kansas City, Kansas, and continues its historic 
use today. Cross -Lines Retirement Center is an example of the “Residential” property subtype of the  “Urban 
Renewal Development” property type, as outlined in the Urban Renewal -era R esources in the United States  
Multiple Property Documentation Form (MPDF). According to the MPDF, a major goal of urban renewal  
sought to eliminate substandard housing and replace it with improved housing for low -income residents, such 
was the case at Cross -Lines.3 Planning for the Urban Renewal Area (URA) that the Cross -Lines Retirement 
Center is located on, along with the development itself, required considerable efforts  on the part of the local 
elected officials and community groups to identify and approve the necessary documentation to access urban 
renewal program funding. 
 
The period of significance begins in 1970 with construction of Phase 1 of the development and ends in 1976 
with the completion of Phase 2 of the development. As noted in the MPDF, by the time the program 
legislatively ended in 1974, over half of all approved Urban Renewal Areas (URAs) were still in progress. 
Given this, urban renewal projects that extend beyond 1974 are still eligible under that context if significant 
planning activities occurred prior to the end of the program. Since Phase 1 predates the legislative end of the 
program, Phase 2 is considered an addition that meets the stated goal of the program and was built within the 
boundaries of the URA.

URBAN RENEWAL IN THE UNITED STATES 
 
Urban renewal was the largest and broadest attempt to address the perceived ills of American cities. Progressive 
Era-programs that sought to improve housing and social conditions suffered from a lack of funding and overall 
scale of the program, as most were centralized and carried out exclusively by municipalities. 4 Examples of pre-
urban renewal programs included the City Beautiful Movement, and the Model Tenement Movement. Up to 
that point, most federal legislation involved private development of single family homes through mortgage

financing, but as World War II drew to an end, President Harry Truman signed into law the Housing Act of 
1949, which addressed housing conditions and slum prevention (Title I), mortgages (Title II), expanded public 
housing programs (Title III), housing research (Title IV), farm housing (Title V), and created a housing census, 
among other things (Title VI).5 
 
To prevent the perceived spread of slum conditions in major urban areas, Title I of the Housing Act of 1949, 
entitled “Slum Clearance and Community Development and Development,” established a s ystem for the federal 
government and local municipalities to share costs associated with activities related to “neighborhood 
conservation and rehabilitation of structures…[and] clearance and redevelopment of structures and 
neighborhoods.”6 With urban renewal, the latter activities of clearance and redevelopment became more 
common as proponents advocated more for wholesale clearance. 7 Recognizing the monumental task of clearing 
large swaths of land for redevelopment required significant resources, Title I established a cost-sharing structure 
where the federal government provided two- thirds of the total project costs and local municipalities provided 
the remaining third associated with acquiring the land, clearing it, and preparing it for redevelopment.8 Once the 
land was cleared and prepared, it was often sold at a loss to make it more attractive for redevelopment. 
 
The goal of the federal urban renewal program was  for municipalities to acquire and clear blighted areas and 
make them available for redevelopment .9 Proponents of urban renewal believed that new development and 
revitalization was the solution to stopping the decentralization of the nation’s city centers and the resulting loss 
of the tax base. A typical urban renewal project consisted of three distinct phases: planning, acquisition of 
blighted areas for clearance, and redevelopment . In the planning stage, the locally administered Urban Renewal 
Agency (or equivalent) identified blighted areas and produced a formal Urban Renewal Plan (URP) outlining 
the goals and objectives for the designated Urban Renewal Area (URA).10 In Kansas City, the Urban Renewal 
Agency of Kansas City, Kansas (established 1955) operated as the local public agency to implement the 
program.11 
 
The URP would then be reviewed  and approved by the United States Housing and Home Finance Agency 
(HHFA) or, after September 1965, the United States Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD).

An approved URP was a critical step in the legality of urban renewal activities as defined in the Housing Act of 
1949. The program required the URP to “clearly identify the area slated for urban renewal and the reason why 
the area was blighted or needed to be redeveloped.”12 
 
Once the federal agency approved the plan, the local Urban Renewal Agency would acquire the land in the 
URA, demolish the identified blighted buildings, and prepare the land for new development. Once the land was 
ready, the local agency offered it for sale (often at a loss) for development. S eparate projects by disparate 
developers often occurred simultaneously in the URA. Urban renewal -era resources, therefore, are those new 
constructions built on land that had been acquired, cleared, and sold by the local agency.  Demolition was a 
common component of urban renewal projects, and communities demolished many homes within URAs simply 
because they were in that area, and not due to any physical issues. For the area within the Silver City URA that 
corresponded with the Cross -Lines Retirement Center, the Kansas City, Kansas Urban Renewal Agency 
approved demolition of forty-four “structures” to make way for the new building.13 
 
In Kansas City, Kansas, the federal government approved fifteen  URAs between March 1956 and March 1970 
for a total federal outlay of $52,749,727. 14 For the Silver City Urban Renewal Area (discussed further below), 
the federal commitment was $7,096,623, or about 13 percent of the project total.

assistance, furniture donations, legal services, and housing assistance. In addition to the Cross -Lines Retirement 
Center, the council  also operated a rent -supplemented housing development about one mile south of the 
nominated property.23

CONCLUSION 
 
The Cross -Lines Retirement Center is locally significant under Criterion A  in the areas  of SOCIAL 
HISTORY and COMMUNITY PLANNING AND DEVELOPMENT  for its role in utilizing the urban 
renewal program to offset costs related to providing affordable senior housing in Kansas City, Kansas. In 
1970, the Cross -Lines Co-operative built Phase 1 of the Cross -Lines Retirement Center. Construction of the 
building aligned with the organization’s values and mission to provide services to the community. The Cross -
Lines Retirement Center provided a significant percentage of affordable housing units for the elderly 
population in Kansas City, Kansas , which met a stated goal of the urban renewal program . The building 
retains its historic senior housing use. In the late 1970s, the Cross-Lines Co-operative Council purchased the 
former Town House Hotel in downtown Kansas Ci ty, Kansas, and converted it into senior housing known as 
the Cross Lines Tower. Prior to the development of Cross Lines Tower, the council operated the Cross -Lines 
Retirement Center as its only retirement property.
