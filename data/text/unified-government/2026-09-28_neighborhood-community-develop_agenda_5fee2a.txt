• Budget Breakdown by Category:

Budget Scenario Personnel Operations Total 
Base Budget (2026 Approved + 3%) ~$1.1 million ~$400,000 ~$1.5 million 
Department Request ~$1.3 million — ~$1.7 million 
Proposed 2027 Budget ~$1.3 million — ~$1.7 million 
• Notable 2027 Changes: 
A. Two (2) additional FTEs were added:  one Administrative Support Specialist and

B. Contractual services and commodities budgets remained relatively unchanged due

The Chairman opened the public hearing.  No comments were received.  The Chairman closed 
the public hearing. 
 
Discussion: 
Commissioner Bynum asked for clarification on what constituted contractual services and 
commodities within this and other departments. 
Alyssa Marcy clarified that contractual services in the Planning Department include:  a 
floodplain contractor managing the floodplain program, consultant fees (the largest of which is 
related to the ongoing zoning code update), and software licensing fees.  Sam Her, Lead Analyst 
in the Budget Office, clarified that commodities represent a very small percentage of the budget 
(approximately 1%) and consist primarily of office supplies, training and travel expenses, and 
fuel.  Michael Peterson, Deputy Budget Director, further defined commodities as tangible items a 
department acquires, while contractual services represent fees paid for someone to perform a 
service. 
 
Commissioner Davis asked whether contracted planning services – being utilized due to current 
staff shortages – would continue even after new staff came on board. 
Alyssa Marcy responded that some contracted services, particularly for floodplain management, 
would likely continue long-term due to the size and complexity of that program.  She further 
Page | 4 
explained that as the sole long-range planner, contracted services for major planning efforts such 
as the zoning code update would also continue. 
 
Commissioner Davis inquired about the Vision Zero Plan and whether adequate resources 
existed to complete it. 
Alyssa Marcy responded that the Vision Zero Plan was adopted by the Commission in March 
(2026) and is essentially complete.  She noted that additional work beyond the original project 
scope is being conducted using remaining grant funds (the project was under budget), and that 
wrap-up is expected by September 15.  She further indicated that approval had been obtained to 
pursue implementation of five projects, and that the Build Kansas match grant had been received 
to fully cover the required match.  The department is still awaiting a response from Federal 
Highway regarding the balance of the grant funding. 
 
Commissioner Davis also inquired about the status of the zoning code rewrite. 
Alyssa Marcy explained that the current budget covers the code rewrite effort through 
approximately March of 2027, and that funding for the remainder of the rewrite has not yet been 
secured.  She noted that the zoning code rewrite is a multi-year, 300-plus page effort requiring 
substantial community engagement, and that remaining needs could potentially be addressed 
through the 2027 budget if directed. 
 
Commissioner Howard asked four substantive policy questions regarding the department’s 
personnel-heavy budget structure, requesting the following information in response. 
1. What measurable service capacity are taxpayers receiving from personnel costs? 
2. Is the current staffing structure the most efficient way to deliver services? 
3. Are there opportunities for technology, automation, shared services, or process 
improvements to increase productivity without simply adding personnel? 
4. If the two additional FTEs are approved, what performance improvement should the 
committee expect? 
Alyssa Marcy provided the following responses: 
1. Service Capacity:  The department currently has offers extended to five staff members, 
most of whom had been unfilled for more than one year, with one staff member having 
already started.  A new organizational structure is being implemented that will increase 
capacity significantly. 
A. Two additional planners are being hired to handle day-to-day/case planning, 
doubling existing capacity (from two to four planners) for the entire city. 
B. A planner will replace the historic preservationist, and a new planner will focus 
specifically on floodplain and environmental planning – functions previously 
managed solely by Ms. Marcy. 
Page | 5 
C. A planning technician began two weeks ago and is stationed at the Neighborhood 
Resource Center (NRC) to support the Development Review Committee (DRC) 
process and work directly with NRC staff daily. 
D. An intern is expected to begin shortly, which will assist with existing backlogs. 
2. Technology and Efficiencies:  The department is in active discussion with the County 
Administrator’s office regarding automating and digitizing the application process, which 
Ms. Marcy described as a critical need that would significantly increase capacity and 
reduce the risk of items falling through the cracks. 
3. Performance Improvement from Two New FTEs:  The enforcement specialist will 
directly increase the department’s capacity for enforcement, including reviewing DRC 
projects for compliance.  The administrative support specialist will relieve current staff of 
multi-role duties (phone coverage, email management, and casework simultaneously), 
allowing more focused attention to specific casework and increasing overall productivity. 
 
Commissioner Davis stated that the planning department charges fees for services and asked 
whether staff had an estimate of revenue generated in prior years. 
Reginald Lindsay, Budget Director, indicated he did not have the current year figure on hand but 
recalled that in prior years of high development activity, fee revenue had reached approximately 
$1.2 million. 
 
Commissioner Davis noted for the public’s benefit that departments with fee-for-service 
revenue can represent net-neutral or net-positive operations that are not primarily reliant on 
property or sales tax funding. 
 
Commissioner Davis confirmed with staff that the planning department’s proposed budget 
represents their full budget request, meaning nothing was left unfunded for this department. 
 
Commissioner Howard concluded the discussion by posing the following policy question to the 
full committee for reflection throughout the budget process:  “If the budget is a policy document, 
what policy choice are we making by funding this request at this level, and what policy choice 
are we making by not funding the remaining request?”

Page | 8 
Director of Parks and Recreation Angel Ferrara provide contextual remarks on the department’s 
key operational challenges and capital priorities. 
• Parkwood Pool:  A significant emergency repair occurred approximately ten days prior to 
the meeting.  During routine pool cleaning, a chunk of the pool basin crumbled.  The pool 
is over 50 years old, and predicting ongoing maintenance needs is increasingly difficult.  
Director Ferrara and the maintenance tech superintendent personally performed the repair 
on a Friday morning using hydraulic cement, swimming the repair materials to the four-
foot area of the pool where the hole occurred.  The repair was made within a 90-second 
application window and succeeded in keeping the pool operational through the end of the 
season (the pool’s last day was the Sunday preceding the meeting).  Had the repair failed, 
a season ending closure would have resulted.  This incident underscored the critical need 
for a determination regarding the level of capital investment appropriate for this aging 
facility. 
• Sunflower Hills Golf Course:  Director Ferrara noted that in 2024, 65% of participants at 
Sunflower Hills came from outside Wyandotte County, providing justification for 
tourism funding as a revenue source for that facility. 
• Earl Watson Community Center:  The facility has been closed for two years due to 
staffing challenges.  The Recreation Division has 15 full-time employees to staff six 
(coming to seven) community centers.  The department has established a minimum of 
two employees standard for each community center for safety reasons, but ongoing 
vacancies, retirements, time-off requirements, and accommodation obligations have made 
meeting that standard difficult.  If fully staffed with currently budgeted positions, Earl 
Watson could be reopened, though bringing Parkwood back online at 40 hours per week 
capacity would require creative scheduling.  Director Ferrara also noted that a contracted 
basketball program operated by Earl Watson Jr. has been running in the facility, and that 
conversations with Earl Watson Jr. about potential formal partnership agreements have 
begun. 
• Community Centers – Operating Hours:  Community centers currently operate only 40 
hours per week, largely due to staffing capacity constraints.  Director Ferrara noted that 
traditional community centers are generally open 15 to 16 hours per day. 
• Parkwood Community Center:  The department is working to bring the Parkwood 
Community Center back online, which will require additional staffing as well as interior 
facility investments to bring the space to an appropriate operating standard. 
• Recreation Programming:  The department has experienced programming challenges due 
to vacancies in program coordinator and recreation specialist positions.  An offer has 
recently been extended for a program coordinator position following a previous offer that 
fell through six weeks prior.  A sports coordinator position, which had been vacant for a 
significant period, has been filled, and the adult sports program is being rebuilt.  Youth 
programming has also been impacted by staffing vacancies. 
• Deferred Maintenance:  The department continues to carry a substantial deferred 
maintenance backlog.  Several large capital items requested for 2027 were not included in 
the final proposed budget, including a pedestrian bridge at Beatrice L. Lee Community 
Center, annual playground and trail programs, water line work at one of the county parks, 
Page | 9 
and new park amenities including a long-discussed park in the western portion of the 
county. 
• Vacancy Rate:  The Parks Division alone has a current vacancy rate of approximately 
28%, due to two recent retirements and other staffing losses.  Additionally, the 
department’s assistance with Monday blight cleanups (with approximately four staff 
members) has further reduced available labor for parks maintenance during the week.  
The combination of weekend work schedules (spring through October 15) and Monday 
blight cleanup assistance has created significant operational compression mid-week. 
• Mowing Challenges:  The mowing season has been extending earlier in the spring and 
later in the fall.  The $125,000 mowing budget increase is intended to address this 
extended season and provide approximately one additional mowing cycle.  An asset 
management system is being brought online by the end of the current month, which will 
provide real-time data on work orders by location, enabling better tracking of 
maintenance complaints and resource allocation in future budget discussions. 
• Fish Stocking Program:  The department has been in discussion with the Kansas 
Department of Wildlife regarding transitioning from a department-managed fish stocking 
program to the state-managed stocking program, similar to what Johnson County recently 
adopted.  A meeting with the state was scheduled for the following day.  The transition 
would result in the elimination of the local fishing permit fee and the associated 
approximately $90,000 in revenue, but the state program offers significant benefits 
including habitat management, species control, and programming resources not currently 
provided by the department.  Director Ferrara described the transition as likely to be 
budget-neutral. 
• Park Inventory – Underutilized Parcels:  Director Ferrara, prompted by questions from  
Commissioner Davis and public comments, addressed several underutilized or formerly 
partnership-operated parks. 
• Riverview Park (74th and Riverview):  This has always been a Unified 
Government park with a longstanding partnership with Turner Recreation.  This 
year, Parks and Recreation assumed maintenance (mowing only) after Turner 
Recreation’s programming numbers declined.  It is currently a locked baseball 
complex without playground or trail amenities.  Future use would require 
community engagement and Commission direction. 
• Coronado Park:  An underutilized park with Land and Water Conservation Fund 
(LWCF) grant funding restrictions that limit non-park uses of the land. 
• Silver City:  Another larger, underutilized park parcel. 
• Director Ferrara noted that across the 54-park inventory, some parks resemble 
medians or pocket parks, and that a formal evaluation of underutilized assets 
could be undertaken with Commission direction. 
Alan Howze, Interim County Administrator, noted that the department has been operating off a 
2017 master plan (described as more of a catalog of park needs rather than a true master plan) 
and that investing in new master planning is difficult without a dedicated capital funding source 
to plan against, as unfunded master plans can become obsolete and represent wasted expenditure.

When federal funds were available, the department was able to execute on the 2017 plan 
effectively.

Commissioner Pacheco asked for clarification on the approximately $10–$11 million gap

confirmed that departments were asked to define the fully funded level as what it would take to 
manage their current activities to an appropriate level — not to add new services — making the 
gap a statement of what is currently unmet to sustain baseline operations effectively. 
 
Commissioner Howard asked about the Earl Watson Community Center specifically, noting his 
personal connection to the facility and its history (formerly Bethany Community Center).  He 
asked whether there was an opportunity for a formal partnership with Earl Watson Jr. given the 
naming of the facility.  Director Ferrara confirmed that conversations with Earl Watson Jr. have 
occurred regarding programming and partnership models, and that broader discussions about 
alternative operational models — including space-sharing arrangements with organizations such 
as the YMCA, Boys and Girls Club, and Turner Recreation — are underway. 
 
Commissioner Howard also asked for specific data on additional mowing cycles and acreage 
that the $125,000 investment would address, and whether parks could identify specific locations 
with the highest maintenance complaints to demonstrate whether the additional funding would 
address those areas.

Director Greg Talkin, Director of Neighborhood Resource Center, provided supplementary 
remarks addressing several operational matters: 
1. Development Review Turnaround Times:  The NRC is experiencing significantly 
increased review and processing times for development projects.  Contributing factors 
include the growing complexity of projects, increased volume of projects, and 
insufficient plan review staff resources.  The department had requested a residential plan 
reviewer and a commercial plan reviewer, both of which were not approved in the 
proposed budget.  Director Talkin noted that the DRC process involves multiple 
departments — particularly the Fire Department, which has a single plan reviewer 
carrying multiple duties — and that systemic capacity concerns extend beyond NRC 
alone. 
2. Technology Enhancement — Accela Platform:  Director Talcott expressed appreciation 
for the $200,000 allocation designated for Accela (managed through the Knowledge and 
Innovation Office).  The managed services funding will allow some Accela work to be 
delegated to the vendor, enabling the Knowledge and Innovation Office to advance 
progress more quickly despite juggling numerous concurrent projects.  The goal is to 
make the DRC process fully digital and online, centralizing all communications and 
submissions into the Accela platform, which will provide data tracking from plan 
submission to return, enabling identification of where delays are occurring — with the 
department or with developers. 
3. DRC Technology Implementation Timeline:  Director Talkin indicated that the goal is to 
have the DRC process live in Accela by the third quarter of 2026 (or possibly second 
quarter), though he acknowledged uncertainty on the exact timeline. 
4. Staffing Partnerships:  Director Talkin noted that the Planning and Zoning Department's 
new enforcement specialist and planning staff will provide meaningful support to NRC 
operations, as planning cases are often more complex and require verification of property 
history and grandfathered conditions. 
5. Livable Neighborhoods Division — Staffing Gap:  Director Talkin identified the Livable 
Neighborhoods Division as the NRC's top operational concern.  The division remains a 
one-person operation despite supporting a network that once included 50-plus 
neighborhood and business revitalization groups, now numbering 35–37.  He 
acknowledged that this position was his primary omission from his earlier remarks and 
committed to exploring part-time, full-time, or consistent volunteer resources to provide 
support. 
6. Office Space Lease Expiration:  The NRC's current lease at 4049 Estate Avenue is in its 
last five-year option term, which will expire in May 2028.  Director Talkin indicated this 
would need to be addressed in the next year's budget process, as the organization will 
need to either renegotiate the lease or identify an alternative location.
