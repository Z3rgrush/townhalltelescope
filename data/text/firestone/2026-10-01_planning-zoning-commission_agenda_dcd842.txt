RECOMMENDATION  
Staff recommends the Planning & Zoning Commission approve Resolution PC 26-03: A Resolution of the Planning and 
Zoning Commission of the Town of Firestone, Colorado, Approving A Comprehensive Plan Update.  The next step will be 
to take the comprehensive plan to the Board of Trustees to ask them to ratify the Planning & Zoning Commission's 
decision. 
  
SUMMARY  
A comprehensive plan is an advisory document that establishes the community's vision and guides future Town decision-
making and public and private investments to bring that vision to life.  The Plan provides guidance on topics that shape 
Firestone's future, including: Land Use and Development, Housing, Economic Development, Community Character and 
Identity, Transportation, Park and Recreation, and Community Facilities.  
  
HISTORY AND PREVIOUS BOARD ACTION  
For the past 18 months, the Town of Firestone has been working to update the community's Comprehensive Plan.  This 
effort has included community events, stakeholder meetings, open houses, online surveys, and meetings with the Board 
of Trustees and the Planning and Zoning Commission.  The updated Comprehensive Plan will replace the Town's 2013 
Master Plan.   
 
The planning process and schedule included the following milestones, presentations, and public engagement:  
• Spring 2025 kickoff with current consultant; 
• June 18, 2025, public open house;  
• 4th at Firestone 2025 public engagement effort;  
• June 24, 2025, presentation at the Board of Trustees Retreat;  
• October 15th and 16th, 2025, meetings with the Board of Trustees and the Planning & Zoning Commission.

WHAT IS A COMPREHENSIVE PLAN?
A comprehensive plan is a policy document that 
serves as a guidebook for future decision-making, 
establishing a foundation for public actions and laws 
that benefit residents and the broader Firestone 
community. A comprehensive plan does not contain 
codes or ordinances. Instead, it establishes a broad 
vision for the community that will inform local and 
regional projects, programs, budgets, codes, and 
standards. The resulting document establishes the 
vision and goals of the community, provides policy 
recommendations, and identifies implementation 
strategies for Town staff, the Board of Trustees,  
the Planning and Zoning Commission, Town 
committees, and citizens.

INTRODUCTION & PURPOSE
WHO IS FIRESTONE?
A BRIEF HISTORY
Incorporated in 1908, the Town of Firestone was 
established as a coal mining community. The Town 
was named after Jacob Firestone, who at one time 
owned the land on which this community sits. Coal 
mining remained the main industry in Firestone 
up until the 1960s when the primary focus of the 
local economy shifted to natural gas drilling. The 
availability of natural gas sparked development on 
a larger scale and since that time, the Town has 
experienced significant residential and  
commercial growth.
2013 MASTER PLAN & CONDITIONS
The Town’s previous comprehensive plan, the 
Firestone Master Plan (2013), was adopted in 2013 
and addressed the community’s land use patterns 
and growth at that time.

WHERE WE ARE TODAY
The Town of Firestone has experienced steady growth 
in the last decade following the adoption of the 
2013 Master Plan, and therefore an updated plan is 
warranted: the Found in Firestone Comprehensive Plan 
2026 (‘Plan’). The community has nearly doubled in 
size since 2010, with an overall population of 10,147 in 
2010, growing to a population of 18,561 in 2023.1
STATUTORY REQUIREMENT
The Colorado State Statute, C.R.S. § 31-23-206(4)(a), 
requires municipalities with a population of 2,000 or 
more, within a county with a population of 10,000 
or more, to “prepare and adopt a master plan for 
the physical development of the municipality.” The 
statute also states these master plans should include:
 > A narrative description of the procedure used for the 
development and adoption of the plan.
 > The most recent version of the master plan for 
potential areas of growth within three miles of the 
municipality’s existing boundaries.
 > A water supply element, including water conservation 
policies as determined by the municipality.
 > A strategic growth element to discourage sprawl and 
promote the development/redevelopment of vacant or 
underutilized parcels in urban areas.

PLANNING PROCESS & SCHEDULE
This Comprehensive Plan was developed through 
close coordination and engagement with Town staff, 
community members, and local stakeholders, as well 
as extensive data gathering and analysis.
PHASE 1: DISCOVERY
The first phase included information gathering, 
analysis of existing conditions, and public/
stakeholder engagement in order to establish the 
primary focus of the Plan. This phase occurred in 
the Spring and Summer of 2025. The community 
engagement process and presentation of key  
themes is provided in Chapter 4.
PHASE 2: DEVELOPMENT OF 
COMPREHENSIVE PLAN DRAFT
Phase 2 included team organization of the 
information gathered in Phase 1 to guide the 
development of the Draft Comprehensive Plan. The 
Draft Plan incorporated the feedback from Phase 
1 in the Plan elements, and presented the vision, 
goals, policies, and actions needed to address the 
key issues identified. It was then shared with the 
community for review and input.
PHASE 3: FINAL PLAN
Following the community’s review of the Draft 
Comprehensive Plan, the project team evaluated the 
feedback received and incorporated appropriate 
revisions into an updated version of the Draft Plan. 
Refinement of the Draft Plan continued through 
work sessions with the Town staff and Board of 
Trustees, as well as input from the community. 
PURPOSE OF THIS PLAN
The purpose of the Found in Firestone 
Comprehensive Plan is to identify long-range 
planning and development goals and policies within 
the Firestone community. It is intended to guide 
Town leadership, Town staff, and other decision-
makers on implementing such goals and policies. 
HOW TO USE THIS PLAN
The Firestone Comprehensive Plan is a guiding 
document for the Town and a resource for local 
and regional stakeholders. As an example, Town 
staff refer to the Comprehensive Plan when 
reviewing development applications to ensure 
development aligns with the goals, strategies, and 
actions of the Plan.  The following groups will use 
this Comprehensive Plan on a regular basis to help 
inform decision-making:
 > Town staff
 > Board of Trustees
 > Planning & Zoning Commission
 > Developers
 > Partner agencies
 > The community at-large
Each chapter begins by introducing relevant goals 
and ends by outlining recommended strategies and 
actions to achieve these goals. The overall vision 
and guiding principles, along with the developed 
goals, strategies, and actions are designed to guide 
the decision-making of the current and future 
community for the next 20 years. 
VISION & GUIDING PRINCIPLES
This Plan establishes a vision and guiding principles 
that serve as the foundation for the other elements 
and components within the Plan. The vision and 
guiding principles acknowledge Firestone’s history 
while reflecting the present and future of this 
growing community, and are described further in 
Chapter 4.
GOALS
Defined by the community and stakeholders, each 
chapter will include a series of goals that provide 
overarching direction to achieve the community’s 
vision. Goals are the desired result of the planning 
process that will guide the Town’s future development.
STRATEGIES & ACTIONS
Each chapter concludes with a list of strategies and 
actions that may be implemented or bridge gaps 
and create an attainable path to implementation. 
The strategies outline the overall approach for 
achieving the goals described. Actions are specific 
steps that need to be taken, such as changes to 
regulatory policy and tools, capital improvement 
investments, and meaningful partnerships to develop 
or strengthen.

COMMUNITY CONTEXT
The following summarizes key aspects of the 
Firestone community and the major factors 
influencing its current and future conditions, 
which played a role in shaping the discussions and 
decisions throughout the Comprehensive  
Plan process.
POPULATION GROWTH
As noted, Firestone has experienced steady 
growth over the last decade. The Town anticipates 
the population will more than double in the next 
25 years, from an estimated population of 19,000 
in 2025 to a projected population range of 45,000 
to 55,000 by 2050.1 Population growth will 
depend on a variety of factors, including water 
availability, economic conditions, and development 
of property, renter and owner-occupied.
HOUSING DIVERSITY NEEDS
Existing housing in Firestone consists primarily 
of detached single-family homes, accounting for 
76% of the Town’s housing units.2 This indicates 
a need for additional types of for-sale housing 
to diversify the types of housing available. 
There is also a need for more multifamily, for-
rent units, as further described in the Housing 
chapter of the Plan. Young professionals and 
older populations are generally more likely to 
require smaller housing units in higher-density 
neighborhoods. Diversifying the availability of 
different housing types that are both for-sale and 
for-rent will provide more options for a wider 
range of demographics who may desire to reside 
in Firestone.
RETAIL & COMMERCIAL NEEDS
Firestone has experienced strong growth in the 
commercial sector in recent years compared to 
other communities in its trade area (as described 
in the Planning Influences section of this chapter). 
The community must continue to build its 
commercial tax base in order to provide amenities 
and services to a growing population. While the 
Town has generally been successful at attracting 
franchise retailers, there is a desire among 
the community to see a greater share of small 
businesses in the Town.
LOCAL EMPLOYMENT NEEDS
Firestone is considered to be a “bedroom 
community,” as many residents commute outside 
of the Town for employment. Discussions with the 
Town and stakeholder groups indicate there is a 
strong desire to retain a larger population during 
the daytime, with more opportunities for local 
employment in all sectors, including retail.

Carbon Valley region of the Front Range, which is 
comprised of the Towns of Firestone, Dacono, and 
Frederick. Also known as the “Tri-Town” area, these 
communities are known for their shared mining 
history and suburban-style development near the 
greater Denver Metro Region. Firestone’s proximity 
to the other towns of the Carbon Valley creates a 
unique opportunity for regional partnerships and 
an opportunity to work collaboratively on regional 
economic and development efforts.

URBAN GROWTH BOUNDARY
The Urban Growth Boundary (UGB) includes land 
within and beyond the Town limits, representing the 
Town’s projected geographical limit for growth. The 
boundaries of the UGB have been adjusted over 
the years based on intergovernmental agreements 
between Firestone and its neighbors: Frederick, 
Mead, Platteville, Thornton, Fort Lupton and  
Weld County.
The UGB provides opportunities for the Town to 
expand its limits, and it may choose to annex and 
incorporate property to provide services and political 
representation. Future annexations are required 
to be within the UGB and must align with the 
Comprehensive Plan.
THREE-MILE PLAN
On January 8, 2025, the Town of Firestone adopted 
its Three-Mile Plan. This state-mandated long-range 
planning document identifies potential annexation 
areas within a three-mile boundary to ensure 
that future growth is coordinated with service 
provisions and supports community goals for future 
development. This plan is governed by Colorado 
Revised Statute 31-12-105 and helps guide annexation

INTRODUCTION
Located north of the Denver Metro Region, the Town 
of Firestone is the northernmost community of the 
Carbon Valley, a tri-town region characterized by its 
low-lying plains and legacy of shared culture and 
industry. Along with its neighbors, Firestone has 
experienced growth over the past 10 years, nearly 
doubling its population. This rapid growth has greatly 
influenced the Town’s character and infrastructure. 
As a result, Firestone’s boundaries have expanded 
over the past decade to accommodate new 
residential neighborhoods and large retail centers, 
primarily around the intersection of I-25 and 
Firestone Boulevard.
Currently, Firestone accommodates a variety 
of land uses. The predominant land use in the 
Town is communities of detached residential 
development. Firestone is home to several residential 
neighborhoods such as Neighbors Point, Stoneridge, 
Mountain Shadows, Barefoot Lakes, and St. Vrain 
Ranch. These neighborhoods typically feature parks 
and open space amenities, and several are connected 
to the Firestone Trail, which runs north to south 
through the Town. The multifamily residential land 
uses within the Town are generally located adjacent 
to areas of commercial development. 
The Town is primarily auto-oriented, as major road 
corridors like Colorado Boulevard, Sable Avenue, 
and Firestone Boulevard offer residents connections 
between the Town’s land uses. Most commercial 
land uses are located along these corridors or are 
clustered at the intersection of active roadways.
As Firestone looks towards the future, how it 
allocates land and for what purpose will be of great 
significance. The Town strives to find a balance 
between embracing growth, change, and opportunity 
while simultaneously preserving and enhancing the 
character and sense of community that residents 
value. Thoughtful planning is required, as the 
outcomes of land use decisions can influence the 
quality of life for both current and future residents. 
By embracing new land use opportunities aligned 
with Town values, Firestone can ensure that it 
remains a vibrant and welcoming community for 
years to come.

EXISTING CONDITIONS HIGHLIGHTS
Firestone’s population is expected to double again 
over the next 25 years. Such significant growth will 
bring changes in land use; in response, the Town 
should look to diversify and balance its land use mix 
appropriately across the Town. 
Firestone is primarily a single-family residential 
community. Residential is the most prevalent

neighborhoods of detached residential homes. These 
neighborhoods are home to a significant portion 
of Firestone’s population and, as such, help define

neighborhoods. The prevalence of single-family

The land use patterns of the Town are fragmented. 
Places of community activity are separated by great

lack of cohesiveness and discouraging connections

feedback received during the community outreach 
where residents expressed a desire for a Town center, 
neighborhood gathering spaces, and more cohesive 
community design across the Town.
The Town has several unique land use opportunities 
to build upon. Existing assets such as Central Park,

addition to providing opportunities for recreation,

amenities contribute to Firestone’s sense of place.
Firestone has the means and capability for 
expanding trail connections. The Firestone Trail 
runs north-to-south through the Town, providing an

CENTRAL PARK 
The Town of Firestone purchased the Central Park 
property in 2005 with the vision of creating a future 
municipal complex while maintaining flexibility to 
partner on portions not needed for government use. 
Located in the heart of town, the approximately 
250-acre site represents a rare opportunity to create 
a transformational destination that enhances the 
quality of life for residents. Thoughtful development 
of Central Park can support economic vitality, attract 
visitors, and generate long-term tax revenue while 
creating a lasting community asset for generations  
to come.
A major Town planning effort, the Central Park 
Master Plan began in 2021. This planning effort 
identified a street network for the site to ensure 
circulation and connectivity to the various potential 
uses. Future development of the site should work 
with the natural contours of the land and highlight 
mountain views to the west. While the development 
of Central Park is envisioned to accommodate 
flexibility in design and programming, initial planning 
identified six planning areas: Civic, Sports Field 
Complex, Active Outdoor Recreation, Naturalized 
Area, Mixed-Use Activation, and Gateway Area.
Not only will the full realization of Central Park 
provide Firestone with a vibrant community events 
space and regional destination, the park’s location 
along the Firestone Trail and adjacency to future 
development opportunities (see Focus Areas 2 and 
3 in this chapter) position it as an ideal opportunity 
to serve as a catalyst for future development and 
enhance a connected network of parks, trails, and 
open space throughout the Town.
PLANNING AREAS
CIVIC
Central Park currently serves as Firestone’s civic 
hub, home to Town Hall, the Police Department, 
the Municipal Court Building, and the Carbon 
Valley Regional Library of the High Plains Library 
District. Future civic elements may include a 
central plaza, amphitheater, and other outdoor 
gathering spaces designed to host community 
events, along with potential educational facilities 
and complementary mixed-use programming that 
supports civic activity.
SPORTS FIELD COMPLEX
The site has the potential to accommodate a 
multi-field sports complex, including soccer, 
lacrosse, and baseball fields, as well as tennis, 
pickleball, and basketball courts. Indoor recreation 
facilities and supporting buildings could also be 
incorporated, creating opportunities to host local, 
regional, and potentially national sporting events 
and tournaments.
ACTIVE OUTDOOR RECREATION
A large portion of Central Park may be 
dedicated to active outdoor recreation, including 
playgrounds, disc golf, gathering areas, a leash-
free dog park, and other activity-based amenities. 
These elements would be connected by an 
integrated trail system to promote accessibility 
and encourage movement throughout the park.
NATURALIZED AREA
Naturalized open spaces would be incorporated 
throughout the site to provide areas for passive 
recreation, environmental enhancement, and 
scenic character. These areas would be connected 
through trails and green corridors, helping to 
create a balanced park environment.
MIXED-USE ACTIVATION
Supporting mixed-use development, such as

help activate the park, attract visitors, and create 
a vibrant destination that complements the 
recreational and civic elements of Central Park.
GATEWAY AREA
The Gateway Area will serve as a transition from 
Colorado Boulevard across the Firestone Trail into 
the main park areas. This zone may include a mix 
of passive open space and trails along with more 
active uses such as food trucks, small commercial 
services, or recreational attractions like water-
based amenities.

FIRESTONE TRAIL
In survey results from the 2020 Parks, Open Space

This major town amenity provides the community

connections to the Firestone Trail to help grow the 
Town’s trail network and offer more recreation and 
transportation choices for residents.
In addition to providing connectivity to local 
destinations, the Firestone Trail is also an important 
regional trail system and part of the larger Colorado 
Front Range Trail system. The trail expands beyond 
the Town’s boundary, providing connections to 
Dacono, Frederick, and unincorporated Weld

coordination with neighboring jurisdictions will be 
vital to ensuring the trail’s continued success as a 
recreation amenity and transportation system.
HISTORIC FIRESTONE
Along the southwestern boundary of the Town is 
Historic Firestone, commonly known as Old Town, 
a neighborhood at the location of the original Town 
settlement. As the historic heart of the community, 
preservation and reinvestment in this neighborhood 
are essential to the Town’s legacy and sense of 
community. The Town adopted the Historic Firestone 
Neighborhood Plan in 2022 which identifies 
opportunities for investment and improvements to 
ensure that as the Town grows, this neighborhood 
will retain its historic character.
ST. VRAIN STATE PARK & THE ST. VRAIN CREEK CORRIDOR
Firestone is home to St. Vrain State Park, a regional 
destination for outdoor recreation enthusiasts 
centered around the St. Vrain Creek and the only 
Colorado State Park to be within a town’s limits. 
The state park offers Firestone opportunities to 
enhance local connectivity to this natural resource 
and to align land use plans to support this regional 
tourist destination and protect the character of this 
important asset. 
Beyond the boundaries of the park, the St. Vrain 
Creek runs through the northern portion of Firestone. 
Along its banks are the sites of former gravel pits, 
remnants of the historic mining industry in Firestone. 
The Town is pursuing acquisition of some of these 
pits to serve as water storage reservoirs and 
envisions the future of this area as an opportunity 
to expand open space and trails along the creek 
corridor. The corridor’s adjacency to St. Vrain State 
Park and future development areas (see Focus Areas 
2 & 3 in this chapter) makes it a prime location for 
advancing connectivity and expanding the Town’s 
recreational amenities.
Looking ahead, the Town should emphasize the 
integration of these assets into the broader fabric 
of the Town by recognizing their opportunity to 
serve as catalysts for future development and by 
expanding connectivity to these areas. Allowing 
these assets to achieve their full potential and more 
fully integrating them into the Town will help sustain 
and enhance Firestone’s sense of place, ensuring 
that the Town continues to thrive while honoring its 
heritage and natural resources.

ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT
INTRODUCTION
Firestone has grown significantly since 2010, 
attracting the interest of some of the nation’s most 
recognizable commercial retail brands and many 
of the state’s largest home builders. The Town is 
an essential community within the Carbon Valley 
economy and can build on its positive reputation 
for being “business-friendly” and having “supportive 
policies and regulations”.
The Economic Development chapter provides a road 
map for improving the community’s economic well-
being and quality of life through job creation and 
infrastructure improvements. Key objectives, based 
on input from Town leaders and the community, 
include sustaining existing businesses; diversifying 
future job offerings; protecting existing assets 
(both natural and manmade); ensuring a sustainable 
environment; and reinforcing the Town’s fiscal 
balance sheet.
The goals, strategies, and actions recommended 
in this chapter align with these objectives, and 
are intended to help guide Town policy decisions, 
programmatic offerings, and priority expenditures to 
promote the area’s strengths and mitigate potential 
barriers in pursuit of opportunities.
EXISTING CONDITIONS HIGHLIGHTS
Firestone benefits from its location within the greater 
region. As part of the Carbon Valley, Firestone is 
part of a local market that supports shared growth, 
workforce mobility, and a growing customer 
base drawn from neighboring communities. More 
broadly, the Town’s location within Colorado’s Front 
Range places it within one of the state’s fastest 
growing economic corridors, enhancing its visibility, 
competitiveness, and long-term appeal to businesses 
seeking access to a diverse regional economy.
More residents commute out of the Town for work 
than commute into the Town. While Firestone’s out-
commuting patterns are similar to other communities 
along I-25, the high-rate of out-commuting points 
to the lack of primary employment opportunities 
within the Town and results in reduced local 
daytime spending at restaurants, shops, and 
service businesses which makes it difficult for local 
businesses to thrive.
Regional collaboration with Frederick and Dacono 
is essential to Firestone’s long-term economic 
future. The Tri-Town area functions as a shared 
economic region, not three isolated towns. Residents 
live, work, shop, and travel across town boundaries, 
and businesses make location decisions based 
on access to a regional workforce, transportation 
network, infrastructure, and customer base. By 
working together, the Carbon Valley communities 
can more effectively attract and retain employers, 
coordinate infrastructure investments, reduce 
competition for the same opportunities, and 
strengthen the overall economic vitality of the 
region. This collaborative approach positions 
Firestone to capture greater local employment, 
support a more resilient tax base, and remain 
competitive as the region continues to grow.
Firestone fosters a healthy business environment. 
The Town benefits from a business environment 
supported by local policies and regulations that 
encourage investment rather than create barriers. 
This has allowed Firestone to attract and retain a 
diverse mix of businesses over time. The Town’s 
established business base fosters stability and long-
standing relationships with loyal customers.

The Town has financial mechanisms in place to 
support revitalization, however, they are not 
fully maximized. The Firestone Urban Renewal 
Authority and Downtown Development Authority 
were established to oversee investment in certain 
areas of Firestone. Older urban renewal authorities 
may require amendments to maximize their ability

families that support local businesses. Over time 
this can reduce the customer base, make it harder 
for businesses to hire and expand, and ultimately 
limit growth in the Town’s sales and property tax 
base needed to fund services and infrastructure.
Firestone has few business-ready development 
sites. A lack of properties with appropriate zoning, 
infrastructure, and access already in place, puts 
the Town at a disadvantage for attracting  
new businesses.
Improving connectivity and focusing on more 
integrated land use patterns will strengthen 
Firestone’s economic potential. Disconnected 
land uses, a lack of mixed-use development, 
limited mobility options, and a fragmented system 
of public spaces restrict the growth of vibrant 
commercial areas and result in commercial 
development where businesses have fewer 
customers nearby, higher infrastructure and 
service costs, and less visibility.
ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT OPPORTUNITIES
Both near and long-term opportunities exist for 
Firestone to capitalize on untapped economic 
development potential. 
Realizing these opportunities will require 
participation by regional partners, support from 
local officials, and a carefully composed economic 
development program designed to capitalize on 
the community’s strengths, overcome challenges, 
and prioritize completing all aspects of the area’s 
business infrastructure.
GROWING DEMAND FOR RETAIL, OFFICE,  
& INDUSTRIAL SPACES
Firestone has strong potential to expand its 
commercial base as it grows. Market analysis 
indicates that there is capacity to support additional 
retail operators, including neighborhood-serving 
businesses as well as larger format retail that could 
draw customers from across the region. Future 
spending by new households is estimated to reach 
$672 million by 2035, an amount that could support 
approximately 1.2 million square feet of commercial 
retail space.1
At the same time, current spending patterns 
reveal a substantial opportunity to strengthen 
Firestone’s local economy. An analysis of retail sales 
and purchases showed residents are spending a 
significant share of their dollars outside of Firestone, 
particularly on electronics and appliances, groceries, 
health and personal care, clothing and accessories, 
miscellaneous retail goods, and food and beverage 
services. The value of this unmet demand is 
estimated to be approximately $242 million which, 
if captured within the Town, could support an 
additional 450,000 square feet of retail space.1 Town 
initiatives to attract new retail development should 
target a broad range of retail sectors but place 
particular emphasis on those listed where spending 
leakage is greatest.
In addition to retail growth, Firestone is also 
positioned to benefit from regional employment 
growth and rising demand for office and industrial 
space. Employment projections across major industry 
categories suggest that, assuming an annual growth

Trade Area (as defined in the Community Profile

generating demand for more than 1.8 million square 
feet of office and industrial space.1
Firestone is well-positioned to capture a significant 
share of this regional demand. Even assuming a 
modest capture rate of 8% to 10 % could result in the 
development of 140,000 to 180,000 square feet of 
new office and industrial space within the Town.1
These opportunities highlight the importance of 
proactive planning and targeted investment to 
ensure Firestone can convert market potential into 
lasting economic benefits.

MIXED-USE DEVELOPMENT
Firestone can benefit from integrating more mixed-
use development in certain strategic locations 
within the Town. Mixed-use development can be 
a powerful catalyst for economic development by 
bringing together complementary land uses in a 
more walkable setting. By concentrating housing, 
employment, retail, and civic spaces near one 
another, mixed-use areas create consistent foot 
traffic and a built-in customer base that supports 
local businesses and reduces reliance on a single 
market or specific time of day for business. 
Successful mixed-use areas benefit from ease of 
access and high visibility, and as such are ideally 
located along major corridors or at key  
community nodes.
A defining feature of successful mixed-use 
development is the intentional emphasis on 
pedestrian-oriented design. Sidewalks, public 
spaces, street level storefronts, and building 
frontages that engage the public realm encourage 
walking, lingering, and spontaneous interaction. By 
deemphasizing car travel, such as placing parking 
behind or below buildings and prioritizing street level 
activity, mixed-use areas create environments where 
businesses benefit from increased visibility and 
casual pass-by traffic, which is especially important 
for the success of retail, dining, and service uses.
Mixed-use developments also provide flexible spaces 
for events and public gatherings, such as plazas, 
green spaces, or shared streets. These spaces can 
host farmers markets, festivals, and community 
events that draw people from across the region, 
strengthen local identity, and generate additional 
economic activity for nearby businesses. Over time, 
these gathering spaces help establish mixed-use 
areas as recognizable community destinations rather 
than isolated developments.

INTRODUCTION
Essential to supporting local businesses and 
population growth, housing will play a defining 
role in shaping Firestone’s community character, 
economic vitality, and quality of life over the next 20 
years. As it grows, Firestone will need to pay close 
attention to the type of housing being built. Housing 
affordability and maintaining an adequate supply of 
diverse housing types for a wide range of households 
are central issues facing the Town and communities 
throughout the region and the State of Colorado. 
Within Firestone, there are many established 
residential neighborhoods that contribute to the 
community character residents value and seek to 
preserve, and there are also a growing number of 
households new to the community. Designing new 
housing in a manner that preserves and protects the 
desired Town character, while expanding housing 
options and choice, will be important to the Town’s 
future success.
Providing a diverse, attainable, and well-planned 
housing supply is essential to support economic 
resilience and ensure that Firestone remains a 
welcoming community where residents can put 
down roots, raise families, and call Firestone home 
throughout their lives.
EXISTING CONDITIONS HIGHLIGHTS
There is an undersupply of housing in Firestone 
as housing production has not kept pace with 
population growth. Vacancy rates have hit historic 
lows, indicating a constrained housing market 
for both ownership and rental housing. There is a 
low percentage of vacant households in Firestone 
(approximately 3-4%).1 This is an indication that the 
housing market is underserved by both available 
existing homes and new construction.
Increased housing diversity is needed to meet 
the needs of residents of all ages, incomes, and 
abilities, with an emphasis on creating more 
equitable housing access and affordability. 
In Firestone, the existing housing stock is 
predominately single-family homes, although housing 
and market trends show growing demand for a 
wider range of housing products, particularly for 
young adults, seniors, and lower-income households. 
There is a need to fill existing gaps in the housing 
market, to provide a wider variety of higher-density, 
lower-maintenance housing products, and increase 
the supply of affordable and attainable housing for 
households earning between 60 – 120% of the area 
median income.

to find opportunities to diversify its housing stock, 
focusing on building housing to meet the needs of 
different household types and people at varying 
income levels and stages of life.
Specifically, there is a need for increased housing 
options for renters, entry-level homebuyers, and 
retired households looking to downsize. Within 
Firestone, the percentage of renter households is 
approximately 19.8% and is expected to grow slightly, 
to approximately 20.5%, over the next five years.1
By providing a range of housing options, at varying 
price points, Firestone can ensure it provides:
 > Housing which supports a local workforce to reduce 
commuting burdens
 > Opportunities for young professionals looking to enter 
the housing market
 > Housing for seniors who want to age in place
There is a strong market for a continuum of housing 
choices in Firestone. The number of smaller 
households in Firestone and the larger Trade Area, 
suggest a growing need for higher-density housing, 
both ownership and rental.1 While Firestone has 
made a concerted effort to diversify its housing stock 
over the past several years, there are still gaps that 
could be filled by accommodating housing products  
such as:
 > Small-lot Single-Family Detached Units (Cottages)
 > Single-Family Attached Units (Duplexes, Townhouses 
and Condominiums)
 > Workforce housing targeted to “middle income” 
renters (80% to 120% of Area Median Income)
 > Expanded supply of market-rate rental apartments
 > Senior housing – wide spectrum of products from 
independent living to assisted living
THE OPPORTUNITY OF THE “MISSING MIDDLE”
Many of these housing products can address the 
need for “missing middle” housing. “Missing middle” 
is generally defined as a range of multi-unit, house-
scale buildings that fit between single-family homes 
and larger apartment buildings (including duplexes, 
triplexes, townhomes, and cottage courts). They 
are labeled “missing” because they have become 
difficult to build in many places due to zoning 
regulations that favor only single-family homes or 
large apartment complexes. They are designed to be 
built in walkable neighborhoods and provide diverse 
housing options that are often more affordable than 
detached single-family homes.

Smaller lots and “missing middle” housing help to

reduce costs associated with development (e.g., less 
landscaping, more efficient provision of infrastructure

PROACTIVEL Y ADDRESS HOUSING AFFORDABILITY ISSUES
Trends indicate a widening gap between area 
incomes and housing prices, pointing to growing 
concerns about the affordability of housing in 
Firestone. A common rule for assessing housing 
affordability is that households can typically afford 
homes priced between 3 and 4 times their household 
income. However, Table 1 indicates that the median 
home value is approximately 4.5 to 5 times the 
median household income, in both Firestone and 
the surrounding Trade Area. This indicates an 
affordability concern which is expected to continue 
based on future income and home value projections. 
NOTABLE HOUSING TRENDS & OPPORTUNITIES IN FIRESTONE:
1. Multi-generational housing that allows residents 
to age in place (e.g., accessory dwelling units).  
Accessory dwelling units (ADUs) are a creative 
way to provide more housing variety in existing 
single-family neighborhoods, while preserving 
the community character.  ADUs provide 
opportunities for small-scale rental options, 
as well as multi-generational housing, where 
multiple generations of one family can live 
together on the same property. The Town should 
consider expanding the allowance of ADUs and 
work to ensure that there is enough parking in 
place for this use.
2. Housing densities of approximately 10 dwelling 
units per acre can provide smaller, more 
attainable housing options.
3. Amenity-driven housing developments that 
provide amenities such as trail connections, 
parks, play areas, gathering places, swimming 
pools, fitness centers, food truck plazas, etc.  
Active adult communities such as Barefoot Lakes 
are popular and there is a desire for more of 
these types of developments.
4. Lock and leave, maintenance free or low-
maintenance housing (e.g., maintenance done by 
a metro district).

Currently, the Town has a balanced housing stock 
which means that it is well positioned to deal with 
affordability concerns. However, the Town should 
be proactive in ensuring that its housing stock 
remains affordable. Expanding more diverse housing 
options will be imperative, as well encouraging the 
development of attainable housing, and fostering 
regional collaboration on housing goals. 
The Town’s development fee structure is a key 
component of development costs that the 
Town could assist with to help reduce the cost 
of developing attainable housing. There is an 
opportunity to evaluate Town development 
regulations, permitting fees, and procedures to 
remove barriers to housing production, such 
as excessive open space and off-street parking 
requirements, development costs, time delays, 
uncertainty, and risk. 
The issue of housing affordability is not limited 
to Firestone; it remains an ongoing concern for 
the broader Trade Area as well. As the Tri-Towns 
of the Carbon Valley share employment bases, 
infrastructure, schools, and services; they function 
as one single, interconnected housing market. As 
such, a coordinated approach between the Tri-Towns 
will be critical to keeping housing affordable in 
Firestone. When housing supply, housing types, or 
growth patterns are not aligned, housing demand 
shifts across town boundaries, driving up prices and 
rents region-wide rather than solving affordability in 
any one community. By coordinating housing goals, 
the Tri-Towns can reduce competition between 
jurisdictions, manage growth more effectively, and 
help ensure that housing costs remain attainable for 
current and future Firestone residents. 
SUSTAIN EXISTING NEIGHBORHOODS
While it is vital for the Town to expand new housing 
options, such efforts should not come at the expense 
of preservation of existing residential neighborhoods. 
The Town should look to protect Firestone’s 
established residential neighborhoods, through 
strategies such as maintaining and expanding the 
Town’s existing Home Modification Repair Program 
and ensuring compatibility and buffering of new 
developments adjacent to existing neighborhoods. 
As previously mentioned, “missing middle” housing 
offers an opportunity for expanding smaller, 
more affordable housing choices into existing 
neighborhoods in a manner that preserves the 
existing character. Key approaches for supporting 
existing residential neighborhoods include increasing 
the availability of home rehabilitation programs for 
existing homes, providing separation and buffering 
between low- and high-density housing, and ensuring 
the upkeep of existing infrastructure to maintain 
community character.

INTRODUCTION
Great places are more than locations on a map 
– they are spaces where people connect, build 
their lives, discover community, do business, and 
enjoy healthy, well-connected living. A thriving, 
sustainable community doesn’t separate its variety of 
neighborhoods into isolated boxes; instead, it blends 
them into a thoughtful mix of livable spaces where 
the right combination of activities creates something 
truly unique. This chapter serves as a guide for how 
land in Firestone may be thoughtfully developed 
and maintained, grounded in the understanding 
that community design strongly influences the 
community’s character, form, and overall experience.
EXISTING CONDITIONS HIGHLIGHTS
The Firestone community has a strong sense of 
pride in its hometown character, as well as its 
safe and welcoming neighborhoods. Community 
members love the mountain views and small, 
hometown feel of Firestone, noting that the Town 
is an ideal place to raise a family. Residents feel 
that Firestone is a town of safe and welcoming 
neighborhoods and value the abundance of 
recreational opportunities that the Town has to 
offer, such as parks, trails, and open space. All these 
elements help define Firestone’s town character. 
As the Town grows it should seek to preserve 
these special things while also celebrating them as 
distinctive elements of the Town’s identity. 
People value the shared public spaces of Firestone 
and identify these as areas which contribute to the 
Town’s character. Firestone has several important 
public spaces which enhance the Town’s character, 
such as St. Vrain State Park, the Firestone Trail, and 
Central Park. These public places are significant 
assets valued by residents. The Town’s open space 
and trails and the Carbon Valley Regional Library 
within Central Park were identified as resident’s 
“favorite places in Firestone” per community surveys. 
Residents want to see public assets such as parks, 
open space, and trails prioritized and envision better 
connectivity between these places. The plans for 
Central Park also present a substantial opportunity 
to create a regional destination that will enhance 
options for recreation as well as provide spaces for 
community events.
There is a need to strengthen and celebrate 
community identity through design and public 
improvements. This can be achieved by focusing 
on physical improvements to the public realm that 
reflect the inherent character of the community such 
as right-of-way improvements at town gateways 
and along corridors, as well as creating vibrant 
community spaces, such as parks, streetscapes, and 
plazas which can support community events.
COMMUNITY CHARACTER 
OPPORTUNITIES
Firestone is poised for future growth. With its many 
community assets and thoughtful approach to 
planning and development, the Town has a valuable 
opportunity to develop a comprehensive vision, 
along with clear policy and design guidelines to 
enhance its built and natural environment. This will 
help to create a stronger sense of place that has 
been desired by residents and community leaders.

BUILDING COMMUNITY CHARACTER THROUGH PLACEMAKING
Placemaking is a process of creating attractive, 
functional, and memorable spaces that bring people 
together and strengthen a community’s sense 
of identity. Firestone has a unique opportunity 
to build upon its existing assets to enhance the 
community’s sense of place and create environments 
that are inviting, warm, and vibrant. Enhancing 
the community’s systems of trails and open space, 
improving neighborhood access to amenities, event 
spaces and recreational uses are just a few ways the 
community can elevate its character and identity. 
Efforts should be made to create a cohesive and 
consistent design to establish a Town brand. 
Residents frequently cite access to parks and trails as 
a defining feature of the Town. The Firestone Trail is 
a unique regional asset and facilitating expansion of 
the trail system will be crucial as the Town grows. Of 
note is expanding trail connections to several of the 
unique destinations within the Town. As discussed in 
the Land Use chapter, the Town has several existing 
assets including Central Park, Historic Firestone, the 
St. Vrain State Park and Creek Corridor; as well as 
the Focus Areas identified in this plan. These places 
represent opportunities for strengthened community 
connections and placemaking improvements to 
reinforce the Town’s character.
A commitment to placemaking will not only improve 
the quality of life for Firestone residents, but it will 
also support economic development efforts that are 
often aligned with community features that enhance 
a unique sense of place.
GATEWAY, CORRIDOR, & WAYFINDING DESIGN ELEMENTS
Firestone covers a wide geographic area and as an 
auto-oriented community, the daily experience of 
residents and visitors involves traveling along the 
major road corridors throughout the Town. The 
Town’s gateways and corridors play a role in how the 
town is perceived by those who move about it. These 
areas of the public realm represent highly visible 
opportunities to reinforce the Town’s sense of place 
and overall character through thoughtful design, 
landscaping, and multimodal improvements that 
make Firestone more attractive, welcoming,  
and pedestrian friendly. The Town’s legacy as a 
historic coal mining community offers a design 
inspiration for a theme of gateway, corridor, and 
wayfinding improvements.
Design opportunities include forms and materials 
evocative of traditional shaft mining equipment and 
patterns reminiscent of the natural geology of coal 
seams and mineral veins.
