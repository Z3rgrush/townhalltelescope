Parking License Plate Recognition Policy.
Director Greear and Manager Klein presented a draft policy for the use of Parking
License Plate Recognition (LPR) technology by the Town and Town contractors. Since
2019 the Town followed a guiding principles document for LPR use. The draft policy
would address the issues of privacy and data retention by establishing the types of data
collected, the retention period for storing data, and the permissions to access the data.
Town LPRs collect a context photograph, a photograph of the license plate, the GPS
coordinates of the vehicle, the date and time of the potential violation, and the license
plate text. Data would be retained for one (1) day unless connected to a citation, in
which case it would be retained for a year following the final disposition. The aggregate
occupancy data not connected to individual license plate information would be retained
permanently. Permitted uses of LPR data would include to verify paid parking; verify
virtual permits; digital chalking; identify scofflaws; support parking citations with
photographs of plates, environmental context, and GPS coordinates; and use
anonymized occupancy data to help plan the parking program. Prohibited uses included
monitoring First Amendment activity, enforcing federal immigration law, selling or
sharing the LPR data, tracking Town employee attendance or activities, general
surveillance, or supporting investigations unrelated to parking. Limited exceptions would
be made to support investigations in crisis situations. In the presence of exigent
circumstances and the existence of probable cause to believe a crime was committed,
as certified by the Chief of Police, the Parking and Transit Manager would provide the
requested information to the Chief of Police. Access to LPR data would be limited to
trained Parking and Transit staff and vendors. The data would be protected by unique
passwords and encrypted storage and transmission. Access would be tiered to ensure
employees are only able to access the data necessary for their position. Board
comments and questions have been summarized: asked about the capabilities of the
LPR system, Klein confirmed it was possible to set an alert for a license plate and to
keep data longer than dictated in the draft policy; questioned where LPR was used,
Klein confirmed it was used to monitor all Town-owned, paid parking areas; requested
clarification of the parking violation citation process, Klein explained LPR flagged
potential violations and then the violation was verified in-person by a Car Park
employee before issuing a citation; asked how many citations were issued annually,
Klein estimated between 1,500 and 1,800 total parking citations were issued annually

(3) scofflaw or repeat offenders were identified annually; Klein confirmed The Car Park
was not paid based on the number of citations issued; inquired who were the parking

Kramer explained Genetec's system operated in such a way that Genetec did not have
access to the data and Mistall would hold the plate numbers for a day; discussed
considering an ordinance to limit private use of LPR systems within Town limits, Klein

Winter Event Strategy Proposal.
Director Hinkle provided an overview of annual events. The Events Division facilitated
nearly 200 events, both public and private, on Town owned or managed properties
annually. 90% of events occurred between May and October. There were nine (9) full-
time, year-round positions: three (3) event coordinators, one (1) administrative
assistant, and five (5) maintenance staff. There were 13 large, annually recurring winter
events. Maintaining current procedures and costs to develop new events would allow
the team to conduct necessary maintenance in the winter to prepare for the summer.
Additional staff would be required to increase the number of winter events while
maintaining Town property and continuing to adequately staff the summer programs.
Board comments and questions have been summarized: questioned the benefit of
events to the local economy compared to the cost of hosting events, Town
Administrator Machalek stated approximately $1 million annually has been recovered by
the Events Division in 2024 and 2025 of an approximate $2.5 to $3 million spent
annually; recognized the challenge of sufficiently staffing a business to accommodate
heavy weekend visitation; discussed the benefit of hosting fewer events in the winter to
allow time for facility maintenance and event planning; questioned the level of interest
for new winter events, Hinkle stated there continues to be interest; requested
community feedback on the desirability of additional winter events; discussed
opportunities to enhance current winter events including providing power for snow
machines; referred to visitor surveys from Visit Estes Park indicating events were not a
primary reason people chose to visit Estes Park; and reminded the Board of the 2027
Provisional Strategic Plan objective to bolster the winter economy.
Organizational Climate Action Plan.
Abbey Stapleton, a Larimer County Sustainability Coordinator, introduced the Larimer
County Climate Smart Future Ready initiative. The Town has been a member of the
initiative's Executive Board since the program began in 2022. Climate Smart Future
Ready facilitated the creation of internal and community-level climate action plans
(CAPs) for municipalities throughout Larimer County. ICLEI - Local Governments for
Sustainability USA produced a CAP for Town operations for Estes Park, funded through
the Colorado Energy Office's Energy Efficiency Conservation Block Grant received by
Larimer County. Melany Arriola, a Program Officer with ICLEI, presented an overview of
the CAP. The plan focuses strictly on the assets and activities under the Town's direct
budgetary and administrative control. Steps to create the plan included conducting a
greenhouse gas (GHG) inventory for 2024 to establish a GHG emissions baseline,
interviewing key Town staff, and modeling emissions reduction scenarios to identify top
emissions reduction actions. The 2024 GHG inventory found Town operations resulted
in the production of 2,841 metric tons of carbon dioxide equivalent (MT C02e)
emissions in the categories of Buildings and Facilities, Employee Commute, Vehicle
Fleet, Transit Fleet, and Process and Fugitive Emissions. Buildings and Facilities
accounted for 80% of emissions. Climate Smart Future Ready estimated Estes Park's
community-wide emissions totaled 117,056 MT C02e in 2022. Two emissions reduction
scenarios were modeled, a moderate scenario resulting in an 84% reduction of
emissions by 2050 and an ambitious scenario resulting in a 90% reduction of emissions
by 2050. In both scenarios, the reductions in emissions from the electricity grid
accounted for over 70% of emissions reductions. Roadmaps were created to reduce
emissions in the categories of Buildings and Facilities, Vehicle Fleet, Transit Fleet, and
Employee Commute. The roadmaps included recommendations for Town leads and
partners, priorities, costs, metrics to measure progress, challenges, case studies, and
funding opportunities. The primary opportunities to reduce emissions were energy
efficiency and electrification efforts in Buildings and Facilities, fleet and procurement,
and supporting Platte River Power Authority's (PRPA) clean energy goals. Board
comments and questions have been summarized: inquired how the recommendations in
the CAP could be incorporated into the 2027 budget, Town Administrator Machalek
recommended the Board consider integrating the CAP findings in the strategic planning
process in 2027 and the budget process for 2028; expressed support for PRPA's efforts
to reduce emissions; stated desire to conduct a Return on Investment study of all
energy efficiency retrofits identified in the CAP; questioned the capacity of the existing

Overview of Fish Hatchery Project to Date.
Deputy Town Administrator Damweber provided an overview of the history of the Fish
Hatchery property, an approximately 75-acre parcel located near the Fall River entrance
to Rocky Mountain National Park. In 1982 the site was identified as a potential location
for workforce housing. The Town entered into an Exclusive Negotiation Agreement
(ENA) with AmericaWest in 2018, with the goal of coming to a mutually agreeable
Disposition and Redevelopment Agreement (DRA). The ENA expired in 2019 with no
DRA reached in part because of development constraints revealed during the site
evaluation process that would limit the density of the development. The Town partnered
with AmericaWest again in 2021 as a development partner, but an agreement was not
reached due to supply chain issues and the challenge of keeping proposed rental rates
affordable. In 2024 the Town partnered with Estes Park Housing Authority (EPHA) to
develop workforce housing on the site. It was determined an investment of at least $5
million in new water infrastructure would be required to provide adequate water supply
to the development. The Town and EPHA continued to investigate opportunities to
develop workforce housing at Fish Hatchery. Board comments and questions have
been summarized: questioned the estimated cost for the water infrastructure, staff
stated a new pump station and water storage tank were necessary to provide adequate
water and the estimated cost was a minimum of $5 million; Town Administrator
Machalek explained comparing the cost of water infrastructure projects was challenging
due to differences in topography and infrastructure needs of different developments;
asked about the availability of other locations for workforce housing development,
EPHA Executive Director Moulton stated EPHA was prepared to pivot if developing
workforce housing at Fish Hatchery was not financially viable; inquired how many
people were served by the current infrastructure, staff stated approximately six (6)
homes were served by the existing water infrastructure and any development in the
area would require water service to fire hydrants which could not be serviced by the
existing two (2) inch water main; questioned if other properties would benefit from a new
water tank, staff stated an accommodations business in the area as well as the Fall
River Visitor Center may benefit from the construction of a new water tank; discussed
desire for any development to be financially viable; noted as the land borders the river it
may have value for uses other than housing; questioned the cost of the water
infrastructure as a percentage of potential workforce housing development, Moulton
estimated the workforce housing development would cost a total of $70 million; and
acknowledged the annual operation and maintenance costs required to maintain
additional water infrastructure.
