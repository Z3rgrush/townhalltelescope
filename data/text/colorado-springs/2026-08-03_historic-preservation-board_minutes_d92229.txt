Ms. Flewelling began by defining historic preservation as the study, 
documentation, designation, protection, and physical preservation of 
historically significant buildings, structures, sites, objects, and districts. She 
noted that sites often include archaeological locations, farms, and ranches, 
while objects may include statues, murals, and artworks. She outlined 
reasons communities pursue preservation, explaining that it strengthens 
local identity, reduces landfill waste by reusing existing buildings, supports 
downtown revitalization and heritage tourism, increases skilled jobs, 
boosts property values, assists small businesses by providing usable 
historic spaces, and can result in affordable housing.
Ms. Flewelling reviewed the federal preservation framework established by 
the 1966 National Historic Preservation Act, which created State Historic 
Preservation Offices (SHPOs), the National Register of Historic Places, 
and federal project review under Section 106. A 1980 amendment 
introduced the Certified Local Government (CLG) program. She described 
the structure connecting the National Park Service, History Colorado’s 
SHPO, local preservation boards, and various nonprofit partners. Ms. 
Flewelling explained SHPO roles, including administering federal and state 
programs, maintaining statewide cultural resource data, coordinating tax 
credit programs, overseeing Section 106 compliance, and managing the 
State Historical Fund. She emphasized that each state has a CLG 
coordinator.
Ms. Flewelling described the CLG program as a partnership among local 
governments, the National Park Service, and SHPOs, and said Colorado 
Springs has been a CLG since 1994 and is one of 70 CLGs statewide. 
She noted recent additions, Rangely, Silverton, and Englewood. She 
stated CLGs benefit from annual non-match grants supporting preservation 
plans, surveys, design guidelines, and public education; and additional 
National Park Service grants may also be available. Ms. Flewelling added 
that locally designated properties and contributing district properties qualify

for state residential and commercial tax credits.
Ms. Flewelling stated that understanding local preservation ordinances is 
essential because they define board authority, procedures, and 
relationships with municipal bodies. She said ordinances and bylaws guide 
designation and design review decisions, ensure consistency, and clarify 
public participation requirements. She reminded members to avoid ex 
parte communication, declare conflicts of interest, and rely solely on 
testimony and evidence presented at hearings. Ms. Flewelling outlined 
procedural due process expectations for quasi-judicial boards, including 
public notice, opportunity to present arguments, and maintaining decorum. 
She stressed that decisions must be tied directly to adopted standards and 
codes, with clear reasoning when precedent does not apply. Board 
members should avoid statements that appear arbitrary or based on 
personal preferences.
Ms. Flewelling then reviewed preservation program components: survey, 
designation, design review, incentives, and public education. She 
explained survey types, reconnaissance, intensive, archaeological, 
comprehensive, and selective, and highlighted the role of historic context 
studies. Survey plans and preservation plans guide long-term program 
development. Ms. Flewelling summarized designation criteria based on 
significance and integrity, noting that only local designation provides 
regulatory protection. She said district creation requirements vary by 
ordinance, and CLGs cannot allow opt-out provisions. She said all reviews 
must align with the Secretary of the Interior’s Standards, supplemented by 
local design guidelines for specificity.
Ms. Flewelling outlined available incentives, including local zoning flexibility, 
technical assistance, state and federal tax credits, and loans. She 
described the state residential tax credit (20% up to $100,000, refundable 
beginning next year) and the commercial credit (20 to 25% up to $1 
million). She then described the State Historical Fund, noting annual grants 
for construction, planning, archaeology, surveys, and education, with 
competitive deadlines on April 1 and October 1.
Ms. Flewelling concluded with public education and advocacy strategies, 
where communities use walking tours, outreach materials, toolkits, mailers, 
brochures, door hangers, and annual notices. She highlighted creative 
methods such as QR-code coasters, preservation-month signage,
