RESOLUTION 2026-16 
City Council considered  Resolution 2026- 16, a Resolution of the City Council of the City of 
Montrose, Colorado, making a commitment to increase affordable housing units consistent with 
Colorado Proposition 123. 
 
Community Development Director Jace Hochwalt reported that adoption of Resolution 2026-16 
authorizes the City to opt in to Colorado Proposition 123 for the 2027- 2029 funding cycle. Mr. 
Hochwalt stated that Proposition 123 was voter approved in 2022 and established a dedicated 
state funding source to support affordable housing. The program o ffers grants, loans, and other 
financing opportunities to local governments, nonprofits, and housing developers to develop and 
preserve affordable housing, acquire  land for future affordable housing , and support  home 
ownership.

Mr. Hochwalt said t o remain eligible, the City must formally opt in and commit to meeting the 
State’s affordable housing production requirements. For the current cycle, the City approved 221 
affordable housing units, which exceeded the requirement to approve 162 units. Projects in the 
community benefiting from Proposition 123 include Black Canyon Flats, Community Options land 
banking, and MADA land banking. Mr. Hochwalt said the Community Development Department 
also received grants for the Comprehensive Plan update and the purchase of permitting software.  
 
Mr. Hochwalt said that the commitment for the 2027 -2029 cycle is approval of approximately 
125 units. If the goal is not achieved, the City is not eligible for funding in 2030 but could reapply 
in 2031.  
 
Public comment was accepted.  
 
Cliff Faurer questioned if the City-owned property at North Park Avenue and the San Juan Bypass 
will benefit from this funding. Mr. Hochwalt said this specific property was committed as part of 
the MADA land banking project. 
 
A motion was made by J. David Reed, seconded by Adam Woodden, to adopt Resolution 2026-
16 as presented. All voted yes. Motion passed.

RESOLUTION 2026-17 
City Council considered Resolution 2016- 17, a Resolution of the City of Montrose, Colorado, 
rescinding the City Manager's declaration of a local emergency within the City of Montrose, 
Colorado. 
 
Interim City Manager Ann Morgenthaler reported that the City Manager issued a declaration of 
a local emergency on June 29, 2026, in response to the Gold Mountain fire near Ouray. Extremely 
dry conditions existed throughout the region increasing the threat of fire danger, and the City 
was a possible evacuation site for those fleeing wildfires.  Ms. Morgenthaler said the declaration 
also halted the sale of fireworks within the city limits. Per state statute, a city manager may issue 
an emergency declaration for up to seven days. On July 6, 2026, City Council adopted Emergency 
Ordinance 2722 extending the City Manager’s emergency declaration.  
 
Ms. Morgenthaler recommended adoption of Resolution 2026-17 to rescind the declaration. 
 
Public comment was accepted. No comments were received. 
 
A motion was made b y Adam Woodden, seconded by Ed Ulibarri, to adopt Resolution 2026-17 
as presented. All voted yes. Motion passed.

Dear Members of the Montrose City Council, 
 
I am writing to be considered as a candidate to fill the at-large City Council seat left vacant by 
the resignation and reassignment of Council Member Adam Woodden to the District IV seat in 
which he resides. 
 
I moved to the City of Montrose 8 years ago to design and build my sister and brothers-in-law’s 
house located in Montrose County.  Determined to leave right after the completion of this project 
I instead fell in love with the friendly and much slower pace of living here and decided to make 
Montrose my permanent home. 
 
I practiced architecture as a senior project manager in Phoenix, Az. for over 35 years until my 
semi-retirement in 2013.  My years of experience helped me gain a solid understanding of urban 
planning, city center blight, walkability, growth and commerce success to help build a better 
community and ultimately a better citizen base that will thrive now and into the future. 
 
During my years in Phoenix, I worked with various local, city, state and even federal agencies to 
help better the landscape of Phoenix and the surrounding cities.  Through various church and 
private organizations, I engaged in neighbor clean-ups, volunteered with youth groups and 
various women groups to help create positive influences and experiences.   
 
Here in the City of Montrose, I have been serving on the Historic Preservation Commission for 
around 6 years and have engaged through my church to work with and teach in women 
organizations.  I also volunteer in various clean-up activities and frequently donate to homeless 
shelters and food pantries.  
 
I believe all these experiences equip me to better understand issues facing a rapidly growing city 
and all the challenges that come with such growth.  Such as public safety, infrastructure, 
economic growth and citizen prosperity.

Letter of Intent 
September 17, 2026 
Dear Mayor Badagliacco and Members of the Montrose City Council, 
I did not come to Montrose seeking public office. When I was asked to consider applying for the 
vacant District IV Council seat, I took the invitation seriously. Since then, I have spent time 
learning more about the responsibilities of Council, reviewing current City priorities, and 
attending both a City Council work session and regular Council meeting. That process has 
strengthened my interest in offering my experience and perspective in service to our community. 
When my husband, Todd, and I chose Montrose as our new home, we made a commitment that 
we would not simply move here, but that we would become part of the community. After 
spending more than three decades deeply rooted in our former community, we understood the 
importance of relationships, involvement, and community service. From our first month in 
Montrose, we intentionally began meeting people, supporting local businesses, getting involved, 
and looking for ways to contribute. 
My professional background includes more than 35 years of business ownership and leadership 
across multiple industries, along with experience in education, real estate, leadership 
development, and mentoring. Our family has owned restaurants and service businesses, giving 
me firsthand understanding of the opportunities and many challenges facing small business 
owners, employers, and employees. 
One of my most relevant civic leadership experiences was serving as President of the 
Downtown Murrieta Merchants Association. The association served as a liaison between 
downtown merchants, City staff, and City Council. During my tenure, we worked collaboratively 
to strengthen our downtown, including developing Market Nights, which grew from a 
merchant-led initiative into a partnership with the City. That experience taught me the 
importance of listening to different stakeholders, building productive relationships with City staff 
and elected officials, and moving good ideas toward sustainable community solutions. 
Leadership development has been a consistent thread throughout my career. I began my 
professional career in education and served as an Upper Elementary Mentor Teacher, leading 
department meetings and conducting monthly teacher performance reviews. Later, within our 
family business, I developed an employee leadership program around a question that became 
important to me: “How can we help our people leave their experience with us better equipped 
for whatever comes next?” Today, I work as a Fractional Leadership Development Partner, 
helping businesses and organizations develop stronger leaders and teams.

Since arriving in Montrose, our priority has been to listen and learn rather than arrive with 
assumptions about what this community should become. We are not here to recreate the 
community we left. We have worked to understand what matters to the people who have built 
their lives, businesses, farms, ranches, and families here. I have been deeply impressed by the 
work ethic, generosity, entrepreneurial spirit, and sense of community we have encountered. 
At the same time, having lived through decades of significant growth in our former community, I

If appointed, I would bring particular interest in thoughtful growth, a strong small-business

community that make Montrose a place people want to call home. I would approach the 
responsibility first as a representative of the residents of District IV, while recognizing that the 
decisions made at the Council table affect the entire Montrose community. 
Todd and I came to Montrose because we saw something special here. I would be honored to 
help serve and steward that community as it prepares for the future. 
Respectfully, 
Cindee Williams

Honorable Mayor, Members of the City Council and Montrose Citizens: 
The administrative team of the City of Montrose respectfully submits the 2027 municipal budget for all funds of 
the City in the amount of $ 158,186,207. The 2027 municipal budget is the result of a comprehensive and 
collaborative effort by the City staff, elected officials, and members of the Montrose citizenry to establish a 
budget designed to meet the current and long -term needs of the City organizatio n as well as those of the 
Montrose community. 
The City of Montrose is in a solid financial position, and we are optimistic that our economic situation will remain 
steady throughout 2027. City sales tax collections have risen over the past several years due to an increase in 
consumer spending, regional growth, and the overall economic health of the Montrose area. We did see a leveling 
off in 2026 and expect the same trend in 2027. The general public’s spending related to retail shopping, 
restaurants, and lodging has continued to increase over the past several years and is projected to generate 
approximately $695,800 for the Retail Sales Enhancement Fund and $944,826 for the Tourism & Promotion Fund 
in 2027.   Note that these funds are designed to be used toward various activities, programs, services, and 
infrastructure related to tourism, marketing, and business development, as required by the voters upon the 
establishment of the funds. We are very thankful t o our Montrose residents who approved an increase to our 
City’s Hotel/Lodging tax rate effective January 2025. With the additional revenue generated by those visiting our 
community, we are able to provide additional support to programs related to Street Maintenance, Affordable 
Housing, Childcare Services, Public Transit, and Community Events. 
Municipal Operations: Our local job market remains competitive and challenging, with low unemployment. 
Therefore, it is important to be a responsible employer to recruit and retain a highly productive and impactful 
workforce that will serve Montrose citizens with respect, ethics, leadership, accountability, transparency, and 
excellence in accordance with our City organization’s core beliefs . We are proud to say that we have built an 
amazing team of highly educated and experienced employees by offering a competitive Wage and 
Compensation System based on both Individual Performance and the City’s comparable market of organizations 
with similar budgets, number of employees, services provided, and community population . As part of this 
process, every position description at the City is reviewed in detail on an annual basis and updated to make sure 
that our employees fully understand what is expected of them regarding performance and productivity. The City 
of Montrose is currently budgeting 218 full -time employees, which has increa sed primarily due to new public 
safety positions funded by the citizen supported Public Safety Sales Tax that was approved in 2019  and some 
changes to our staffing in the Parks and Streets divisions. We also employ continuous part -time and seasonal 
positions here at the City. City staff work efficiently to provide the best possible public service at the lowest cost. 
We are proud to say that we are providing better quality services and a wider variety of services to the Montrose 
public than ever before, and we are doing it more effectively  and efficiently using a combination of innovative

Financial Condition: Sales Tax, Use Tax, and Franchise Fees are a prominent revenue source for the City’s General 
Fund at approximately 89% of total revenues. Over the last few years, sales tax revenues have steadily increased 
on an annual basis with modest increases in 2026 over 2025 actual collections; therefore, 2027 sales tax revenue 
is budgeting a 2.0% increase from the actual revenue collections in 2025. The remaining revenues were calculated 
using various forecasting methods and historical trends data. The General Fund Operating budget for 2027 is 
projected to be approximately $26.4 million, which does not include the Rec District’s Recreation Center sales tax 
collections, capital expenses, grant expenses, or internal transfers. 
The General Undesignated Fund Balance as of December 31, 2027, is estimated to be approximately $3.6 million. 
This equates to approximately 25.6%, or slightly over 3 months, of 2027 operating expenditures and is in 
compliance with the policy in the City’s Regulations Manual in Section 7-8-1 (B) stating that the “long range plan 
for the General Fund provides for a recommended minimum balance that equals 15 to 20 percent of ope rating 
expenditures as a carryover for each year.”  This balance is lower than in previous years as a result of the 
completion since 2020  of major capital projects including several roundabouts, street extensions, the 
construction of the Public Safety Complex, the purchase and renovation of a new City Hall, the Year of Main Street 
infrastructure improvements, and the construction of a new public works facility which will be completed in 2027. 
These projects will ensure excellent and efficient service delivery to our community through the next 50 years or 
more. We expect to build our reserves starting in 2028.  
Each year the City transfers funds from the General Fund to the Capital Improvements Fund. The City’s Municipal 
Code Section 5 -15-32 states that “at minimum $800,000 shall be annually appropriated to the Capital 
Improvements Fund.” The anticipated transfer to the Capital Improvements Fund in 2027 is $6.6 million. The 
transfer amount is always contingent upon revenue projections and the past three years have resulted in an 
annual increase to the transfer amount to complete a variety of capital projects including sidewalk and street 
reconstruction, traffic flow impro vements, and other necessary infrastructure improvements. The City’s streets, 
sidewalks, curbs, and gutter systems are aging and our efforts to put millions of dollars into rehabilitation and 
major maintenance is making a difference throughout our community. The City Council and staff are responsive 
to concerns brought up in the most recent Citizen Survey related to street maintenance and traffic congestion.

Action 
City Council consideration of proposed revisions to the fee schedule effective January 1, 2027. 
Staff members will be present at the work session to address questions from City Council. 
 
Background 
Proposed fee schedule revisions are brought to City Council each year for discussion at an 
October work session. Adoption of the fee schedule for the upcoming year is considered by City 
Council at the first regular meeting in November in conjunction with the first reading of the 
budget ordinance. 
 
Listed below is a summary of proposed revisions for 2027: 
 
3-1-1 (A) Administrative Fees 
 Special Waste Collection fees were moved to Section 3-1-3 (C). 
   
3-1-1 (C) Cemetery Fees 
 Lot sale fees were increased to align with other regional cemeteries. 
 Lot sale fees were added for infant lots. 
Open/close fees were increased to cover actual costs; additional categories were added. 
 Overtime fees were increased and updated to cover actual costs. 
 Columbarium niche plate costs were updated to reflect actual costs. 
 
3-1-1 (D) Land Use Fees 
The Land Dedication Fee in Lieu – School Fee was increased to reflect current property 
values and maintain consistency with Montrose County’s fee structure.  
 
3-1-1 (F) Parks/Private Use of Public Property 
 The memorial bench fee was increased to reflect the actual cost of the bench. 
 A $75 fee was added for staff to turn on electricity for a park shelter reservation. 
 
3-1-1 (H) Building Code Fees 
A $50 reinspection fee was added and applies to each reinspection beginning with the 
third violation identified for the same inspection under the same permit.
