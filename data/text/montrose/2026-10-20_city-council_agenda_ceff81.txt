To: Honorable Mayor and Members of the City Council        
From: Lisa DelPiccolo, City Clerk 
Council Work Session Date: October 20, 2026  
Re: 2027 Fee Schedule 
Attachments: 
● Proposed Fee Schedule Revisions for 2027 
 
 
Action 
City Council consideration of proposed revisions to the fee schedule effective January 1, 2027. 
Staff members will be present at the work session to address questions from City Council. 
 
Background 
Proposed fee schedule revisions are brought to City Council each year for discussion at an 
October work session. Adoption of the fee schedule for the upcoming year is considered by City 
Council at the first regular meeting in November in conjunction with the first reading of the 
budget ordinance. 
 
Listed below is a summary of proposed revisions for 2027: 
 
3-1-1 (A) Administrative Fees 
 Special Waste Collection fees were moved to Section 3-1-3 (C). 
   
3-1-1 (C) Cemetery Fees 
 Lot sale fees were increased to align with other regional cemeteries. 
 Lot sale fees were added for infant lots. 
Open/close fees were increased to cover actual costs; additional categories were added. 
 Overtime fees were increased and updated to cover actual costs. 
 Columbarium niche plate costs were updated to reflect actual costs. 
 
3-1-1 (D) Land Use Fees 
The Land Dedication Fee in Lieu – School Fee was increased to reflect current property 
values and maintain consistency with Montrose County’s fee structure.  
 
3-1-1 (F) Parks/Private Use of Public Property 
 The memorial bench fee was increased to reflect the actual cost of the bench. 
 A $75 fee was added for staff to turn on electricity for a park shelter reservation. 
 
3-1-1 (H) Building Code Fees 
A $50 reinspection fee was added and applies to each reinspection beginning with the 
third violation identified for the same inspection under the same permit. 
 
 
Page 10 of 144
 
 
 
 
3-1-3 (A) Water Rates and Fees 
 Water connection fees will increase by 3 percent. 
Water use fees per 1,000 gallons will increase by 3 percent plus a $0.12 per 1,000 
gallons pass-through for Project 7 Water Authority.  
 
3-1-3 (B) Sanitary Sewer Rates and Fees 
Sewer connection and usage fees will increase by 20 percent to fund the debt service for 
the WWTP Improvements, the CIP3 Sewer Interceptor and the Sewer Fund portion of 
the new Public Works Facility projects that were funded by the $40 million Bond Issue 
earlier this year. 
 
The following chart lists the residential sewer rate comparisons for many of our 
neighboring communities. 
 
The site-specific BOD surcharge formula was adjusted to reflect the 20% increase in 
sanitary sewer rates. 
A $200 sewer retap fee was added for those properties that need to retap into the City 
sewer main. 
 
3-1-3 (C) Solid Waste Collection and Recycling Rates and Fees 
Solid waste collection and recycling rates will increase by 3 percent. 
A monthly service charge was added for non-residential customers.

Background 
On June 29, 2026, in response to the Gold Mountain Fire near Ouray, Colorado, the incredibly 
dry conditions in Montrose County and other nearby counties, the overall threat of fire danger in 
the region, the possibility that the City of Montrose may be an evacuation location for those 
fleeing regional wildfires, and the desire to quickly stop the sale of fireworks in the City of 
Montrose, the City Manager signed a Declaration of the City Manager Declaring a Local 
Emergency. Per the Colorado Revised Statutes, a City Manager may issue an emergency 
declaration in certain circumstances, and for up to seven (7) days.  
On July 6, 2026, the City Council approved an Emergency Ordinance Extending the City 
Manager’s Declaration of an Emergency. This was done to ensure that the City of Montrose was 
prepared in case the existing wildfires or evacuees from the wildfires directly impacted the City. 
If Ouray and Ridgway were to evacuate, we expected to see an influx of approximately 5,000 
people into Montrose. By declaring an emergency within the City of Montrose ahead of time, we 
were able to avoid the need to call a special city council meeting during a disaster or emergency 
and instead focus on immediate response needs should they arise.  
The attached Report to City Council prepared by City of Montrose Emergency Manager Paul 
Eller describes the support offered by the City of Montrose during this emergency. Fortunately, 
the communities of Ouray and Ridgway did not need to evacuate and the Gold Mountain Fire 
did not cause damage to property within the City of Montrose.

History 
 
The City of Montrose, located in Montrose County, within the Uncompahgre Valley, 
experienced very mild and dry conditions during the 2025-2026 winter season. 
 
The lack of measurable snow and lack of spring-time precipitation compounded existing 
drought conditions across the region, and the State of Colorado. 
 
Unseasonably hot temperatures and dry conditions in May and early June, further 
exacerbated drought conditions in the valley, within the San Juan Mountains, and along 
the Uncompaghre Plateau. Area forests were in record stages of drought. 
 
By late June numerous small to medium sized wildfires were occurring on the western 
slope, and on the eastern slope of the state of Colorado. Available firefighting resources 
became limited due to the number of wildfires. The Montrose County Sheriff had 
enacted Stage-1 fire restrictions throughout Montrose County. Other surrounding 
counties entered into Stage 2 fire restrictions on June 23, 2026. 
 
On June 27, 2026, the Gold Mountain Fire started in the mountains between the City of 
Ouray and the Town of Ridgway. The fire caused the evacuation of several subdivisions 
within Ouray County and also threatened the City of Ouray. 
 
During this same time, four other major wildfires started within the state, depleting fire 
resources and increasing threats to life-safety. 
 
Emergency Declarations 
 
Ouray County 
On June 27, 2026, Ouray County Sheriff Justin Perry declared an emergency in support 
of the Gold Mountain Fire for Ouray County. 
 
State 
On June 28, 2026, Governor Jared Polis orally declared an emergency for the Gold 
Mountain Fire in Ouray County. 
 
Page 31 of 144
City of Montrose 
On June 29, 2026, City Manager Bill Bell declared an emergency due to Montrose 
County and surrounding county fire restrictions, local drought conditions, and to 
support the needs of Ouray County during the Gold Mountain Fire response. 
 
The emergency declaration was ratified by members of the Montrose City Council on 
July 6, 2026, and extended through October 20, 2026. 
 
Emergency Declaration Rescinded 
The emergency declaration was rescinded by members of Montrose City Council on 
October 20, 2026. 
 
Actions Taken 
On June 29, 2026, as a result of the City of Montrose emergency declaration, the sale of 
fireworks within city limits were banned until September 1, 2026.  
 
As part of the Gold Mountain fire response, the Montrose Police Department provided 
both mutual-aid and requested support resources to support Ouray County. 
 
Mutual-aid support was provided by sending police officers to assist with traffic control 
points and perimeter security on June 27 and June 28, 2026. This support equated to 
four police officers providing 31 hours of mutual-aid support. 
 
The city’s emergency manager provided five days of mutual-aid support as the Plans 
Section Chief for the Montrose County Emergency Operations Center (EOC) while the 
EOC was activated in support of Ouray County evacuations and livestock sheltering 
operations at Montrose County Fairgrounds. This span of time was from July 1 to July 5, 
2026, for a total of 51.5 hours. 
 
On June 28, 2026, Chief Hall  directed all members of the Police Department to be in 
“Stand-by On-call” status, in the event that the City of Ouray needed assistance with 
mass evacuation, or if Montrose County needed additional assistance with a future 
wildfire within our county.  Chief Hall rescinded the “Stand-by On-call” status at the end 
of the day on July 1, 2026. Total “Stand-by On-call” hours were 227 hours. 
Page 32 of 144
On June 6, 2026, City Emergency Manager Paul Eller was requested by Sheriff Perry and 
the Colorado Division of Homeland Security and Emergency Management  to assist with 
Emergency Operations Center Manager duties for Ouray County through June 16, 2026. 
EM Eller provided the requested assistance. EM Eller provided a total of 139.5 hours in 
support managing the Ouray County EOC. 
 
On July 12, 2026, following a fatal firefighting helicopter crash, the Montrose Police 
Department Peer Support Team provided two days of initial peer support to the 
firefighters and incident command staff working the fire. The Peer Support Team was 
provided as a mutual-aid resource until federal peer support teams could arrive on 
location. The Peer Support Team provided 43.5 total hours of mutual-aid support. 
 
On July 14, 2026, Sergeant Cameron Pensyl was requested to provide traffic control and 
perimeter security in the Middle Fork of the Cimarron in support of a forward operations 
base and continuing fire response operations.  Sgt. Pensyl provided 13 hours of support. 
 
During the emergency response to the Gold Mountain Fire, the Rocky Mountain Critical 
Incident Management Teams requested assistance from the City of Montrose. This 
assistance was provided as land use agreements for the use of city water to fill fire 
engines, fire tenders, and potable water trucks. Disposal of grey water from the main 
operation sites. And, to provide a property at the top of Cerro Summit for a helicopter 
refueling location.

Summation 
 
The 2026 drought and wildfire conditions have been some of the worst conditions in our 
region’s history, and within the state of Colorado.  
 
The possibility of rapidly growing wildfire threatening lives, critical infrastructure, and 
communities, necessitated swift and decisive actions to protect communities across the 
Uncompahgre region to include the City of Montrose. 
 
While additional wildfires did not develop in the region, the Gold Mountain Fire not only 
affected the communities within Ouray County, but the fire also greatly impacted the 
Montrose, Olathe, and Delta communities. Impacts included but were not limited to 
health impacts from smoke, impacts to livestock and the ranching communities, impacts 
to local tourism, and economic losses. 
 
City staff worked cooperatively with each other, Montrose County staff, and Ouray 
County staff to provide critical and meaningful support during this time.  
 
I am proud of the City staff and their commitment to the needs of the community and 
our neighbors, as well as the outstanding support provided during the Gold Mountain 
Fire.

Re: Proposition 123 Opt-In for the 2027–2029 Funding Cycle 
Attachments: 
● Exhibit A - Resolution 2026-16 
 
Action 
Consider the approval of Resolution 2026-16, authorizing the City of Montrose to opt in to 
Colorado Proposition 123 for the 2027–2029 funding cycle and maintain eligibility for state 
funding opportunities that support the development and preservation of affordable housing. 
 
Background 
Colorado Proposition 123 was approved by voters to establish a dedicated state funding source 
to increase the supply of affordable housing and support housing initiatives. The program 
creates funding opportunities for local governments, nonprofit organizations, land trusts, tribal 
governments, and private developers through various programs administered by the Colorado 
Department of Local Affairs, Division of Housing (DOH), and the Colorado Office of Economic 
Development and International Trade (OEDIT). Certain programs are administered through the 
Colorado Housing and Finance Authority (CHFA). 
 
Proposition 123 funding supports a range of eligible activities, including the development and 
preservation of affordable housing, land acquisition for future housing projects, and other 
initiatives designed to expand housing opportunities for qualifying households. By providing an 
additional source of state funding, the program helps communities address local housing needs, 
advance projects that may otherwise face financial constraints, and leverage partnerships to 
increase the availability of affordable housing. 
 
To access these funding opportunities, local governments must formally opt in and commit to 
meeting the state's affordable housing production requirements over each applicable three-year 
cycle. This commitment establishes a baseline for qualifying affordable housing units and helps 
ensure that participating communities are actively contributing to the expansion of the state's 
affordable housing supply. Participation also allows eligible organizations and developers 
operating within the jurisdiction to pursue available Proposition 123 funding for qualifying 
projects. 
 
During the 2023–2026 cycle, the City of Montrose committed to approving at least 162 
qualifying affordable housing units over the three-year period. The City exceeded this 
requirement, with 221 qualifying units approved, surpassing the established threshold by 59 
units. The City also implemented an expedited review process in 2024 to facilitate the timely 
review and advancement of affordable housing projects. 
 
Page 44 of 144
 
 
 
2 
The City's participation in Proposition 123 has helped secure significant state investment in local 
housing initiatives. Black Canyon Flats received over $8 million in Proposition 123 equity 
funding to support a 60-unit mixed-income housing development. Additionally, Community 
Options, Inc. received a $1.25 million Proposition 123 land-banking award for a proposed 50-
unit affordable housing development. The Mexican American Development Association (MADA) 
was also awarded $475,000 in land-banking funds in September 2026 for a future project. 
These awards demonstrate how Proposition 123 funding can support both near-term housing 
development and the acquisition of land for future affordable housing opportunities. 
 
As Proposition 123 enters the 2027–2029 cycle, the City has the opportunity to renew its 
participation and maintain eligibility for these state funding resources. Based on current 
estimates, the City's affordable housing production commitment for the upcoming cycle is 
expected to be approximately 125 units over three years, subject to final confirmation by the 
state. Continued participation will build on the City's successful record of exceeding its prior 
commitment, strengthen partnerships with housing providers, and provide additional 
opportunities to secure funding for projects that expand affordable housing availability in 
Montrose. 
 
Fiscal Impact 
There is no direct financial obligation associated with the City's decision to opt in. Opting in does 
not guarantee an award of state funding; however, it maintains eligibility for the City and 
qualifying community partners to apply for available Proposition 123 funding. 
Any future financial commitments associated with specific projects, matching requirements, or 
other funding conditions will be evaluated as applicable during the review and approval of 
individual projects. 
Conclusion 
Staff recommends that City Council approve a resolution authorizing the City of Montrose to opt 
in to Colorado Proposition 123 for the 2027–2029 cycle. The City successfully met all 
requirements of the 2023–2026 cycle and approved affordable housing units in excess of the 
required threshold. Continuing participation will allow the City and its community partners to 
pursue additional state funding opportunities and further the City's ongoing efforts to increase 
affordable housing availability for the Montrose community.

Action 
Consider the approval of extending the Bruin Waste Landfill Agreement for the period of up to 
one year. 
 
Background 
The City’s Solid Waste operation collects trash from residential and commercial customers 
and then delivers that trash to a landfill.  The City has been utilizing Bruin Waste for 2 years 
for Landfill Services.  Bruin Waste operates a transfer station that City of Montrose deliver solid 
waste to and then Bruin transfers that waste to a landfill near Naturita, CO.  When the City 
awarded the original contract 2 years ago it was determined that the option of using Bruin 
was a significant savings to City operations vs the other bidders.  Since then, Bruin has 
performed very well and is a good partner in this operation.   
The City of Montrose’s current landfill agreement is expiring and it is time to renew the 
agreement.  If approved, this is the second renewal of this landfill agreement.  Bruin Waste is 
proposing a rate increase with this contract renewal.  The tipping fee tonnage rate would 
increase from $66.37/ton to $71.00/ton.  This increase is in alignment with the annual CPI 
increase, labor cost increases and fuel costs.  This increase was anticipated in the 2026 and 
2027 budgets and does not require an increase to the rates that the City of Montrose 
customers can expect to pay. 
 
 
Fiscal Impact 
Tipping Fees are paid from GL 550-7100-358-000 in the Trash and Recycle Division budget.  
The anticipated total waste to be collected in the next year is between 9,500 and 10,500 tons.  
For a total annual expense of about $675,000 to $750,000. The annual budget of $820,000 for 
disposal fees will cover these expenses as well as the costs of recycling tipping fees.

This management discussion and analysis is intended to serve as an introduction to the City’s basic financial 
statements.  The City’s basic financial statements comprise three components: 1) government-wide financial 
statements, 2) fund financial statements, and 3) notes to the financial statements.  This report also contains other 
supplementary information in addition to the basic financial statements themselves. 
Government-wide financial statements.  The government-wide financial statements are designed to provide the 
reader with a broad overview of the City’s finances, in a manner like a private-sector business. 
The statement of net position  presents information on all the City’s assets and liabilities, with the difference 
between the two reported as net position.  Over time, increases or decreases in net position may serve as a useful 
indicator of whether the financial position of the City of Montrose is improving or deteriorating. 
The statement of activities  presents information showing how the City’s net position changed during the most 
recent fiscal year.  All changes in net position are reported as soon as the underlying event giving rise to the 
change occurs, regardless of the timing of related cash flows .  Thus, revenues and expenses are reported in this 
statement for some items that will only result in cash flows in future fiscal periods (e.g., uncollected taxes and 
earned but unused vacation leave). 
Both government-wide financial statements distinguish functions of the City of Montrose that are principally 
supported by taxes and intergovernmental revenues ( governmental activities ) from other functions that are 
intended to recover all or a significant portion of their costs through user fees and charges ( business-type-
activities).  The governmental activities of the city include general government, public safety, Pavilion operations, 
parks maintenance and street maintenance.  The business-type activities of the City of Montrose include Water 
distribution, Sewer collection and treatment, Trash and Recycling, Black Canyon Golf Course, Fleet 
Management, Information Services, and Facilities management. 
The government-wide financial statements can be found on pages 4 and 5 of the financial statements. 
Fund financial statements .  A fund is a grouping of related accounts that is used to maintain control over 
resources that have been segregated for specific activities or objectives.  The City of Montrose, like other states 
and local governments, uses fund accounting to ensure and demonstrate compliance with finance-related legal 
requirements.  All the funds of the City of Montrose can be divided into two categories: governmental funds and 
proprietary funds. 
Governmental funds.  Governmental funds are used to account for essentially the same functions reported as 
governmental activities  in the government-wide financial statements.  However, unlike the government-wide 
financial statements, governmental fund financial statements focus on near-term inflows and outflows of 
spendable resources, as well as on balances of spendable resources available at the end of the fiscal year.  Such 
information may be useful in evaluating a city’s near-term financing requirements. 
Because the focus of governmental funds is narrower than that of the government-wide financial statements, it is 
useful to compare the information presented for governmental funds  with similar information presented for 
governmental activities in the government-wide financial statements.  By doing so, readers may better understand 
the long-term impact of the City’s near-term financing decisions.  Both the governmental fund balance sheet and 
the governmental fund statement of revenues, expenditures, and changes in fund balances provide a reconciliation 
to facilitate this comparison between governmental funds and governmental activities.
