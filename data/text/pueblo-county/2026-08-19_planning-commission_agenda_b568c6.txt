SUBJECT: Use-Specific Standards for Data Centers 
 
PURPOSE 
 
The Pueblo County Unified Development Code (UDC) provides for Data Centers as a Special Use 
in the LI, HI, and PP zone districts. However, the UDC does not prescribe use-specific standards 
for Data Centers, which can be an intensive use. The purpose of this work session is to provide 
draft standards to the Planning Commission and receive feedback on same, with the end goal of 
adopting suitable use-specific standards into the UDC. 
BACKGROUND  
 
Data Center, which the UDC defines as “a use where the primary purpose is the storage, 
management, processing, and/or transmission of digital data, and where the majority of the space 
is occupied by computers, servers, telecommunications equipment, and related supporting 
infrastructure,” is a broad term for a facility which houses IT infrastructure for data storage and 
management. Data centers started out as on-premises facilities housing traditional IT infrastructure 
for the exclusive use of one company. However recently they have increasingly become remote 
facilities or networks of facilities owned by cloud service providers which house virtualized IT 
infrastructure for the shared use of multiple companies and customers. 
 
The demand for Artificial Intelligence (AI) services have created the need for larger-scale data 
centers. As these data centers become larger in scale and are developed in closer proximity to 
residential uses, the externalities created by these facilities have come under close scrutiny by 
residents, local governments, and environmental agencies. 
 
One critical tool which can be used to mitigate the negative external impacts of data centers is 
zoning. The Pueblo County UDC already establishes use-specific standards for high-impact uses 
such as animal feeding operations (feedlots), marijuana cultivation, processing, and retail facilities, 
and certain industrial uses. Because data centers are a land use which have a high likelihood for 
external impacts and can be highly controversial, staff recommends adopting use-specific 
standards for data centers in order to be prepared for such a time when and if a data center is 
proposed within Pueblo County.

RESEARCH 
 
In order to understand the impacts of data centers which are most commonly regulated through 
zoning, staff reviewed city and county land use and zoning codes from across Colorado, as well as 
two major data center hubs in the United States: Northern Virginia and the Phoenix, Arizona metro 
area. From review of these codes, staff determined that regulation of data centers varies widely. 
Some zoning codes do not contemplate data centers at all, some do list them as a use but contain 
no or minimal use-specific standards, and others do include standards specific to data center uses. 
 
From the codes that do include use-specific standards, staff compiled the various external impacts 
which these codes regulate. These impacts, as well as a range of example regulations and 
analysis, can be found below. Based on this analysis, staff has also drafted proposed use-specific 
standards for the Planning Commission to review. These are attached as Exhibit 1. 
 
NOISE 
 
Data centers require continuous cooling, generally by means of a closed circulating water-cooling 
system not unlike a car radiator. This cooling system can generate noise and vibration which is a 
common concern voiced by those impacted by data center operations. 
 
Many of the codes analyzed imposed a specific decibel limit which data centers could not exceed; 
these ranged from 55 dbA (Phoenix, AZ) to 70 dbA (Loudoun County, VA). Others (Chandler, AZ) 
simply prohibited data centers from exceeding the baseline (pre-development) ambient decibel 
level. 
 
These noise restrictions are often enforced by a two- or three-part noise study requirement. First, 
the developer is required to measure the baseline noise level prior to the development of the data 
center. Then, the developer must submit a second measurement taken once the center has been 
fully built out and is operational. Some jurisdictions require these measurements only onsite, but 
others (Mesa, AZ) also require measurements at the nearest residential use or zone district. These 
two measurements are then compared to ensure the operational center is compliant with sound 
limits, and additional sound-mitigation measures are required if necessary. Some jurisdictions then 
require ongoing testing and reporting, typically yearly for the first five years of data center operation 
(Chandler, AZ). 
 
Staff Recommendation: The existing Pueblo County noise ordinance already provides flexibility in 
allowing for more noise in heavier industrial areas. Staff’s proposal is to ensure data center noise 
generation remains under the maximums allowed by the noise ordinance. In order to enforce these 
noise requirements, staff is also proposing the requirement that pre- and post-construction noise 
studies to be completed. However, staff does not believe there is a need for ongoing noise studies 
in all cases, but does propose allowing the department to require them if noise complaints are 
received. 
 
WATER USAGE 
 
Data centers can utilize large amounts of water in the cooling process. Even sophisticated closed-
loop cooling systems require significant water up front, which is a concern in the arid conditions of 
the American West. 
 
To ensure that data centers are not depleting water supplies relied on by other water users, many 
jurisdictions require a water services plan to be submitted with data center application materials. 
This study, typically prepared by a qualified engineer, will outline both the projected water

consumption of a proposed data center use as well as the proposed source of water and proof that 
the developer will obtain sufficient water.  
 
Some jurisdictions have also enacted additional requirements that data centers cannot utilize 
treated potable water for cooling purposes (Spotsylvania County, VA) or cannot use water from a 
specific vulnerable waterway or basin (Orange County, VA).  
 
Staff Recommendation: Staff recommends requiring data center developers to provide a detailed 
water usage plan in order for decision-makers to determine whether a proposed data center will 
negatively impact existing water users and water quality. 
 
ENERGY USAGE 
 
Another major concern in addition to water usage is power usage. Data centers require a large 
amount of electricity to operate, and there is often concern that existing power infrastructure will 
not be sufficient to serve data center uses. 
 
Some jurisdictions require an energy service plan, similar to the water services plan outlined 
above, to prove that there is a service provider willing and able to provide adequate power to the 
proposed data center, and how supplemental power will be generated onsite if necessary. For 
example, Phoenix, AZ requires the following: 
 
“Preliminary site plan approval will not be granted for a data center until such time that a local utility 
company provides a contractual agreement that affirms its capacity and commitment to serve the 
energy demand for the proposed data center. The agreement from the utility company shall be 
submitted to [the planning department] concurrent with the preliminary site plan.” 
 
Staff Recommendation: Staff recommends requiring data center developers to provide a detailed 
utility usage plan in order for decision-makers to determine whether adequate utility service exists 
in the area of proposed data center. 
 
BACKUP GENERATORS 
 
Because power outages could cause both data loss and damage to servers, data centers typically 
include provisions for backup power generation. The most common means of backup power 
generation is a series of diesel generators. While critical for power maintenance during 
emergencies, diesel generators are a source of both noise pollution and localized air pollution. As 
such, the majority of codes staff reviewed included limitations on the operation of backup 
generators. For example: 
 
“All emergency power generators shall be restricted to backup or emergency use only… Generator 
testing shall only occur between the hours of 10:00 a.m. and 4:00 p.m.” – Orange County, VA 
 
Some jurisdictions (such as Mesa, AZ) also take the additional step of requiring the operator to 
notify the jurisdiction 24 hours ahead of any planned generator test. 
 
Staff Recommendation: Staff does recommend limiting generator usage to emergencies and 
testing purposes only, but does not find it necessary to also require advance notification for testing.

VISUAL IMPACT 
 
The majority of codes staff reviewed include specific design and architectural standards for the 
data center buildings, since these facilities can be quite large in scale and stand out against either 
smaller-scale facilities or the natural environment. These standards can include articulation 
requirements for visual bulk reduction, materials and color standards, and architectural detail 
requirements to break up otherwise blank windowless walls. 
 
In addition to architectural standards for the building itself, a number of jurisdictions also require 
screening for onsite accessory equipment such as backup generators. As an example, 
Fredericksburg, VA requires that “Accessory equipment not screened by a principal building or 
perimeter buffer shall be screened by a visually solid fence, screen wall or panel, parapet wall, or 
other visually solid screen that shall be constructed of materials compatible with those used in the 
exterior construction of the principal building.” 
 
Staff Recommendation: The UDC does not include specific architectural requirements for any 
other uses, so staff does not believe including architectural requirements for data centers would be 
consistent with the intent and purpose of the UDC. However, staff does recommend requiring 
screening for accessory equipment to reduce the visual impact of this equipment, as well as 
additional setback requirements for accessory equipment. 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
The purpose of this work session is to provide the Planning Commission with draft standards for 
data center uses, as well as context as to why these standards are being proposed. Staff would 
like receive direction as to whether these standards should be proposed in a formal Text 
Amendment for Planning Commission recommendation and Board of County Commissioners 
approval.

SUBJECT: Amendments to the Pueblo Regional Comprehensive Plan 
 
PURPOSE 
 
Pueblo County Planning staff refers to the Pueblo Regional Comprehensive Plan (the 
“Comprehensive Plan”) whenever they made a land-use decision or recommendation to a 
decision-making body, in order to ensure the decision is aligned with the plan. Over years of 
working with this document, staff has identified several areas where the Future Land Use (“FLU”)  
designations of certain areas are inconsistent with land use and development patterns. The 
purpose of this work session is to provide draft changes to certain FLU designations to the 
Planning Commission and receive feedback on same, with the end goal of adopting these changes 
into an amended Comprehensive Plan. 
BACKGROUND  
 
The Pueblo Regional Comprehensive Plan was adopted September 2022 as a joint effort between 
the City of Pueblo, Pueblo County, and the Pueblo West Metropolitan District, and serves as the 
guiding long-range planning document for both the City and County planning departments and their 
respective board and commissions.  
 
Section 3 of the Comprehensive Plan includes the Future Land Use Plan and a series of Future 
Land Use maps. Per the Comprehensive Plan, ”the Future Land Use Plan depicts a shared vision 
for the physical growth of the Pueblo County region.” FLU designations determine the future land 
uses, density, and availability of public services in any given area and provide guidance for 
rezonings, special use permits, subdivisions, and other development actions.  
 
However, there are some areas within the jurisdiction of the County where the FLU designation is 
not necessarily consistent with existing land-use or development patterns. This can have the effect 
that staff is unable to support a rezoning or other development action which may be consistent with 
the land uses of neighboring properties and would otherwise be supportable, but is inconsistent 
with the FLU designation and as such not supportable. An incongruous FLU designation can also 
negatively affect property owners who may need to rezone in order to bring a noncompliant or 
nonconforming property into compliance, and the FLU designation prevents them from gaining a 
recommendation of approval from County staff.

These areas will be outlined in detail below. For each area, the section will include the current FLU 
designation, staff’s reasoning for why the FLU designation is incorrect, and staff’s recommended 
new FLU designation. 
 
I-25 Corridor north of Pueblo 
 
The area adjacent to Interstate 25 north of the City of Pueblo, roughly to Exit 106/Porter Draw, is 
designated Rural Ranch (RR) by the Comprehensive Plan. While some agricultural and residential 
uses do exist within this corridor, there are also a wide variety of industrial uses which serve the 
Pueblo area as well as travelers on the interstate, and rely on easy interstate access as a core 
tenet of their business model. These uses include automotive & heavy truck repair and sales, 
contractor’s yards, industrial sales and services, self-storage, and waste transfer sites. Zoning in 
this area also varies, and is a mix of agricultural (A1, A2, and A3), industrial (LI and HI), and 
commercial (CC).  
 
Staff has received multiple inquiries regarding the establishment or expansion of industrial 
operations in this corridor, including on vacant land currently zoned agriculturally. Rezonings from 
agricultural to industrial are not supported by the current RR designation, even when adjacent to 
existing industrial zoning. As such, staff is proposing changing the designation to Transportation-
Dependent Industry (TDI). Transportation-dependent industry is intended for areas with highway 
and/or and rail access, which is the case here, and would allow the predominantly industrial and 
interstate-based development patterns in this corridor to continue.  
 
A map of the area can be found in Exhibit 1.  
 
Commercial parcels near the I-25/CO-165 interchange 
 
Major highway interchanges often see commercial land-use patterns consisting of business 
intended to serve highway travelers, and the interstate exit for Colorado City/Rye is a prime 
example of this. The area is already developed with gas stations, restaurants, and a campground, 
and there are at least 12 additional vacant lots zoned Community Commercial or Mixed-Use 
Commercial which would enable a continuation of this development pattern. However, this area is 
designated a mix of Rural Ranch (RR) and Rural Neighborhoods (RN) which are not consistent 
with the predominantly commercial growth of this area. As such, staff believes a designation of 
Commercial Mixed-Use (CMU) would be more appropriate given the existing land use context 
and projected future growth at this interchange. 
 
A map of the area can be found in Exhibit 2.  
 
North Avondale 
 
The North Avondale area consists of small platted lots, generally 2 acres or less in size, that are 
generally used and zoned for residential purposes or light commercial. The existing Production 
Agriculture (PA) designation prescribes a maximum density of 1 dwelling per 35 acres, and does 
not contemplate the mix of land uses which one typically associates with a denser rural community 
center, including supporting commercial such as retail and professional services.  
 
In this area, staff believes a designation of Rural Communities (RC) would be more appropriate to 
reflect the existing land uses and density and better support future growth of a mix of supporting 
uses.

Northwestern Salt Creek 
 
Portions of the northwestern Salt Creek area adjacent to the existing rail line are unusual in terms 
of land use and zoning, as properties zoned for heavy industry directly abut single-family 
residential. Some properties in this area used for light industry are located on properties 
designated Urban Neighborhoods, which a designation which only contemplates residential uses 
and very light, small-scale commercial. Some of these properties are already zoned for industrial 
purposes, while others are not but are developed with industrial uses which reflect a generally 
industrial development pattern. 
 
A Light Industrial (LI) designation for these properties will allow them to come into compliance 
and rezone if necessary, and provide more of a buffer zone between legacy residences and the 
truly heavy industrial uses west of the railroad. 
 
A map of the area can be found in Exhibit 4.  
 
Federal and state lands 
 
Large portions of far southwestern Pueblo County are owned by federal and state governments, 
generally as state or national forest or other public lands. The Comprehensive Plan does 
contemplate public lands as its own FLU designation, State and Federal Lands (SFL). While most 
of these public lands do reflect the correct designation, staff identified some parcels which reflect a 
designation more appropriate for land in private ownership. 
 
The SFL designation should be the only designation applied to lands owned and managed by state 
or federal agencies, so this change would clean up the FLU map and ensure state and federal 
lands are designated appropriately. 
 
A map of the area can be found in Exhibit 5.  
 
Private land west of Rye 
 
Staff has identified a number of parcels located west of the town of Rye, which are designated as 
Parks and Open Space (POS). The POS designation is intended for natural areas that have been 
preserved for active and passive recreational purposes, generally by governmental agencies, but 
these are developable or developed lots in private ownership and not contemplated for park and 
recreation use.  
 
Staff believes the Rural Neighborhoods (RN) designation is more appropriate for the smaller 
parcels between Linger Longer Ln and Zorn Rd, and Rural Ranch (RR) is more appropriate for the 
larger parcels west of Woodland Acres. 
 
A map of the area can be found in Exhibits 6 and 7.  
 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
The purpose of this work session is to provide the Planning Commission with draft changes to 
certain Future Land Use designations, as well as context as to why these standards are being 
proposed. Staff would like receive direction as to whether these standards should be proposed in a 
formal Comprehensive Plan Amendment for Planning Commission approval.
