Plan Vision 
In 2050, Rifle seeks to be a community with a high quality of life, efficient and sustainable 
infrastructure, affordability and housing choices, a diverse and resilient economy, and 
exceptional access to parks, trails, recreation, and community amenities. Rifle will grow in a 
manner that strengthens neighborhoods, supports local businesses and workforce 
opportunities, delivers on public investments, and preserves the qualities that make Rifle a 
strong and special community.  
The City of Rifle Planning Commission created the above vision statement for the 
Comprehensive Plan. The purpose of the Comprehensive Plan Update is to identify the 
necessary steps to implement this vision.

Planning for Growth 
This 2026 Comprehensive Plan updates the City’s growth assumptions to reflect current 
population, housing, infrastructure, and market conditions. Recent housing analysis indicates 
that Rifle will continue to experience significant housing demand over the next 20 years, with 
projected need ranging from approximately 1,800 to 2,200 additional housing units depending 
on the growth scenario. Rifle has experienced many booms and busts over its history, which 
makes it difficult to predict the pace of growth. Regardless of growth rates, the Comprehensive 
Plan seeks to create the kind of community that Rifle’s residents desire.  
In order to ensure that growth is a benefit to the community, the Comprehensive Plan utilizes a 
“Smart Growth” approach that encourages development on infill properties instead of sprawl on 
the fringes of the community. A critical direction of the reconfirmed Comprehensive Plan is to 
direct growth toward areas with cost-effective infrastructure and services while supporting 
housing production, long-term fiscal sustainability, transportation connectivity, and efficient use 
of public infrastructure.

What is a Comprehensive Plan? 
Every week, City elected officials, appointed officials, staff, community partners, and private 
property owners make decisions that influence Rifle's built environment, including roads, 
utilities, parks, housing, transportation systems, economic development, and community 
facilities. Individually, the decisions may not seem related. Collectively, they shape Rifle's 
growth patterns, housing opportunities, transportation network, economic vitality, infrastructure 
needs, and overall quality of life.  
The purpose of the Comprehensive Plan is to provide long-range policy direction so that 
individual decisions, public investments, development approvals, and community initiatives work 
together to advance Rifle's vision for the future. Comprehensive planning protects public health, 
safety and welfare, and safeguards property rights and values. It provides predictability, 
promotes economic development, and preserves quality of life. 
The Comprehensive Plan answers questions like: 
• How can the City provide infrastructure, housing, transportation, parks, and public 
services in a fiscally responsible and sustainable manner?  
• Where should future growth occur, and how can development be directed to support 
community goals, infrastructure efficiency, housing needs, and preservation of valued 
community assets?  
• How can new development contribute to housing affordability, neighborhood quality, 
economic vitality, recreation opportunities, and community character?  
• How can Rifle's transportation network safely and efficiently serve people traveling by 
walking, biking, driving, transit, and other modes?  
• How can Rifle enable housing opportunities for current and future residents while 
supporting economic competitiveness and workforce retention? 
• How can Rifle strengthen connections between recreation assets, neighborhoods, 
schools, downtown, and employment centers? 
 
The Comprehensive Plan is a long-range policy document that establishes a vision for Rifle over 
approximately the next twenty years while providing guidance for near-term decision-making 
and implementation. Some elements of the Comprehensive Plan may seem unrealistic at first, 
but consider the change that has taken place in the last 20 years. With effective visioning and 
action steps, significant progress towards Rifle’s goals can be made over time. 
Using the Comprehensive Plan 
The Comprehensive Plan serves as the primary policy guide for land use decisions, public 
investments, annexations, development review, capital improvements, and future updates to the 
City's land use regulations. However, If circumstances have changed, or a Comprehensive Plan 
land use designation was not well thought out for an individual property, Planning Commission

6 
 
or City Council may modify the Comprehensive Plan when making a decision about an 
individual development proposal. The reasoning behind modifying the Comprehensive Plan 
should be clearly stated in the development approvals. However, larger variations from the 
intent of the Comprehensive Plan and the direction provided by the public should not be made 
without a wide public process. 
The Comprehensive Plan establishes the community's vision and policy direction. Future 
updates to the land use code, development regulations, and infrastructure planning efforts 
should be guided by the goals and policies contained in this document. 
Updating the Comprehensive Plan 
The Comprehensive Plan should be periodically reviewed by the Planning Commission and City 
Council to ensure that it reflects current conditions, community priorities, adopted plans, and 
implementation progress.  Minor updates to specific areas can occur as needed. Major 
amendments to the Comprehensive Plan should include appropriate public engagement, 
technical analysis, and coordination with related planning efforts to ensure consistency with the 
community's long-term vision.  The Plan was created and updated through a comprehensive 
analysis with public input; major amendments should occur the same way.

Population 
Rifle's population increased from approximately 9,650 residents in the 2015–2019 American 
Community Survey to approximately 10,570 residents in the 2020–2024 American Community 
Survey, representing growth of approximately 9.5%. This growth reflects Rifle's continued role 
as a regional population, employment, and service center within Garfield County. Population 
growth has implications for future housing demand, transportation infrastructure, utility systems, 
public facilities, parks and recreation amenities, and municipal services. 
Age and Household Characteristics 
Rifle remains a comparatively young community. The median age increased only slightly from 
approximately 31 years to 32 years between ACS periods. Approximately 28%of residents are 
under age 18, while approximately 10 percent are age 65 or older. Nearly 44% of households 
include children under age 18. These characteristics indicate that Rifle continues to be home to

many families. The percentage of the population that is over 65 years old dropped from 10.6% 
to 9.6%. Rifle's average household size remains larger than both state and national averages, 
reflecting the community's family-oriented demographic profile. However, Rifle’s average 
household size is slightly smaller than Garfield County’s. 
Diversity and Language 
Rifle has become increasingly diverse since adoption of the previous Comprehensive Plan. The 
percentage of residents identifying as Hispanic or Latino increased substantially between 2019 
and 2024 and now represents nearly half of the community's population. The percentage of 
residents speaking a language other than English at home also increased during this period. 
Spanish remains the most commonly spoken language other than English. These trends 
contribute to Rifle's culture and highlight the importance of ensuring that public information, 
engagement efforts, and community services remain accessible to Spanish speakers. 
Education 
Educational attainment levels have remained relatively stable since adoption of the previous 
Comprehensive Plan. Approximately 86% of residents age 25 and older have completed high 
school, while approximately 18% have obtained a bachelor's degree or higher. Compared to 
Colorado as a whole, Rifle continues to have lower rates of bachelor's degree attainment and 
higher concentrations of residents employed in skilled trades, construction, transportation, 
production, and service occupations. These characteristics reflect the community's workforce 
profile and economic structure. 
Income and Economic Characteristics 
Household incomes increased significantly between 2019 and 2024. Median household income 
increased from approximately $62,000 to more than $80,000, while median family income and 
per-capita income also increased. At the same time, employment patterns suggest a diverse 
local economy with significant concentrations in healthcare and social assistance, education, 
retail trade, construction, service occupations, transportation and logistics, and public 
administration. 
Housing Characteristics 
Rifle's housing inventory continued to expand between 2019 and 2024, with increases in both 
housing units and occupied households. Owner-occupied housing remains the dominant tenure 
type; however, renter households continue to represent a substantial share of the community's 
housing stock. Despite increases in household income, housing affordability remains an 
important consideration. Rising home values, rental costs, construction costs, and financing 
costs have increased the cost of housing throughout western Colorado, including Rifle. The 
relationship between housing supply, housing diversity, and affordability will remain an 
important consideration as Rifle plans for future growth.

Employment and Commuting 
Rifle remains closely connected to the regional economy. Many residents commute to 
employment centers throughout Garfield County and surrounding communities, while employers

Broadband and Connectivity 
Access to broadband internet has increased since adoption of the previous Comprehensive

Reliable broadband service has become increasingly important for education, employment, 
business activity, healthcare access, and remote work opportunities. Continued investment in 
digital connectivity will remain important to Rifle's long-term economic competitiveness and 
quality of life.

Chapter 2: Land Use and Growth 
Framework  
Draft Note: This chapter will be updated to incorporate findings and recommendations from the 
Strategic Growth Element, Water Supply Element, and Land Use Code Audit. Future revisions 
will include refined Place Type (or Future Land Use District) descriptions, additional analysis of 
infill and redevelopment opportunities, and updates to growth management, land use, and 
implementation policies to ensure consistency with Rifle's long-term growth strategy. 
 
Land use decisions shape how Rifle grows, how efficiently the City provides infrastructure and 
public services, and how residents experience their neighborhoods and community. The 
location, intensity, and design of future development will influence all aspects of community 
development, including housing opportunities, transportation choices, economic development, 
public safety, fiscal sustainability, access to parks and schools, and quality of life. 
Rifle’s physical setting creates both opportunities and constraints for growth. The Colorado 
River, Interstate 70, surrounding topography, public lands, existing infrastructure systems, and 
the location of existing neighborhoods affect where development can occur and how efficiently 
new development can be served by infrastructure. The City recognizes that growth decisions 
must also account for the long-term costs of constructing, maintaining, repairing, and replacing 
public infrastructure. 
The City’s land use and growth framework consists of two complementary tools. The Tiered 
Growth System identifies where urban growth should be prioritized based on infrastructure, 
public services, access, development suitability, and fiscal considerations. The Future Land Use 
Map establishes the intended future land use, development intensity, and character of different 
areas. Together, these tools guide annexation, infrastructure investment, zoning, subdivision, 
development review, and neighborhood and subarea planning. 
Rifle’s approach to growth and development can be summarized in the following four Growth 
Principles:  
Growth Principles  
1. Prevent Leapfrog Development Patterns Through a Tiered Growth System 
2. Growth Pays its Own Way 
3. Create High Quality of Life Neighborhoods 
4. Strategic Investment to Achieve Community Goals 
Planning Context: Land Use and Growth 
The 2019 Comprehensive Plan established several principles intended to guide Rifle’s future 
growth: 
Page 16 of 314
 
14 
 
• Prevent leapfrog development through a Tiered Growth System. 
• Ensure that growth contributes its fair share toward infrastructure and public services. 
• Create neighborhoods that support a high quality of life. 
• Use strategic investments and implementation tools to advance community goals. 
These principles remain the foundation of Rifle’s land use and growth strategy. They recognize 
that the location and form of development are closely linked to infrastructure costs, 
transportation connectivity, housing opportunities, public service delivery, neighborhood quality, 
and the City’s long-term fiscal health. 
Tiered Growth System (Growth Principle #1) 
Rifle will direct future growth toward areas that can be efficiently served by public infrastructure, 
public services, transportation networks, parks, schools, and community facilities. The Tiered 
Growth System is intended to prevent leapfrog development and promote fiscally responsible 
growth patterns. 
Under this framework, growth is prioritized in locations that can support housing and economic 
development while maximizing existing infrastructure investments. Urban development is 
discouraged in places where infrastructure cannot be provided efficiently or where rural, 
agricultural, open space, resource, or other nonurban lands should be preserved. 
Directing growth toward areas with existing infrastructure capacity, or where infrastructure can 
be provided efficiently, can: 
• Reduce long-term infrastructure costs 
• Improve transportation and neighborhood connectivity 
• Support housing production and housing choice 
• Strengthen established neighborhoods and commercial areas 
• Promote efficient public service delivery 
• Reduce pressure for premature infrastructure extensions 
• Support the City’s long-term fiscal sustainability 
 
The Tiered Growth System uses three categories: 
• Tier 1: Priority Growth Areas 
• Tier 2: Secondary Growth Areas 
• Tier 3: Rural Preservation Reserve 
Growth should generally be directed toward Tier 1 areas. Tier 2 areas may be considered when 
identified constraints can be adequately addressed. Tier 3 areas are intended to remain 
predominantly rural and are not anticipated to urbanize during the planning horizon of this 
Comprehensive Plan.

Tier 1: Priority Growth Areas 
Tier 1 areas represent the locations most appropriate for growth during the planning horizon of 
this Comprehensive Plan. These areas can generally be served by existing infrastructure or by 
infrastructure that can be extended efficiently and cost-effectively. 
Tier 1 areas should receive the highest priority for residential, commercial, mixed-use, industrial, 
civic, and employment-generating development, consistent with the Future Land Use Map and 
applicable place types. 
Tier 1 boundaries are based on the following considerations: 
1. The area is within the City or is eligible for annexation. 
2. The area is adjacent to established neighborhoods or existing development. 
3. The area has, or can provide, adequate pedestrian and vehicular access. 
4. The area is served by existing water, wastewater, transportation, and other 
infrastructure, or the necessary infrastructure can realistically be funded and 
constructed. 
5. The area is near or contains schools, parks, civic destinations, businesses, employment 
areas, or other community facilities. 
6. The area has a size, shape, location, and development pattern conducive to high-quality 
neighborhood or employment-area development. 
7. Development would efficiently use existing public infrastructure and contribute to the 
City’s long-term fiscal sustainability. 
8. The area can support Rifle’s housing and economic development goals through land 
uses and development intensities appropriate to its location. 
Development within Tier 1 remains responsible for its proportionate share of required 
infrastructure and public service costs. Developers are generally responsible for constructing 
infrastructure required to serve a project and for dedicating applicable public infrastructure to the 
City in accordance with City standards. 
In some cases, strategic City participation in infrastructure within Tier 1 may help make priority 
development feasible and reduce pressure to develop less efficient locations. Any public 
participation should be evaluated based on the project’s community benefit, consistency with 
this Comprehensive Plan, fiscal implications, and ability to advance other City goals. 
Tier 2: Secondary Growth Areas 
Tier 2 areas contain properties that face significant challenges in meeting the Tier 1 criteria. 
These areas may require major infrastructure improvements or extensions, have limited 
transportation access, lack adequate connectivity, or present development patterns that would 
be costly to serve or maintain. Some Tier 2 areas may also face environmental, topographic, 
ownership, public service, or neighborhood design constraints. 
Page 18 of 314
 
16 
 
Tier 2 areas should not be considered primary growth locations unless their identified 
constraints can be adequately addressed. Relevant constraints and infrastructure needs 
associated with specific Tier 2 areas should also be considered through neighborhood and 
subarea planning. 
Tier Change Application Process 
In some cases, a Tier 2 property may be able to resolve the issues that led to its designation 
and become appropriate for Tier 1. In other cases, resolving those issues may not be feasible 
during the planning horizon of this Comprehensive Plan. Each proposed tier change should 
therefore be evaluated on its individual merits. 
A property owner may request that property be changed from Tier 2 to Tier 1. The City should 
maintain a formal process for evaluating such requests. 
Depending on the location, scale, and potential impacts of the proposed change, the City may 
require a fiscal impact analysis, infrastructure capacity assessment, transportation analysis, 
master plan, or other supporting information. Review should occur before, or in coordination 
with, an annexation, rezoning, subdivision, or major development application, as appropriate. 
A request to move property from Tier 2 to Tier 1 should demonstrate: 
• Consistency with the Comprehensive Plan and applicable neighborhood or subarea

• Availability of adequate infrastructure capacity.

2025 Housing Study & Action Plan 
The 2025 Housing Study and Action Plan provides a comprehensive assessment of Rifle's 
housing market, evaluates future housing needs, and identifies strategies to expand housing 
opportunities across a range of income levels. The study found that Rifle's housing supply has

housing options for many residents. While Rifle remains more affordable than some neighboring 
communities, housing costs have increased significantly over the past decade, and many 
households face challenges accessing attainable ownership and rental opportunities.

Proposition 123 
In 2022, Colorado state voters approved Proposition 123 to create the State Affordable Housing 
Fund, a dedicated revenue stream to direct one-tenth of one percent (0.1%) of existing state 
income tax revenue toward affordable housing. The Fund is split between two state agencies: 
40% flows to the Department of Local Affairs (DOLA) for homeownership, homelessness, and 
local planning capacity programs, and 60% is managed by the Office of Economic Development 
and International Trade (OEDIT) through the Colorado Housing and Finance Authority (CHFA) 
for land banking, equity investment, and concessionary debt.  
To access these funds, local governments are required to file an affordable housing 
commitment to increase the total number of affordable housing units by a certain number of 
units, most commonly a percentage (3%) of the municipality’s existing affordable housing units, 
over a specified time period (typically 1-3 years).  
In 2023, Rifle opted into Proposition 123 and set a 3-year 3% (9% total) increase in affordable 
housing units commitment goal of 65 new units by 2026. With the completion of the LIHTC 
affordable housing apartment development near the movie theater in 2026, the City has met this 
goal.  
Key Findings 
1. Housing Affordability Remains the Community's Primary Concern 
Housing affordability is consistently identified as Rifle's most significant challenge. Rising 
housing costs, limited inventory, and low vacancy rates are constraining housing opportunities, 
while the scarcity of attainable housing is a major barrier to workforce recruitment and business 
expansion. Community survey results reinforce these findings: 85% of respondents identified 
affordability and cost of living as a major challenge, and 74% said housing affordability for the 
local workforce should be a top consideration when permitting new housing. 
2. Housing Costs Influence Workforce and Commuting Patterns 
Housing and employment are closely connected throughout the Colorado River Valley. The 
CRVEDP Strategy found that housing availability and affordability are major barriers to 
workforce recruitment and retention, while commuting patterns show that many workers do not 
live in the same communities where they work; as of 2023, more than 4,000 Rifle residents 
commuted outside the city for work, while over 2,000 workers commuted into Rifle and only 17% 
of Rifle residents worked within city limits. Rifle’s housing challenges are part of a larger

regional housing ecosystem and are closely related to both regional employment and regional 
transportation systems. 
3. Housing Demand Will Continue to Increase 
The Housing Study estimates that Rifle could add 5,202 to 6,346 residents and require 
approximately 1,800 to 2,200 additional housing units by 2044. Community survey results 
indicate residents generally recognize the need for growth, with 47% supporting growth that 
accommodates housing and jobs, while only 15% support maintaining Rifle as it exists today

4. Housing Choice Is Limited 
Rifle's housing stock remains dominated by single-family homes and there is a need for

dwelling units, and other missing-middle housing options. Households earning 50% to 80% of

5. Affordability and Housing Costs Matter to Young People  
Rifle High School students are already aware of Rifle’s housing and cost-of-living challenges.

or expensive housing would make them want to move away, as would the high cost of living. In 
open-ended responses, more than 30 students mentioned lowering the cost of living or 
increasing housing affordability as the one thing they’d like to change about Rifle. It is notable 
that high school students, who typically do not bear the burden of paying housing costs, see 
housing affordability as such a pressing concern.  
6. Existing Housing Partnerships Provide Momentum 
Recent efforts including the Housing Study and Action Plan, Proposition 123 participation, 
affordable housing development, and partnerships with organizations such as Habitat for 
Humanity demonstrate that Rifle has already begun addressing housing needs. Through 
Proposition 123, the City committed to creating 65 affordable housing units by 2026 and has 
already met that goal.

Chapter 4: Transportation & Mobility  
Transportation plays a critical role in supporting Rifle's quality of life, economic development, 
housing opportunities, public safety, and long-term growth. The city's transportation network 
must safely and efficiently serve residents, businesses, visitors, and emergency services while 
providing transportation choices for people of all ages and abilities. 
Rifle's transportation system is influenced by the Colorado River, Interstate 70, surrounding 
topography, and a limited number of major north-south corridors. These physical constraints 
influence how people move throughout the community and create unique challenges and 
opportunities for future transportation planning. 
Several transportation planning efforts continue to inform transportation decisions in Rifle, 
including the Transportation Master Plan, Gateway Master Plan, Highway 13 Access Control 
Plan, Safe Streets and Roads for All Plan, and various pedestrian, bicycle, and trail planning 
initiatives. 
Planning Context: Transportation 
Transportation Master Plan 
The 2003 Transportation Master Plan established the long-term framework for Rifle's 
transportation network and identified roadway, connectivity, and mobility improvements intended 
to accommodate future growth. 
Since adoption of the Transportation Master Plan, several conditions have changed 
significantly: 
• Construction costs have increased substantially. 
• Infrastructure maintenance and replacement costs have become a greater financial 
consideration. 
• Community growth patterns have become more focused on infill and efficient use of 
existing infrastructure. 
• Community priorities have expanded to include transportation safety, wlkability, bicycle 
access, recreation connectivity, and multimodal transportation options. 
As a result, several roadway projects identified in the original Transportation Master Plan are no 
longer considered feasible or necessary, including: 
• The East Bypass connection between County Road 291 and Highway 6. 
• Graham Mesa connections to the proposed East Bypass. 
• Birch Avenue extension to Highway 6. 
These projects face significant right-of-way, topographic, environmental, and financial 
constraints and are not recommended during the planning horizon of this Comprehensive Plan.

Chapter 5: Infrastructure and Utilities 
Utility System Overview 
The City of Rifle owns and operates municipal water and wastewater systems that provide 
essential services to residents, businesses, and institutions throughout the community. Over the 
past decade, the City has made significant investments in treatment facilities, water storage 
infrastructure, transmission lines, and utility system improvements to support existing customers 
and accommodate future growth. These investments have positioned Rifle to accommodate 
additional development in many areas of the community while maintaining reliable service and 
protecting public health. At the same time, continued growth, aging infrastructure, changing 
regulatory requirements, and long-term operational costs require careful planning to ensure 
utility systems remain financially sustainable and capable of serving future generations. 
Consistent with the City's Strategic Plan, maintaining and improving core infrastructure remains 
a priority for the City Council and an essential component of Rifle's long-term growth strategy. 
Findings: Utility System 
Existing Infrastructure Can Support Significant Growth 
Over the past two decades, the City has invested significantly in water treatment, wastewater 
treatment, storage facilities, transmission infrastructure, and distribution system improvements. 
As a result, many Tier 1 Growth Areas can be served by existing utility systems with limited 
additional public investment. 
Existing utility service areas generally correspond with the Tier 1 Growth Areas identified in the 
Comprehensive Plan, including Downtown, the Railroad Avenue corridor, South Rifle, portions 
of the Colorado River corridor, Graham Mesa, and other established growth areas. 
Growth Should Support Long-Term Utility Sustainability 
The City has invested substantially in utility infrastructure over time. Future growth should help 
support the long-term operation, maintenance, repair, and replacement of these systems. 
Development that utilizes existing infrastructure is generally more cost-effective for both the City 
and taxpayers than extending utilities into areas requiring significant new infrastructure 
investment. This is a major purpose of the City’s Tiered Growth System: to align future 
development with infrastructure realities and promote fiscally responsible growth patterns. 
Infrastructure Capacity Varies Across the Community 
Utility capacity varies across the community due to topography, pressure zones, storage 
requirements, and infrastructure availability. Several areas identified for future growth may 
require additional infrastructure investments to fully realize their development potential. Future 
growth on Graham Mesa may require evaluation of storage and transmission capacity

Asset Management and System Resilience Are Increasingly Important

investments should balance growth needs with long-term system performance and lifecycle 
costs. 
Continued Infrastructure Investment Remains a Community Priority 
Utility infrastructure represents one of the City's largest public investments and one of its most 
important responsibilities. Maintaining high-quality utility services was reinforced in the City's 
Strategic Plan and remains central to Rifle's success as a community.

Economic Development Strategy for the Colorado River Valley Economic 
Development Partnership (CRVEDP) 
The 2025 Colorado River Valley Economic Development Partnership (CRVEDP) Economic 
Development Strategy provides a long-range roadmap for strengthening and diversifying the 
economy of Western Garfield County. The strategy was developed in response to long-term 
changes in the energy industry, workforce challenges, housing constraints, and the need to 
build greater economic resilience while maintaining the area's economic strengths and 
community character. 
Western Garfield County includes the communities of Parachute, Battlement Mesa, New Castle, 
Rifle, and Silt, while Glenwood Springs and Carbondale are included in Eastern Garfield 
County. In this strategy, Rifle is identified as the primary commercial and employment hub of 
Western Garfield and a key location for future business growth, workforce development, 
housing investment, and industry diversification. 
Key Notes: 
• Since 2010, Rifle has accounted for 43% of all new business filings in Western Garfield 
County and has experienced the fastest growth in new business filings in the entire 
county since 2010. 
• Oil and gas remain central to Western Garfield’s economy but have declined sharply 
from their peak levels in the mid-2000s. Drilling permits in the region fell from nearly 
4,000 in 2008 to about 135 in recent years, reflecting a long-term contraction in activity. 
Despite this decline, oil and gas is still a primary economic driver in the region. The 
share of Garfield County GDP tied to oil and gas has fallen from more than 30 percent in 
2009 to about 16.3 percent in 2023.

Key Findings: Economic Development 
A. Rifle is a regional hub 
Rifle is situated at the center of the Middle Colorado River Valley region, with a trade area 
population of 34,7651 people in 2023 in Western Garfield County. Rifle generated approximately 
$16.2 million in sales tax revenue in 2024 and experienced the largest increase in new business 
filings in Western Garfield, reflecting its role as the region’s primary commercial and service 
center. This demonstrates Rifle’s position as a burgeoning regional center between the more 
established regional centers of Glenwood Springs and Grand Junction. Future growth in the 
Middle Colorado River Valley will continue to enhance Rifle’s status as a regional center for 
retail, services, and entertainment.  
B. Rifle’s economy, formerly based in oil and gas, is diversifying 
While oil and gas's share of Garfield County GDP declined from more than 30% in 2009 to 
16.3% in 2023, it remains a major economic driver, supporting 14.7% of jobs and generating 
28.5% of Western Garfield's GDP. Rifle's largest employment sectors are now concentrated in 
education, health care and social assistance, retail trade, and construction2.  
C. Many people don’t live and work in the same community 
Commuting is a large part of life in the Colorado River Valley. As of 2023, over 2,000 people 
commute in to work in Rifle, many from communities down-valley, while over 4,000 Rifle 
residents commute out of Rifle, often to jobs up-valley; only 17% of Rifle residents (848 people) 
were employed within Rifle city limits.  
D. Housing costs are a limiting factor for job creation and economic 
development  
Housing availability and affordability have emerged as one of the most significant barriers to 
workforce recruitment, retention, and business expansion, and lead to such intense commuting 
dynamics. Western Garfield businesses identified housing as the single greatest barrier to hiring 
and workforce recruitment, while 74% of Rifle community survey respondents said housing 
affordability for the local workforce should be a top factor in future housing decisions.
