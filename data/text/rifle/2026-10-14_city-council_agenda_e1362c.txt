Background: 
Data centers can provide substantial private investment, construction activity, property tax base, franchise 
revenue potential, and fiber/telecommunications infrastructure. They can also create unusual public impacts 
because they can require high electric capacity, cooling water, backup power systems, constant mechanical 
operation, major utility upgrades, intensive security, and specialized emergency response. 
A dedicated CUP structure gives the City a clearer basis to require studies, impose conditions, protect public 
infrastructure and nearby land uses, and deny applications that do not demonstrate net public benefit.

Define the use 
Create a specific “Data Center” definition and also preserve a broader “Bulk Resource Consumer” category for 
high-impact users that exceed defined electric, water, waste, or pollution thresholds. A broader umbrella may 
be helpful because future high-resource uses may not call themselves data centers but may create similar 
infrastructure, utility, and nuisance issues. 
Proposed definition: 
“Bulk Resource Consumer” means a commercial, industrial, or institutional use that, based on projected or 
contracted demand, is expected to consume more than a defined threshold of electric capacity, water, or other 
municipal or environmental resources; requires substantial utility or grid interconnection upgrades; or produces 
waste, heat, air emissions, noise, or discharges requiring state or federal permit review.  
A Data Center is a subset of Bulk Resource Consumer when the primary use is the housing of information 
technology or telecommunications equipment used to process, transfer, or store digital information, including 
associated cooling, mechanical, security, office, backup power, and utility infrastructure.”  
Require studies and analyses as part of a complete application  
The following studies are recommended to be required before an application is deemed complete. This ensures 
the Planning Commission and City Council are not asked to decide a project before the basic operational 
impacts are understood.

Required study Description and expected deliverable Policy basis 
Electrical / Power Supply Analysis 
Documents anticipated peak and average electrical demand in 
MW, interconnection needs, substation/switchgear improvements, 
backup power, redundancy, and written utility confirmation that 
service can be provided without reducing service to existing users 
or shifting system costs to residents and small businesses. 
Hayden requires electric utility capacity 
verification and an energy supply plan; 
Weld requires a utility will-serve letter. 
Electric and Gas Rate Study 
Identifies likely rate class, tariff treatment, franchise fee 
implications, cost-recovery structure, utility upgrades, standby 
service, early exit penalties, and whether project-related costs 
could be socialized to other ratepayers. If no gas service is 
proposed, the study should focus on electric rates and franchise 
impacts. 
Supports Council review of fiscal benefit 
and ratepayer protection. 
Water Supply & Cooling Plan 
Identifies source and legal availability of water, initial fill, annual 
and peak demand, drought-stage limitations, fire suppression 
needs, cooling technology, blowdown/wastewater, discharge 
method, and whether closed-loop, dry, recycled, or other low-
water systems are proposed. 
Logan and Hayden both require 
detailed water/cooling documentation; 
Hayden favors closed-loop or recycled 
systems. 
Ldn Noise Study 
Establishes baseline conditions and evaluates day-night average 
sound levels, continuous mechanical noise, tonal noise, low-
frequency noise, backup generator testing, and modeled impacts 
at property lines and sensitive receptors. Rifle should consider 
both dBA and dBC metrics. 
Weld adds a 65 dB(C) property-
boundary limit for data centers; peer 
jurisdictions regulate generator testing. 
Thermal Impact & Mitigation 
Analysis 
Identifies sources of waste heat and evaluates potential off-site 
heat discharge, thermal plumes, localized heat island effects, and 
mitigation such as equipment orientation, screening, waste heat 
reuse, or mechanical design changes. 
Hayden requires a thermal impact 
mitigation plan for data centers. 
Fiscal & Municipal Services Impact 
Analysis 
Quantifies expected City revenue, one-time versus recurring 
revenue, property tax distribution, franchise revenue, staff review 
cost, road maintenance, emergency response, utility impacts, and 
capital facility impacts. 
Logan requires applicants to address 
effects on public services and capital 
facilities. 
Economic Impact Analysis 
Distinguishes direct jobs, construction activity, permanent FTE, 
taxable investment, local procurement, induced/indirect effects, 
supplier attraction, and potential displacement or opportunity 
costs. 
Needed because data centers often 
have high capital investment but fewer 
permanent jobs per dollar than 
traditional employment uses. 
Housing Impact Analysis 
Evaluates construction-phase workforce housing, lodging, 
RV/campground pressure, rental market effects, long-term 
employee housing needs, and mitigation for short-term and long-
term impacts. 
Important for Rifle given regional 
housing constraints and a potentially 
large temporary construction workforce.

Environmental Waste Study 
(Planning Commission Proposed) 
The Environmental Waste Study should identify all solid, liquid, 
hazardous, universal, special, construction, operational, and 
decommissioning waste streams that may be generated by a Bulk 
Resource Consumer. For a data center or similar high-resource 
facility, this may include electronic waste, batteries, backup power 
system components, diesel or fuel-related materials, oils, 
coolants, refrigerants, glycol, water treatment chemicals, cleaning 
solvents, cooling system blowdown, wastewater discharge, 
construction waste, contaminated soil, and waste generated 
during equipment replacement or decommissioning. 
The purpose of the study is to ensure that waste is properly 
identified, stored, transported, recycled, treated, discharged, or 
disposed of without creating risk to public health, City utilities, 
surrounding properties, stormwater systems, wastewater systems, 
or emergency responders. 
 
The Environmental Waste Study 
supports the City’s authority to evaluate 
whether a proposed Bulk Resource 
Consumer protects public health, 
safety, welfare, municipal infrastructure, 
emergency services, stormwater 
systems, and wastewater systems. 
Colorado hazardous waste regulations 
apply to persons who generate 
hazardous waste and require 
compliance with generator 
requirements based on the type and

ability to verify that state and federal 
waste obligations have been

Air Quality Study 
(Planning Commission Proposed) 
The Air Quality Study should identify and evaluate all potential air

The Air Quality Study is justified

Recommended CUP conditions and performance standards  
Condition area Recommended approach 
Utility capacity and cost shifting 
Require written confirmation from the serving electric utility and, if applicable, gas utility. 
Any project-specific upgrades, capacity extensions, standby service, or early-exit 
liabilities should be paid by the project and not shifted to residents, small businesses, or 
future City budgets. 
Water and cooling 
Require closed-loop cooling, dry cooling, recycled water, or another demonstrably 
water-conservative system unless the applicant proves an equal or lower annual and 
peak-day demand. Require water-wise landscaping with limited irrigated plantings. 
Noise 
Require baseline measurement, predictive modeling, compliance testing, and 
enforceable limits. Include dBA and dBC or other low-frequency standards because 
data center mechanical systems may produce continuous or tonal noise not fully 
captured by dBA alone.

Require a thermal impact mitigation plan and periodic monitoring if modeling shows

Lighting and visual impacts 
Require fully shielded, downward-directed fixtures; no glare or light trespass; screening

Sensitive receptors 
Evaluate setbacks from housing, educational facilities, roads, campuses, parks, medical 
facilities, and other sensitive uses. The draft 1,500-foot educational facility setback 
should be reviewed against actual sites, legal standards, parcel geometry, and 
mitigation feasibility. 
Public notification 
Require enhanced notification at least one-half mile from property boundaries and a 
public meeting after a complete application is received but before Planning Commission 
and City Council hearings. 
Emergency services 
Require Colorado River Fire Rescue review, emergency pre-plans, access design, 
funding for specialized training/equipment if needed, and annual updates to emergency 
contacts and shutoff/de-energization procedures. 
Decommissioning and ownership changes 
Require financial assurance before building permits. Any transfer of ownership should 
require notice to the City and written assumption of CUP obligations by the successor 
operator. 
Duration and amendments 
Allow Council to set CUP duration, review intervals, and expiration conditions. Any 
material increase in load, water use, backup generation, cooling method, or building 
area should require an amended CUP. 
Proposed code structure for staff drafting 
Staff should prepare a text amendment organized around the following sections. The language below is a code-
structure recommendation rather than final legal text. 
 Sec. 16— Definitions: Add Data Center and Bulk Resource Consumer definitions; clarify thresholds; 
exclude ordinary accessory server rooms below the threshold. Add Sensitive Receptors definition. 
 New Article — Bulk Resource Consumer / Data Center Standards: Add intent, applicability, CUP 
requirement, complete-application requirements, required studies, operating standards, monitoring, 
reporting, enforcement, and decommissioning. 
 Sec. 16— Schedule of Uses: Allow Bulk Resource Consumer / Data Center only as a conditional use in 
selected industrial districts and prohibit in incompatible districts. 
 Sec. 16 — Additional Requirements: Add setbacks, height limits, cooling requirements, noise and heat 
monitoring, public notification, landscaping/screening, and decommissioning bond requirements. 
 CUP Procedures: Add explicit authority to require third-party review at applicant expense and to condition, 
deny, revoke, or require amended CUP review for material operational changes.

Draft considerations: 
 Data centers and similar high-resource uses can create fiscal and economic benefits but can also create 
impacts that differ from typical light industrial development. 
 A specific definition and CUP process is needed to avoid reviewing data centers as ordinary warehouse, 
office, or light industrial uses. 
 Mandatory technical studies are appropriate because public impacts may depend on utility capacity, water 
availability, cooling technology, noise, thermal discharge, emergency response needs, housing impacts, 
and ratepayer protections. 
 The City should require applicant-funded third-party review for technical studies that exceed staff expertise 
or capacity. 
 The City should require enforceable conditions, monitoring, reporting, decommissioning security, and public 
notification before considering approval. 
 
Draft code concepts : 
 Intent: protect public infrastructure, municipal utilities, surrounding land uses, and public health, safety, and 
welfare through standards for location, development, operation, and decommissioning.  
 CUP application: require scaled site plan showing boundaries, rights-of-way, physical constraints, utility 
structures, cooling/mechanical structures, security/administrative structures, drainage, streets, access, 
pedestrian walkways, utilities, easements, owner/operator, and zoning.  
 Required studies: electrical/power, rate/franchise, water/cooling, noise, thermal, fiscal/municipal services, 
economic impact, housing impact, Construction housing mitigation plan, emergency operations, and 
decommissioning. 
 Permits and licenses: identify applicable federal, state, and utility approvals, including water rights, air quality, 
discharge, Clean Water Act, NEPA if applicable, and utility/grid interconnection.  
 Use table: consider Bulk Resource Consumer as conditional in Light Industrial and Industrial districts only.  
 Setbacks and height: 
 50-foot height limit including rooftop equipment. 
 200-foot setback from property line 
 200-foot setback from Right-of-Way 
 1,500 foot setback from existing residences and/or sensitive receptors  
 Cooling: require closed-loop or similar water-conservative system. Facility Operator shall provide the City with 
annual water use reports sighting initial fill, cooling system top off, domestic/employee use, maintenance and 
cleaning, and fire system testing. 
 Initial Fill shall use no more than 30 Acre Feet of water  
 Annual Consumption shall use no more than 1 Acre Feet of water for cooling  
 Monitoring: require noise/decibel and released-heat testing at defined distances and intervals; allow 
unannounced compliance testing. 
 Provide baseline ambient noise study at application. Provide Predictive Noise Model for all cooling, electrical, 
transformer, generator, and backup systems. dBA and dBC will be measured at property line and all 
sensitive receptors (see definition). Post-construction verification testing will take place quarterly and before 
Certificate of Occupancy is issued. Testing may be conducted unannounced if complaints occur. Corrective 
action is required within 30 days if measured noise exceeds approved limits.

 45dBA and 60dBC daytime limit at property line of sensitive receptors.

 Exterior fixtures shall be fully shielded, full cutoff, and downward-directed. 
 No direct uplight shall be permitted, except emergency lighting approved by the City. 
 Maximum property line light trespass of 0.1 foot-candle at nearby residences or sensitive receptor 
property lines. 
 Maximum property line light trespass of 0.2 foot-candle at adjacent industrial or commercial property 
lines. 
 All non-essential lighting shall use timers, dimmers, motion sensors, or adaptive controls. 
 Lighting not required for active operations, emergency access, or security shall be dimmed or turned off 
during overnight hours from at least 9pm to 4am.

 Screening: encourage berms, waterwise trees, and natural landscape separation to reduce line of sight and

How the water assumptions were calculated
A screening methodology for Council discussion. Applicant-specific engineering and water-right analysis would control during CUP review.
1 acre-foot = 325,851 gallons
1  Closed-loop data center
• Real-world anchor: Oracle reports ~10M gallons (30.7 AF) one-time 
fill for four Project Jupiter data center buildings.
• Order-of-magnitude scale: 30.7 AF × (scenario MW ÷ 2,450 MW).
• City planning ranges are then widened roughly 2–4× for building 
count, redundancy, piping, commissioning and design uncertainty.
• 660 MW example: direct proportional scale ≈ 8.3 AF → conservative 
planning band ≈ 15–30 AF initial fill.
2  Ongoing facility use
• True closed-loop cooling top-off is assumed near zero after 
initial fill.
• Employee water proxy: FTE × 20–30 gal/day × ~250 workdays 
÷ 325,851.
• Add modest allowance for maintenance, cleaning, testing and 
small system losses.
• This produces roughly <1, 2–5 and 4–10 AF/year across the 
three scenarios.
3  Dry-cooled generation
• Annual AF = MW × 8,760 hours × capacity factor × water factor ÷
325,851.
• Screening assumptions: 70–95% capacity factor and ~2–4 
gal/MWh for dry-cooled NGCC.
• Example: 600 MW × 8,760 × 95% × 4 gal/MWh ÷ 325,851 ≈ 61 
AF/year.
• Wet cooling is not modeled and would be substantially higher.
Methodology intentionally rounds upward.

INTRODUCTION TO COLORADO WATER LAW 
 
Pursuant to the Colorado Constitution, the people of the State of Colorado own all of the water 
in Colorado. With certain exceptions, land ownership does not give a landowner any right to water 
under or flowing through property.  Instead, the Colorado Constitution provides that water users can 
acquire a right to divert water for beneficial use.  That right is referred to as a “water right.”  
 
Water use in Colorado is regulated by the “prior appropriation system.” Under this legal 
doctrine, water rights are created by intent to place water to beneficial use, action to implement that 
intent (usually the location or construction of water diversion structures or the filing of a Water Court 
application), and actual beneficial use of water. For a water right to be enforceable against other water 
rights, the water right must be confirmed by a Water Court.  A Water Court expresses its holdings on 
water rights claims through a “decree.”  A water right is confirmed with and defined by certain 
characteristics or limiting factors based upon the original water appropriator’s intent.  Ownership of 
a water right merely allows the water right owner to divert and use a defined amount of water from a 
specific body of water for a specific period of time in a specific location and for a specific purpose.   
 
The priority date of a water right is an extremely important aspect of a water right. A water 
right with an earlier priority date has a higher priority to divert water from a stream or water body 
than a water right with a later priority date.  In other words, the holder of a senior priority water right 
has the right to fulfill its water needs before the holder of a junior priority water right can fulfill its 
water needs.  
 
 Prior to the Water Right Determination and Administration Act of 1969 ("Act"), the state was 
divided into approximately eighty water districts , and the rights of all parties claiming water rights  
within a district were jointly adjudicated and recorded in a single decree. The first decree within a 
district was called the “original adjudication.” All priorities in a subsequent decree or “supplemental 
adjudication” were junior to those in the original and any previous supplemental decrees. This was 
true even if a claimant initiated a diversion prior to a party whose rights had already been adjudicated. 
Thus, priority was determined by the date of the decree. Within a particular decree, priorities were 
determined on a "first in time, first in right" basis and a priority number was awarded to indicate a 
water right's seniority in a particular  drainage. The "Priority Number" awarded to a water right 
adjudicated prior to the Act is now largely meaningless because it referred to a water right's priority 
within its district and did not reflect the right’s priority within the larger river system. Since 1969, 
after passage of the Water Rights Determination and Administration Act of 1969, the priority date for 
a subsequently adjudicated water right is based on the year in which the application for the water right 
is filed with the Water Court, and within that year, the date when the water for which the right is 
approved was first “appropriated” or placed to use (the “appropriation date”). When a water right has 
an earlier priority date than another water right, the earlier priority water right is said  to be “senior” 
to the later priority water right, and the later priority water right is said to be “junior” to the earlier 
priority water right.   
 
When there is enough water available to fulfill all water rights holders’ water needs, a water 
right holder can legally receive the amount of water for which the water right is approved. Most rivers 
Page 40 of 90
 
 
Page 4 
 
and streams in Colorado – including the Colorado River, to which all the Water Rights are tributary 
– are considered to be “over appropriated.”  Over appropriation results from the Water Court 
approving water rights on a river or stream in cumulative quantities greater than the amount of water 
actually available at times. In other words, there is more demand for water in the system than there is 
supply. The supply –  demand imbalance most often arises in the months of July, August and 
September when mountain snowpack has melted and agricultural irrigation demands are high.  During 
times of water shortage, a senior water right holder can “call” junior water rights.  A call results in 
DWR officials forcing the holders of junior water rights to cease or diminish the amount of water 
they are diverting so that the amount of water called for by a senior water rights holder can flow 
downstream to meet the calling senior water rights holder’s unmet water demand. When DWR 
officials enforce a call, enforcement results in curtailment of water diversions in reverse order of the 
priority dates (latest to earliest) of the water rights on the river or stream.  
 
Even if a water right is decreed for a certain amount of water, diversion can be limited by the 
Water Commissioner to prevent “waste.”  The right to divert water in Colorado is limited to beneficial 
uses.  Wasteful uses are not deemed beneficial.  As such,  a water right owner may only divert such 
an amount of water as is needed to accomplish the beneficial use (such as irrigating a tract of land) 
together with such additional water as is reasonable to accomplish the use (i.e. water to cover transit 
losses in a ditch between the point of diversion and the land to be irrigated). 
 
Colorado adheres to the doctrine of maximum utilization of water with the intent that limited 
water supplies meet the water needs of the most people. As part of creating flexibility in allocating 
limited water resources, Colorado law allows water rights ho lders to implement a “plan for 
augmentation.”  Under such a plan, a water rights holder can continue to divert water during a water 
shortage by replacing in the river or stream system the water that junior water rights holders deplete 
through use. There ar e two primary sources of replacement (or augmentation) water supplies: (a) 
water stored in a reservoir that is released to offset out -of-priority diversions by a junior water right 
holder, and (b) consumptive use credits that relate to “drying up” historic ally irrigated lands and 
transferring the consumptive use associated with the irrigation to a new use (such as municipal use).  
A “consumptive use” of water is a use that permanently removes water from a river or stream system 
through evaporation, transpiration, or incorporation of the water into products, plant tissue, or animal 
tissue.  Consumptive uses are distinguishable from uses where the water is returned to the river or 
stream system after use such as groundwater return flows after was is applied to irrigation.     
 
Water rights are either “absolute” or “conditional.” An absolute water right is a water right 
that has been applied to beneficial use, and which has been confirmed by the Water Court. Once a 
water right has been made absolute, no further Water Court action is necessary to keep the water right 
viable, so long as the water for which the water right is approved continues to be applied to beneficial 
use consistent with the terms of the decree that awarded the water right. A conditional water right 
represents the existence of an intent to beneficially use water at some point in the future. The award 
of a conditional water right by the Water Court preserves the administration priority of the water right 
while providing the owner of the water right (and its successors) time to develop the water right and 
use the water.   To maintain a conditional water right, the water right owner must return to the Water 
Court within six years of the date of the original decree awarding the water right to demonstrate that 
Page 41 of 90
 
 
Page 5 
 
it has been diligent in applying the water right to the beneficial uses approved for the water by the 
Water Court. If the Water Court finds that the owner of the water right has demonstrated diligence in 
the development of the water right, the Water Court will maintain the water right and provide the 
owner another six years in which to develop it.  Thereafter, the owner of a conditional water right 
must apply to the water court every six years to demonstrate diligence, or to demonstrate that the 
water right qualifies for an award of absolute status.    
 
Because groundwater in western Colorado typically is hydrologically connected to surface 
water, many wells cannot be operated without a water right.  However, certain groundwater wells 
may operate without a water right because they are “exempt” from admini stration.  The exemption, 
which is granted by statute, is due to the limited amount of water those wells are permitted to withdraw 
and the limited uses to which the water can be placed.  The most common exempt groundwater uses 
in western Colorado is associated with a well located on a tract of land 35 acres or larger in size.  35-
acre exempt wells can be used to supply water in up to three single family residences, irrigate up to 1 
acre of land, and provide water for livestock.

B. City’s Plan for Augmentation 
 
As discussed above, a junior water right  – or one that would otherwise be out -of-priority –  
can continue to divert water despite a call from a senior water right by having a plan for augmentation 
adjudicated by the water court that makes replacement water supplies available to meet senior calls.  
In the early 1980s, the City adjudicated a water court decree in Case No. 83CW110 that approved a 
plan for augmentation.    The plan was later amended in Case No. 93CW332 to make minor 
corrections in the description of the amounts of the City’s ownership in certain water rights.  Copies 
of both decrees are attached as Exhibit B. The plan contemplates a municipal water supply for a 
population of approximately 14,100 people.  
 
 The City’s augmentation plan utilizes a combination of “consumptive use credits” and water 
stored in Ruedi Reservoir on the City’s behalf. “Consumptive use credits” are associated with the dry 
up of land historically irrigated by ditches on Rifle Creek.  Under the plan, the City maintains 
discretion to forego diversions and irrigation under  ditches on Rifle Creek in exchange for 
consumptive use credits.. Water not utilized for irrigation is then allowed to flow down Rifle Creek 
and into the Colorado River to make up for depletions in the City’s municipal water system attributed 
to diversions under junior water rights not covered by the HUP. When the City diverts this water for 
irrigation (such as at the cemetery and golf course) , the corresponding consumptive use credits are 
unavailable to the plan. The augmentation plan also uses a portion of the City’s contracts for storage 
in Ruedi Reservoir.  Together, these replacement sources are available to allow the City’s junior water 
rights to divert water into the municipal water system at times when there is a senior call on the 
Colorado River. Rifle currently has an estimated population of approximately 11,000.

Island Ditch No. 2.  The City obtained a finding of diligence for these rights from the Water Court in 
Case No. 2022CW3066, and a finding of diligence will be due again in December 2028.

Intake Enlargement in Case No. 2023CW3175.  This water right is conditional in the amount of 23.1 
c.f.s. and was continued for an additional six years. The next finding of diligence will be due in August 
2030. We recommend that the City develop a plan to increase pumping capacity at the Rifle Pond in 
order to implement portions of this water rights. 
 
CONCLUSION 
 
 As a general matter, the City of Rifle has a strong, stable water rights portfolio capable of 
providing water users with a reliable supply.  The City has positioned itself to grow through the 
acquisition of supplies in Ruedi Reservoir and the adjudication of the City’s augmentation plan.  As 
additional properties annex and develop within the City, dedication of new water rights is imperative 
to ensure that the City maintains adequate water rights to meet future demands.  We recommend 
focusing on the following tasks over the next 1 – 3 years:

WATER DEMAND 
 
The current water demand for the City is approximately 620 million gallons (1900 acre feet) based on 
the 2025 Water Year data.  The City currently serves approximately 4,700 EQRs and 11,000 people.  
The winter demand is approximately 30  million gallons per month and the peak summer monthly 
demand is approximately 95 million gallons in July. The pattern of use generally follows the pattern for 
an Equivalent Residential Unit (EQR) as shown on the attached Table 1. Each EQR is estimated to 
require 0.74 acre feet (242,000 gallons) per year including 350 gallons per day for in- house use (3.5 
people) for a total of .39 acre feet and 0.35 acre feet (114,000 gallons) for irrigation of 5,000 square feet 
of lawn and garden area. This equates to an average water use of 190 gallons per capita per day (gpcd) 
which is close to the 198 gpcd determined by  SGM in the 2008 Water Conservation Plan, bu t higher 
than the mor e recent water data is showing in the last 5 years.  The 2021 JVA Utility Maintenance, 
Capital, and Rate Study data indicates a water demand of approximately 135 gpcd and the 2025 Water 
Year data indic ates approximately 155 gpcd . However, the 2021 JVA study indicated the average 
annual water demand was approximately 330 gpd per EQR and the peak day was approximately 810 
gpd per EQR.  This generally indicates more efficient use of water, but more people per EQR than 20 
years ago.  The traditional engineering values for water demand projections listed in Table 1 are still 
valid, but may not be as conservative as in the past
