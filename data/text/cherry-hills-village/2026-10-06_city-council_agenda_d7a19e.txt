Resolution 29, Series 2026; Supporting Ballot Question 2C 
Clerk Gillespie explained that at a prior meeting, the Council had approved Ordinance 8, 
Series 2026, which referred a Charter amendment to voters at the November 3, 2026, 
regular municipal election. Arapahoe County had since confirmed that this measure 
would appear on the ballot as Question 2C. Clerk Gillespie noted that under state law, 
Council may pass a resolution supporting or opposing a ballot measure, subject to the 
limitation that distribution be conducted through established and customary means. 
Mayor Pro Tem Hoellen suggested the resolution be amended to more forcefully 
articulate the point that the land use legislation being referenced purports to be a matter 
of statewide concern and yet does not apply to the entire state. Council discussed 
where that language might be added in the resolution. City Attorney Guckenberger 
indicated Council could approve the resolution with that amendment and she could draft 
such a clause after the meeting. 
Councilor Heller then asked whether the resolution included language expressing the 
City's intent to act to the fullest extent of the authority granted to it by the citizens of 
Colorado and the state Constitution, in particular with respect to home rule zoning 
jurisdiction. City Attorney Guckenberger noted that while she understood the sentiment, 
the phrase “to the fullest extent” can function as a modifier that may be difficult to 
defend judicially at a later date, and she advised against its inclusion. She pointed out 
that the actual Charter amendment language already states that these matters of local 
and municipal concern are vested in the City by the Colorado Constitution and the 
Charter, which accomplishes much the same purpose more cleanly. City Manager 
Cramer directed Councilor Heller's attention to a separate whereas clause in the 
resolution that states the Council desires to affirm the City's constitutionally granted 
home rule authority over zoning and land use planning and regulation, particularly 
despite potential conflicts with land use legislation, which he believed captured the 
essence of what Councilor Heller was seeking, even if not in a single sequential 
passage. Mayor Pro Tem Hoellen offered a more pointed articulation of the underlying 
concern, suggesting that a new whereas clause could say something to the effect that 
the land use legislation purports to usurp the constitutionally granted plenary home rule 
authority to regulate zoning and land use at the local level under the guise that it 
constitutes a matter of statewide concern, even though it does not apply to the 
overwhelming majority of the state.

Resolution 30, Series 2026; Opposing Ballot Issue 7A 
Clerk Gillespie explained that the City falls within the boundaries of the Front Range 
Passenger Rail District, and the District's board of directors had referred a measure to 
District voters that would increase taxes. That measure would appear on the November 
3, 2026, ballot as Ballot Issue 7A. Clerk Gillespie clarified that the Council, without 
taking any position on passenger rail as a concept, had expressed objection to the 
current District structure being used for taxing purposes and had directed staff to 
prepare a resolution opposing the measure. 
Mayor Brown explained that the concern is less about supporting or opposing a specific 
tax and more about the manner in which the Front Range Passenger Rail District was 
gerrymandered to target a smaller geographic area where it was believed to have a 
better chance of passing. The District purported to use a five-mile radius around each 
proposed passenger rail stop, including any municipality with a sufficient portion of its 
area within that radius. However, she noted that unincorporated Jefferson County, 
which lies across the street from the planned stop at the intersection of Santa Fe and 
Mineral in Littleton, was not included in the District, meaning those residents would not 
be subject to the tax. Similarly, Highlands Ranch, a large, unincorporated community in 
Douglas County whose residents live in close proximity to proposed stations, was 
excluded entirely. She also noted that Centennial faces its own inequities, with a 
significant portion of the City falling well outside the five-mile radius while still being 
included in the District.

Councilor Robinson stated that he is supportive of the Resolution and agrees that the 
gerrymandered structure is problematic. He affirmed that the Council's decision not to 
take a position on passenger rail itself is appropriate, since the concept may well 
represent a viable solution if pursued through a more equitable structure. He asked how 
the City would publish or distribute these resolutions. City Clerk Gillespie responded 
that under state law, the City's options are quite limited. The resolutions would be 
posted on the City's website on the resolutions page, in the same manner as any other 
resolution. City Attorney Guckenberger noted, however, that once adopted, the 
resolutions become public documents that any member of the public may reference or 
distribute in the exercise of their own political speech. Mayor Brown acknowledged that 
the practical impact of the resolution is primarily as a statement of principle, and that 
this resolution would likely gain more traction if multiple communities passed similar 
resolutions in opposition. 
Councilor Eber asked about the timeline of the District’s boundaries, the legislative 
process behind Senate Bill 26-172, and public input. Mayor Brown explained that the 
original District, established in 2021, had run the full length of the Interstate 25 corridor 
from Pueblo to the Wyoming border and included communities along that strip; there 
were some legitimate logistical arguments for revising those boundaries, including 
feedback from county clerks who noted that the original strip boundaries did not 
correspond to any existing political boundaries that could be used to target ballots; 
Senate Bill 26-172 changed the boundaries of the District; public testimony had been 
taken at the Capitol on Senate Bill 26-172 and people had testified in opposition, though 
that opposition proved ineffective; the General Assembly, through that legislation, 
specifically amended the District's boundaries, and that the District board subsequently 
referred the tax measure to the voters of the newly defined District. She did not agree 
with the solution. 
Mayor Brown noted that the District's financing structure creates an additional inequity: 
cities that host passenger rail stations would receive reimbursements from the District 
for their role in establishing and maintaining those stations, meaning that the people 
who stand to benefit most from the stations would receive financial rebates funded in 
part by tax revenue collected from communities throughout the broader District that may 
be further from any station. 
Councilor Heller raised a broader policy question. While expressing agreement with the 
concerns about the gerrymandering of the District, he asked what the practical 
consequences of defeating the ballot measure would be. City Manager Cramer replied 
that if the ballot measure fails as a result of opposition from communities banding 
together, it does not spell doom for passenger rail; it would simply mean that the 
preferred financing mechanism would need to be reconsidered, and the District or the 
General Assembly would need to pursue an alternative approach.  
Councilor Eber noted that just as Resolution 29, Series 2026, concerns the General 
Assembly asserting statewide authority over a matter that does not uniformly apply 
statewide, the Front Range Passenger Rail situation represents another instance in
