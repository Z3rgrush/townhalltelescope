Study Session – Legislative Rezoning and Lot Frontage – Paul Workman, Community 
Development Director 
 
The City Council has directed staff to create a new institutional zone district for uses 
such as schools and churches, so that those uses carry an appropriate institutional 
designation. Separately, the Planning and Zoning Commission and City Council had 
heard several zoning and subdivision applications that prompted questions about lot 
frontage requirements. City Council directed that lot frontage requirements be updated 
or added to the zoning ordinance. Community Development Director Workman said he 
and the City Attorney's office were drafting the regulations, including the details of the 
new zone district and the lot frontage requirements. City Council held a study session 
on the topic the previous month and gave staff approval to continue. Both items would 
require a recommendation from the Planning and Zoning Commission because they 
amend the municipal code. Staff brought the concept to the Commission early so 
members would know the project was underway and would see details over the next 
several months. 
 
In summary, Community Development Director Workman said City Council, through its 
legislative power, would create an institutional zoning designation, primarily for schools 
and churches, and rezone those properties from their current residential designations. 
He said communities throughout the Front Range use this tool fairly frequently to 
implement a master plan and preserve community character, and that the authority is 
recognized in the rezoning section of the municipal code. He described lot frontage as a 
common requirement across jurisdictions, intended to ensure every lot is adjacent to a 
public or private street. He said this helps ensure delivery of water, sewer, fire, police, 
and other public services. 
 
Commissioner Wyman then asked whether lot frontage on a private street would be 
recognized by plat or by a recorded easement. Community Development Director 
Workman said it could be either. He cited the roads in Glenmoor and the Sunset Drive 
neighborhood as private roads dedicated by plat, as tracts or easements dedicated to a 
master association for maintenance and upkeep. He said very old private roads may 
have been created by easement, but they are generally created through a plat as a tract 
and dedicated to a homeowners association.  
 
Community Development Director Workman then described the goal of the project, 
which is to align the use of property with a specific zoning designation. Residential 
zoning creates certain expectations about externalities such as vehicle trips per day and 
noise. All institutional uses currently carry a residential designation, yet the purpose and 
intent statements for the residential zone districts in the municipal code do not list 
institutional uses. A separate designation would add clarity and transparency and would 
set expectations for anyone purchasing property adjacent to an institutional zone.

NEXUS STUDIES: 
A nexus study can include a wide variety of issues like roads, sewer, water, and fire district 
costs, or it can be small in scope, like this Study, and limited to parks, trails, and public open 
space dedications and fees-in-lieu thereof. It is a legal, planning, and technical analysis used by 
local governments to justify requiring development to dedicate land or pay a fee-in-lieu of the 
land dedication, in this case, for parks, trails, and public open space.  It is called a nexus study 
because it serves the core function of establishing a “reasonable connection”, or “nexus”, 
between a development’s impact on the parks, trails, and public open space system(s) and the

required dedication. It is used to ensure that land dedication and fee-in-lieu of land dedication are 
proportional to the additional demand on the system, as required by the relevant legal concepts 
noted below. 
 
Typical information included in a nexus study: 
• Essential Nexus and Rough Proportionality – the study proves that development does result 
in an impact on the system and the study provides analysis for the justification for the 
amount of land dedication or fee-in-lieu thereof.  
• Service Area(s) – the study identifies the service area, which is the City. 
• Level of Service Standards – the study identifies current ratios of population to the acreage 
of parks, trails, and public open space. This provides a baseline that the study relies on for 
the dedication and fee-in-lieu recommendations.  
• Land Dedication and Fee-in-Lieu Requirements – the study makes recommendations for 
legally justified land dedication and fee-in-lieu of the land dedication requirement. 
• Legal Analysis – the study ensures that the recommended land dedication and fee-in-lieu 
requirements are compliant with legal standards. 
 
A nexus study is not: 
• A Parks, Trails, and Open Space Master Plan – the study does not identify previously 
unidentified new park and trail locations or new improvements to existing parks or trails. 
• A resource for operations or maintenance – the study does not identify budgets for the 
operations or maintenance of existing parks and trails. 
• An opportunity to fix perceived or real deficiencies in the parks, trails, and public open 
space system – the study is not used to build a “war chest” of financial resources for future 
improvements that are currently unidentified or property acquisition that is currently 
unknown. 
• A wish list for higher service levels – the study does not set fees based on a higher level of 
service than what is currently provided to existing residents. 
 
RELEVANT LEGAL CONCEPTS: 
Nollan/Dolan Test. Two U.S. Supreme Court decisions establish the framework for evaluating 
conditions of approval related to land dedications or fees-in-lieu: the condition must: (1) have a 
rational nexus to a legitimate government interest, and (2) be roughly proportional to the impact 
of the proposed development. 
• Nollan v. California Coastal Commission (1987)1. 
• U.S. Supreme Court Decision: The Court held that a government agency may not impose a 
permit condition unless the condition has a rational nexus connecting it to a legitimate 
governmental interest. In the land dedication context, this means the City must be able to 
identify a genuine public need that the dedication or fee-in-lieu is meant to address.

Recommended Change. 
TischlerBise recommends that Cherry Hills Village amend the dedication/fee-in-lieu requirement 
to a population-based method that relies on the housing type rather than the land area. From 
experience, TischlerBise has found that housing type is a more proportionate and defensible way 
to assess land dedication as compared to a flat percentage of the area of the land being 
subdivided.  
 
In summary, demand for parks, trails, and public open space is a function of the number of new 
homes/increased population and not the size of the property on which a new home is constructed. 
Population growth is the leading indicator for the need to maintain and expand park 
infrastructure, and population growth is better determined by the housing type rather than the 
size of a residential lot; therefore, it is a more proportional method on which to base the 
dedication/fee requirements.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
Cherry Hills Village, Colorado, retained TischlerBise, Inc. to assist in updating its land dedication standards 
(City Code Sec. 17-3-30) to meet the demands generated by development for public open space, parks, 
and trails in the City. This report presents the methodologies and calculations used to generate current 
levels of service, fee-in-lieu of land dedication, and other proposed changes to the existing standards. The 
methodologies used are applied to ensure equity and proportionality in the program. During the effort, 
the project team has gathered input and feedback from the City Council, the Planning & Zoning 
Commission, and the Parks, Trails & Recreation Commission. 
GENERAL OVERVIEW OF EXACTIONS  
Impact fees, land dedications, and fees-in-lieu are all types of exactions. Exactions can be imposed as a 
condition for approval of land development and relate to a public need generated by the development 
itself. Exactions can take several forms, such as dedication of land, construction of an amenity, or a 
monetary charge. These exactions are imposed on a one-time basis. The purpose of the exaction must 
relate to the need created by the development and be roughly proportional to the development’s impact.  
Payment of a fee-in-lieu of land dedication may happen when there are site constraints within the 
development, or the location is not preferred by the jurisdiction. When collecting a fee-in-lieu, the amount 
should be equivalent to the value of land that would have been dedicated and any required infrastructure 
improvements.  
FEDERAL ENABLING LEGISLATION 
In one of the most important exaction cases, the U. S. Supreme Court found that a government agency 
imposing exactions on development must demonstrate an “essential nexus” between the exaction and 
the interest being protected (see Nollan v. California Coastal Commission, 1987). In the Dolan v. City of 
Tigard, OR, 1994 case, the Court ruled that an exaction must also be “roughly proportional” to the burden 
created by development. Lastly, Sheetz v. County of El Dorado (2024) held that the Takings Clause of the 
U. S. Constitution makes no distinction between legislative and administrative land use permit exactions; 
both must satisfy the "essential nexus" and "rough proportionality" tests from Nollan and Dolan. This 
overturned the prior rule that broadly applied, legislatively adopted fees or dedication requirements were 
exempt from that scrutiny. Consequently, subdivision land dedication policies set by ordinance, not just 
case-by-case conditions, need individualized justification tying the dedication to the specific 
development's impacts. 
COLORADO ENABLING LEGISLATION  
For local governments, the first step in evaluating funding options for facility improvements and 
maintenance is to determine basic options and requirements established by state law. Some states have 
more conservative legal parameters that restrict local governments to specifically authorized actions. In 
contrast, “home-rule” states, like Colorado, grant local governments broader powers that may or may not 
be precluded or preempted by state statutes, depending on the circumstances and on the state’s 
particular laws. The City of Cherry Hills Village is a home-rule City.

Colorado Revised Statute 29-20-104.5 authorizes local governments to impose development charges, or 
exactions, to fund capital facilities needed to serve development. Park and recreation facilities qualify as 
essential capital facilities because they provide measurable public health benefits through accessible 
spaces for physical activityi, reduce stressii, enhance mental well-being and social cohesioniii, increase 
property values, and deliver critical environmental services in the form of reduced water and air 
pollutioniv. The land dedication program ensures new residential development defrays its proportional 
impact on park infrastructure by dedicating land commensurate with the demand created by a 
subdivision, as allowed by statute, thereby maintaining existing service levels for both current and future 
residents. 
LEVEL OF SERVICE STANDARDS 
Cherry Hills Village’s current standard requires land dedication of 7.5 percent of the lot size. The 
recommended change is to move from a land-based dedication standard to an acreage per 1,000 residents 
standard (also known as population-based). Specifically, TischlerBise’s analysis shows a dedication of 
18.10 acres per 1,000 residents is the current level of service when the City’s current public open space, 
parks, and trails inventory is compared to the current population. Figure 1 is a summary of the dedication 
standards. Note that the current levels of service have been calculated using a methodology commonly 
used for impact fee programs to establish equity and proportionality for future exactions. This approach 
ensures defensibility in the land dedication policy. Note that if an exaction is assessed higher than the 
current level of service, then development is being burdened to an elevated service level and not just 
addressing the new demand of its development. When new development is burdened beyond its impact, 
the Nollan, Dolan, and Sheetz tests can be introduced by an applicant.

CITY OF CENTENNIAL, CO 
General park and open space land dedication standards in the City of Centennial are as follows: 
• 6.0 acres of public parkland per 1,000 projected residents for parks 
• 0.25 acres per 1,000 projected residents for other public purposes (including trails, fire stations, 
libraries, and civic facilities) 
Park dedication requirements are calculated based on the projected population generated by new 
residential development, which is derived using population assumptions. Population per dwelling unit 
varies by residential density. These population factors are used to convert residential density into an 
estimated population, which is then used to determine required parkland dedication. 
• Up to 7.49 dwelling units/acre: 2.96 persons per unit 
• 7.50 to 14.99 dwelling units/acre: 2.39 persons per unit 
• 15.00+ dwelling units/acre: 1.76 persons per unit 
In addition to land dedication, the City may require a fee-in-lieu of land dedication when park sites cannot 
be practically integrated into a development. Fees are calculated as the required acreage of dedication 
multiplied by a City-established land value, which is currently set at $40,000 per acre (subject to annual 
adjustment by City Council resolution). Applicants may submit an appraisal to challenge the assumed land 
value. 
The City may also accept a combination of land dedication and fee-in-lieu payment. All dedicated parkland 
must be suitable for public use, including safe access, appropriate location, and compatibility with park 
functions. Land that is hazardous, environmentally constrained, or required for utilities or drainage is not 
eligible for dedication. All fees collected in lieu of land dedication are used for the acquisition, 
development, and improvement of park and open space facilities and are held or administered by the City 
for those purposes.

CITY OF ENGLEWOOD, CO 
General open space and civic space standards require residential developments to provide 10.7 acres of 
open space per 1,000 projected residents. Population is estimated using dwelling unit factors based on 
housing type: 
• Detached houses, multi-unit houses, and row houses: 2.15 persons per unit 
• Apartments and other multi-unit residential buildings: 1.8 persons per unit 
• Age-restricted housing (qualifying under federal standards): 1.5 persons per unit 
The City Council determines at final plat approval whether open space requirements will be met through 
land dedication, fee-in-lieu payment, or a combination of both. The fee-in-lieu amount is based on the fair 
market value of the land that would otherwise be required for dedication. 
Dedicated land must be suitable for public use and generally conform to an approved master plan. 
Acceptable uses include public parks, greenways, trails, and other recreation-oriented spaces, with design 
encouraged to incorporate natural, cultural, or scenic features. Land is typically conveyed to the City in 
fee simple or easement form and may require installation of associated public improvements such as 
access and utility infrastructure. 
When a fee-in-lieu is accepted, funds are deposited into a City account and must be used for the 
acquisition and development of parks, trails, open space, and related facilities located reasonably near 
the development. Timing of dedication or payment occurs at final plat or final development approval, with 
phased developments allowed to pay proportionally based on phase population. 
Open space requirements may be satisfied through a variety of approved space types, including 
community parks (25–100 acres), neighborhood parks (2–10 acres), small parks (0.5–2 acres), trails, civic 
plazas and squares, and enhanced streetscapes, provided they are integrated into an overall connected 
system of civic and recreational spaces.  
In addition to its land dedication policy, the City of Englewood also assesses a parks and recreation impact 
fee. Impact fee revenues are used for improvements to existing park facilities; however, the fees are not 
used for land acquisition. Under the current fee schedule, a single-family home pays an impact fee of 
$777, while a multifamily unit pays $490.70. 
1.3 - KEY DIFFERENCES IN PEER COMMUNITIES 
In the following figure, the land dedication standards are presented using two different approaches: 
“population-based” standards (acres per 1,000 residents) and “land area based” standards (percent of 
gross land area). While both are common and reasonable methodologies for establishing park and open 
space requirements, they measure development impacts differently and therefore are not directly 
comparable on an “apples-to-apples” basis. Population-based standards are scaled based on the number 
Cherry Hills Village, Colorado

of anticipated residents generated by a development, while land area-based standards are scaled with 
the physical size of the subdivision regardless of residential density. 
Currently, Cherry Hills Village and Bow Mar apply the land area approach, requiring 7.5 percent and 20 
percent of gross land area, respectively. In contrast, Lakewood, Centennial, and Englewood use 
population-based formulas that calculate required dedication based on projected residents generated by 
the development. These jurisdictions apply population assumptions based on housing type or residential 
density to estimate the park and open space demand created by new development. 
Among the population-based systems, dedication requirements vary: Lakewood requires 5.50 acres per 
1,000 residents, Centennial requires a total of 6.25 acres per 1,000 residents, and Englewood requires 
10.70 acres per 1,000 residents. Each jurisdiction also differs in the range of facilities that may satisfy 
dedication requirements, with some communities allowing trails, open space corridors, or other 
recreational amenities to count toward the requirement. 
The calculation of fee-in-lieu payments are generally consistent across all jurisdictions that were reviewed 
and typically follows the formula below: 
[Required Land Dedication] × [Fair Market Value of Land] = Fee-In-Lieu Payment 
However, jurisdictions differ in how fair market value is determined. Cherry Hills Village, Bow Mar, and 
Englewood primarily rely on appraised market value. Lakewood establishes fees annually using appraisals, 
market studies, and other valuation tools. Lastly, Centennial uses a City-established assumed land value 
per acre that may be challenged through an appraisal process. Arguably, the static land value for 
Centennial ($40,000 per acre) is considerably lower than the current market value. 
Several jurisdictions also express a preference for land dedication over fee-in-lieu when practicable. 
Lakewood specifically emphasizes a preference for fee-in-lieu, while Cherry Hills Village, Bow Mar, and 
Englewood Codes allow the municipality discretion to determine whether land dedication, fee-in-lieu 
payment, or a combination of both best serves the community. Bow Mar additionally requires a separate 
per lot development fee to support the maintenance and improvement of open space facilities. 
Finally, many of the jurisdictions provide flexibility in how dedication requirements may be satisfied. 
Lakewood and Centennial allow certain privately owned park land to count toward dedication 
requirements if publicly accessible and maintained through long-term agreements, while Englewood 
encourages integration between public and private open space systems. These provisions reflect a 
broader trend toward flexibility in meeting park and open space needs as communities continue to 
urbanize and redevelop.

2 - LAND DEDICATION STANDARDS  
This chapter details the level of service calculations for the land dedication. Existing public open space, 
park, and trail inventory are used in conjunction with the city population to understand the current level 
of service. Levels of service have been calculated using a methodology commonly used for impact fee 
programs to capture the currently provided service levels to the existing population for the purposes of 
equity and proportionality. Collecting land or fee-in-lieu at a level of service higher than what is currently 
being provided to address deficiencies or to achieve a higher desired level of service means development 
is being burdened to elevated service levels for existing residents, not just addressing the new demand of 
the proposed development. 
Along with determining the current level of service, the following chapter compares the level of service 
per 1,000 residents to the number of residents per household to find the proposed land dedication 
standards. These standards represent the past investment the community has made in the park, trail, and 
public open space infrastructure.

3.1 - LAND VALUATION 
Under City Code Sec. 17-3-30, payment in lieu of land dedication (fee-in-lieu) may be accepted at the 
discretion of the City when it is determined to more effectively meet the needs for parks, trails, and public 
open space. The amount of the payment is based on the fair market value of the land that would otherwise 
be required for dedication. Fee payments must be made prior to final plat approval, or in accordance with 
a subdivision improvements agreement, and are deposited into the City’s Land Dedication Fund. Funds 
collected are used to acquire, develop, or improve parks, open space, trails, and other recreational 
amenities in a manner consistent with the City’s Master Plan. TischlerBise recommends continuing this 
policy to ensure the City captures the fair value of the land to continue the public benefit. 
The current policy does not state how the fair market value is determined. TischlerBise recommends 
including the stipulation that the market value be determined by a qualified appraiser and approved by 
the City Council.

(a) Application for Variance. A subdivider may submit an application for a variance to this Chapter or

approval with conditions, it shall be sent to the Council for decision.  
(d) Criteria for Granting Variances. When considering the Commission's recommendation, approval of 
variances shall be based fundamentally on findings that unusual topographical or other exceptional 
conditions or circumstances not caused by action of the subdivider require such variance, 
modification or waiver; and that the granting thereof will not adversely affect the general public 
nor have the effect of nullifying the intent and purpose of these regulations. In addition to those 
findings, no approval of any variance under this Section shall be granted unless the Council finds 
that:  
(1) Reasonable protections are afforded adjacent properties;  
(2) The requested variance will not have an adverse impact on the character of the neighborhood 
or have an adverse effect on the physical or environmental conditions of the surrounding 
properties; and  
(3) The variance is the minimum variance necessary to alleviate the exceptional condition or 
circumstance.  
(e) Limitation of Variances. In no case shall a variance be granted to allow for the creation of a lot that 
is not lawful under the applicable provisions of Chapter 16 of this Code.  
(f) Public Hearing. Following a public hearing, the Council shall consider the application for a variance 
and the recommendations of the Commission. This public hearing may be concurrent with the 
public hearing on the preliminary plat. The Council may approve, conditionally approve, or 
disapprove the application. In granting variances, modifications, or waivers, the Council may 
require such conditions that in its judgment will substantially secure the objectives of the 
standards and requirements affected.
