PUBLIC HEARING REGARDING THE 2027 SEDGWICK COUNTY BUDGET.
Chairman Blubaugh
said, “Good evening, Rusty.”
Mr. Russell Leeds,
Assistant County Manager, greeted the Commissioners and said, “First, the purpose of this meeting this evening is to hear comments and input from citizens on budgets related to, I’m sorry, on items related to budget matters. We have staff who will be taking copious notes throughout this process. So if you hear things that you want to have staff follow up on, do research on, and come back with other information later on, we will have that available. Then also starting next week, we will have an item on agenda each week thereafter until budget adoption so that you will have the opportunity to discuss things brought forward in this meeting and other things that you bring forward between now and budget adoption.”
Mr. Leeds
continued, “So with that, this evening is the first public hearing for the 2027 recommended budget. Sedgwick County's recommended operating budget totals $624,195,119. This budget will be supported with a property tax levy of $217,672,598, which is approximately equivalent to 26.842 mils, based on the estimated assessed valuation and subject to technical adjustments. I recommend that you open the public hearing, receive comments, and close the public hearing once it is over.”

Ms. Faith Martin,
608 S. Drury Ln., greeted the Commissioners and said, “I only have three things I want to hit on, so I hope I stay in that five minutes. There's been a lot of talk about core services. I still don't think core services have been defined, especially when I looked through the budget. I see things like $2,000,000 for Intrust Arena, $160,000 for the Appraiser's Office, $271,000 for the DA (District Attorney) even though they already have a $3,800,000 contract for that exact same service, $13,600,000 for WSU (Wichita State University). I guess I'm not understanding what a core service is because I thought core services were Public Works and public safety. None of those things sound like that. I'm really concerned about that, especially when Sedgwick County Elections, which I voted today, and I'm a proud supervising judge (SJ) as part of Sedgwick County Elections. We're gearing up for a huge election and an even bigger one in November, and it's more and more difficult to find election workers. It's more and more difficult to get new voting locations and then staff those. Election workers haven't seen a raise, but when I look at page six of the proposed budget, I see six years of raises and growth to compensation for everyone else. Election workers are regular people. They don't have full-time jobs at Sedgwick County, but they are Sedgwick County staff. We all have to fill out documents for HR (Human Resources), and we all get paid from Sedgwick County Elections.”
Ms. Martin
continued, “Teenagers, who we are trying desperately to recruit, will not come work for $8 an hour, and it's a long day. I don't know how many of you have worked an election, but as a SJ, we're there 14, 15 hours. Not to mention the supplies we pick up, calling everyone ahead of time, scheduling, going to the location, and we're not even staff. So I think it's really, really important that instead of, you know, kicking this down the road for a later date, I think it's time that we find $93,000 out of the, I've mentioned a bunch of things, find $93,000 and pay our election workers because next year is going to be a pretty big election, and we can't wait until next year to try to fund that and try to recruit. We lost a lot of good election workers this year that just didn't come back. I think part of it is people can't take a day off work and then make $8 an hour all day, and so I think this is something that's really critical. People aren't working for free. People don't volunteer to work the elections. People get paid to work the elections because it's a job, and it's really hard. It's our neighbors, and we all take an oath, just like you do, to uphold the integrity of the Kansas Constitution, and I think it's important that we start paying our election workers that.”
Ms. Martin
continued, “The third thing I have is public safety. I would ask that in the future when you're talking about public safety, especially when it comes to the budget simulator, 50 percent of the budget in public safety is the jail and the Sheriff's Office. The rest of the, all of the other departments is the other 50 percent. So when people say they care about public safety, I don't really know that you're getting the full picture of what people care about. If you really do love the jail, and you care about that, and you want to see it funded, that should be a separate item that we can talk about. If you really love the Sheriff's Office, and you want to see them funded, and all the toys, and all the buildings, and all the healthcare that they need, that's wonderful. The public should be able to see the results of that, but wrapping public safety in all together? Is that EMS (Emergency Medical Services)? Is it fire? Is it 911? Like, what does that look like? So I think it's really critical when we talk about funding core services that we’re really clear on what core services are, what people actually want versus using public safety. I think that's an easy way to talk about a pretty massive part of the budget without getting into specifics. I don't think that's really appropriate when we can't fund a lot of the things that we're talking about.”
Ms. Martin
continued, “In the past, I've come up and said, yeah, we need to exceed the Revenue Neutral Rate because I do think we're running kind of lean. When I see appraisal values going up with a bad appraisal process, I don't see lobbying done at the state level to change that process. Property tax relief isn't just cutting taxes. It's ensuring that we have a fair appraisal process. I know that you're not over the Appraiser's Office. It's just part of the budget, but, you know, they're asking for staff, and they're asking for more when people are literally taxed out of their homes. I think the appraisal process is broken. The appeals process is absolutely broken, and people aren't getting the relief that they need. Even when they have a pathway to try to seek that, most of those appeals don't go through. So, if property tax relief is your number one and funding public safety, I think we need to be a lot clearer as to what those mean. Please fund raises for the election workers.”
Chairman Blubaugh
said, “Faith, I appreciate your comments, particularly with the appraisal process. We've actually, this budget includes quite a bit of money that needs to be put back into there, and we've got some philosophy changes as to how we've looked at property in the past, and we're wanting to be more inclusive of looking at everything instead of just a portion of the properties. So certainly appreciate your feedback. Anyone else have any questions or comments for Faith?”
Ms. Martin
said, “Please, please reevaluate the WSU mill levy. This will be the fourth budget cycle that I've asked you to look at that, and we missed all of those benchmarks.”

said, “Okay, thanks Faith. Next speaker, Andree Sisco. Good evening.”

I thank you for your time.”

Commissioner Howell
said, “This isn't part of the public comment. I just wanted to reflect on what's been said this evening. So, thank you for the speakers for coming out tonight. I would note that the three that spoke were all from District Five. I'm very proud of that. Thank you for coming out. I do want to make sure that Bruce, you're aware, and Andree, you're aware that, I guess, we have not adopted the old formula for senior center funding. However, that is essentially what's driving the levels today, and I think we will have a new discussion about potentially taking another look at that. I think that we need to have a formula. I think the old formula actually was okay. I'm not so sure that the static nature of that formula was where I want to be personally, but I do think that the formula was a performance-based formula. It did its job.”
Commissioner Howell
continued, “I would say also for, you know, Andree Cisco, there was two funding models that were proposed by, since last year, there was a motion to kind of take a look at senior center funding, and there was two proposals that came forward. As far as I know, I think they're both dead. I don't think that either one of them are going to go any further. We did see them in February at our retreat, and I think what we see this year in the proposed budget from Manager Stolz is a hold harmless, so no one goes down. I believe there's about a $35,000 enhancement. I believe is the right number. There's a few senior centers that will go up under the old formula. We don't currently have it codified in our county law, if you will, but using that formula as a guide, there's $35,000 that we’ll adjust a couple of the senior centers upwards a little bit because they have met the metrics for a slight increase. So Mulvane is, again, Mulvane is at the higher. I think, what are they at, $57,000? They're at $57,000 right now. They weren't there that long ago, but they're at $57,000 today. They'll still be at $57,000, and Andree Sisco, yours is at $57,000 as well, I believe, and that should not change this year, I don't think.”
Commissioner Howell
continued, “My point is that under those formulas that were proposed, there was going to be a drastic reduction in funding to the Oaklawn Senior Center, and that's not going to happen now. So there have been some things that have changed since we've spoke last. I want to make sure that you're aware of those changes. With regards to a few other things, I agree that, Faith, I agree core services should be defined. There’s got to be a section that says here we define what core services are in Sedgwick County, and I think that'd be healthy for any government to do that. I completely agree. I've actually said the same thing for years. I'm not really happy with the $2,000,000 for Intrust Bank Arena. I have some ideas on what we should do with that this year. We'll probably talk about that. From now through budget adoption, we will have regular discussions at our public open meetings about the budget, and I think this is one of the things we have to talk about. I think there may be some ways we can maybe do that differently this year, hopefully.”
Commissioner Howell
continued, “I would say, with the election pay, I don't know that I disagree with you. However, that would be new spending in a sense. As you said, we don't really have the money. I would say if we have a problem recruiting people, or we can't fill the staff needs we need. A lot of folks will actually do that job more as a volunteer position. Again, do we have open positions we can't fill because of salary? That's really the question. I think if that's where we're at, that would be a reason for us to maybe do something right away. As far as I know, we're still filling all those needs for the most part. So maybe I'm wrong, but, you know, the elections are upon us right now, and as far as I know we have the. Let me actually ask our staff. Do we have the staff we need for Tuesday's election? Are they secured? Do we know? Does anybody know the answer to that question? We don't have staff we need for Tuesday?”

said, “Yeah, let’s find out for Friday. That would be good to know that. Again, if we perceive that we have a deficit in people to run the election, we need to know that so we can hopefully do whatever's needed there. Then, you know, I don't know that I would say the jail ought to be a separate item. It is in the public safety section under the Sheriff's Office, but it's just broken up into small pieces. I mean there is a part for inmate medical, for example. It's in there. I mean, there's details. It's just not in a section for the jail specifically. You know, we do have, I think that's one of our core functions of government. We do have to operate a jail. So it is expensive, but we have to do that. I don't think we have a lot of choice whether we should or should not have a jail. I think we have to do that. I do agree with you that I think the appraisals are, in fact, I'm not really happy with the process. I think we could do better. I have been advocating for years now, but especially this last year, that we need to invest money to make our appraisals as accurate as possible. I don't think that caps work. I think there's some unintended consequences on assessment caps. Primarily, in my opinion, it creates a constitutional conflict. You can't have assessed value at a certain rate if it's also capped at a different rate. So it creates a conflict within the constitution.”
Commissioner Howell
continued, “Second of all, it actually raises property taxes on people whose properties are not increasing as quick in value. They actually take a higher percentage of the tax levy. The tax levy comes as an increase to them specifically. So I don't like the idea of assessment caps, but I do think the best system we have is accurate appraisals for everyone. So anything we can do to drive accuracy helps us divide the tax levy more fairly. So I agree that is a major opportunity, but it's primarily going to have to come from the state. So we are lobbying. I am lobbying. I'm sure that others in this room are lobbying as well. I agree with you completely that that is a great opportunity is to have the most accurate assessments as possible because that's the only way you can divide the tax levy in a fair manner. If you have an unfair assessment, then someone's paying too much, and someone else is paying too little, and that's not fair. So, I think that's all my comments and questions. I want to say thank you again to the three that spoke, and I'm sad we don't have more, but thank you for coming out tonight.”
