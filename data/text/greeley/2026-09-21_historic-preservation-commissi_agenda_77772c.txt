3. Letter to City Council Regarding the Proposed Cuts to the City of Greeley Museums 
Betsy Kellums (Planner III – Historic Preservation) opened the discussion by explaining the 
purpose of the special meeting: to review, refine, and approve a draft letter to the Greeley City 
Council expressing the Commission's concerns regarding proposed budget cuts to the City of 
Greeley Museums. The Commission had resolved at its July 20th regular meeting to hold this 
special session for that purpose. Commissioner Clough had prepared the original draft, and Vice 
Chair Azari had since prepared a reformatted and revised version. Commissioner Clough 
deferred to Vice Chair Azari's version as the working document, acknowledging it to be more 
effective. 
 
City Charter and Ordinance as Legal Foundation 
A central point of discussion was whether the letter should open with a direct citation of the City 
Charter. Mrs. Kellums read aloud Article 16, Section 16-1 of the Greeley City Charter, which 
states that “the City Council shall by ordinance provide for museums and such other cultural 
activities appropriate for the general welfare of the city, including conducting programs designed 
to preserve and improve community and self-understanding and quality of life.” Commissioners 
agreed this language was powerful and should be placed prominently at the beginning of the letter 
to frame the Commission's concerns within the City's own legal obligations. Dr. Chris Bowles 
(Museum Manager) further noted that Ordinance 27 (2024) also contains relevant language 
regarding public access and museum operations and offered to provide a link to that ordinance for 
inclusion. The Commission agreed that citing these foundational documents would strengthen the 
letter considerably. 

Impact on Historic Preservation Functions 
Mrs. Kellums explained that the Hazel E. Johnson Research Center at the City of Greeley 
Museums is a primary resource she relies upon to carry out her duties as Historic Preservation 
Planner, particularly for reviewing permits and nominations involving properties 40 years old or 
older. The research center houses the city's municipal archive, including historic photographs, 
property records, and historical maps. While some materials are available at the LINC Library 
and through an online database portal, much of the collection has not been digitized and exists 
only in paper form. Kellums stated that without consistent access to this collection, she could not 
perform her duties as effectively. 
Dr. Bowles added that under the current budget cut proposal, the museum's research assistant 
position, the staff member who helps researchers navigate the 90,000-item collection, would be 
eliminated. He indicated this would likely result in the Hazel E. Johnson Research Center either 
closing to the public entirely or being available only by appointment, subject to a single 
remaining curator's availability. 
Broader Community and Stewardship Concerns 
Commissioner DePetro raised the broader significance of the museum's holdings, noting that 
residents and families have entrusted personal documents, photographs, artifacts, and heirlooms 
to the City of Greeley for perpetual care. She argued that cutting museum staff does not merely 
inconvenience researchers, it jeopardizes the city's ability to properly preserve, catalog, and make 
accessible the history that citizens have entrusted to it. Chair Podell echoed this, emphasizing that 
the letter should stress both the short- and long-term community impact, not simply the effect on 
one or two staff functions. 
Commissioners agreed that the letter should speak to the integrity of the collection itself, the risk 
that items will deteriorate, be lost, or become inaccessible if not adequately staffed, and should 
convey that adequate staffing is also necessary to advance preservation into the future through 
digitization and curation, rather than simply maintaining the status quo. 
Greeley's Historic Distinction 
Dr. Bowles noted that the Greeley Museums system is the largest municipal museum system in 
Colorado in terms of buildings, and that in terms of collection items, only Colorado Springs 
exceeds it. He also noted that the Department of Culture created in Greeley in 1959 was the first 
such municipal Department of Culture in the country, a distinction that had not yet been 
prominently highlighted in public advocacy. Commissioners felt this fact was meaningful and 
should be included to remind the Council of Greeley's historic leadership in this area. 
Commissioner DePetro suggested the letter also draw a comparison to peer cities such as Fort 
Collins, Boulder, and Colorado Springs. 
Commissioner Clough cautioned that while pride in the scope of the collection is warranted, the 
letter should be careful not to imply the collection is too large to manage, a framing that might 
invite the opposite conclusion from budget-minded council members. 
Current Status and Political Context 
Dr. Bowles briefed the Commission on the current state of the budget process. The cuts were 
proposed by CPRD leadership and presented to Council at the July 7th meeting, where 
approximately 42 members of the public spoke in opposition. He reported that Councilman 
Huddleston made a strong public statement asking CPRD leadership to revisit the proposal with 
fresh eyes, and that Council has since scheduled a special session on September 11th for further 
deliberation. Commissioners noted this indicates that the letter would serve as reinforcement of a 
position Council has already begun to reconsider, rather than introducing the issue for the first 
time. 
Final Drafting Process and Next Steps 
Mrs. Kellums emphasized the urgency of finalizing the letter quickly, noting she would be out of 
the office from July 31st through August 14th, and that the city manager is actively developing 
his recommended budget for the September 11th retreat. Commissioners agreed the letter must 
reach Council before that deadline. 
The Commission agreed on the following process: Vice Chair Azari would incorporate the 
discussion points from the meeting, including the charter citation, the stewardship and community 
impact language, and other refinements, and send the revised draft to Mrs. Kellums and Chair 
Podell for review. Mrs. Kellums would then make the finalized letter available at the front desk 
for all commissioners to sign in person. Commissioners were encouraged to come in to sign by 
Wednesday, July 29th; Chair Podell, who would be returning Monday, August 3rd, would sign at 
that time. 
Regarding distribution, the letter will be formally addressed to the Greeley City Council, with 
copies to the City Manager, Deputy City Manager, and CPRD Director. The Commission 
discussed but decided against proactively releasing the letter to local media, noting that the letter 
is already public record by virtue of being part of the meeting packet, and that given the positive 
signals from the July 7th Council meeting, a direct submission to Council was the more 
appropriate course. 
Mrs. Kellums also noted that the Museum Advisory Board had passed its own resolution at its 
most recent meeting expressing similar concerns, which would accompany the Commission's 
advocacy efforts.

Chair Podell adjourned the meeting at 4:04pm.

8. Budget Update Presentation, Nathan Mosley, Budget and Policy Director  
Nathan Mosley, Director of Budget and Policy, presented the City of Greeley’s 2026 budget 
overview as part of the City’s ongoing effort to improve budget transparency and community 
understanding. 
 
He explained that Greeley operates under a home rule charter and council-manager form of 
government, with City Council setting policy and the City Manager overseeing operations. The 
2026 budget totals approximately $566 million, including $336 million for operations and $171 
million for capital projects, while maintaining required reserves. 
 
Mr. Mosley identified sales tax and property tax as the City’s primary revenue sources. Sales tax 
growth has slowed because of reduced discretionary spending, while property tax revenues have 
become less predictable due to oil and gas valuation changes. He noted that many revenues, 
including voter-approved taxes, restricted fees, and utility enterprise revenues, are dedicated to 
specific purposes and cannot be used to address general fund needs. 
 
The City is addressing an estimated $18 million general fund structural deficit caused by service 
and staffing expansions in 2022 and 2023, slower-than-expected revenue growth, and 
inflationary cost increases. To close the gap, City Council established department-specific 
reduction targets of 4%, 17%, or 25%, based on Council priorities, rather than applying uniform 
cuts. 
 
The reduction plan includes eliminating more than 30 vacant positions, voluntary separations 
involving approximately 64 employees, and updated fee schedules. Nearly 100 positions have 
been reduced overall. Because personnel costs represent more than 60% of general fund 
expenditures, workforce reductions remain a significant component of the strategy. The 
recommended budget was expected to be publicly posted September 4, followed by a September 
11 Council budget retreat, with formal adoption anticipated in October and November. 
 
Commission members raised several questions during the presentation. 
 
One commissioner inquired why, in the context of an emerging structural deficit identified in 
2025, the City Council proceeded with commitments to major capital projects such as the 
downtown civic campus and the Cascadia development. Mr. Mosley clarified that capital 
expenditures are funded through separate capital funds and do not affect the general fund 
balance. He acknowledged that such commitments may reduce financial flexibility going 
forward but emphasized that the two budgetary areas operate independently. 
 
A second commissioner expressed concern that proposed cuts to cultural programming — 
including Centennial Village, Arts Picnic, and the Farmers Market — represented a threat to 
long-established community assets in favor of uncertain future benefits. Mr. Mosley 
acknowledged that community feedback on these programs had been heard and incorporated into 
Council discussions. He confirmed that the Culture, Parks, and Recreation  Department (CPRD) 
had been placed in the 17 percent reduction cohort with a target of approximately $3.7 million in 
cuts. Of that, approximately $2.9 million had been identified, leaving an $800,000 gap. Mr. 
Mosley stated that, at this time, the city does not expect to require CPRD to close that remaining 
gap, and that those specific program reductions are not currently part of the recommended 
budget being assembled. He framed the broader process as a robust public dialogue aimed at 
aligning spending with community priorities within available resources. 
 
In response to a question about whether the proposed cultural program cuts remained live, Mr. 
Mosley indicated that those options were brought forward by staff as discussion items for 
Council consideration, and that the final direction will come from Council at the September 11 
budget retreat. 
 
Mrs. Kellums asked whether museums are funded through the quality of life fund or the general 
fund. Mr. Mosley indicated his belief that museums are funded through the general fund and 
committed to following up via email to confirm. Mrs. Kellums requested general follow-up 
information on the quality of life fund for the Commission, which Mr. Mosley agreed to provide. 
 
A member of the public, Linde Thompson, asked whether the quality of life fund referenced in 
the presentation corresponds to the voter-approved tax from the early 2000s. Mr. Mosley 
confirmed that it does. In response to a follow-up question about whether that tax has a sunset 
provision, Mr. Mosley stated he believed the sunset clause was removed upon the last renewal 
but committed to confirming the details via email. 
 
In response to a question about the financial impact of eliminating approximately 100 positions, 
Mr. Mosley noted that the initial projection called for approximately $12 to $13 million in 
personnel-related reductions to close the bulk of the $18 million deficit. Revenue enhancements 
reduced the extent of personnel cuts required. He did not have exact figures available at the time 
but offered to provide specifics upon request. 
 
A commissioner asked about the proposed 0.5 percent sales tax increase being considered for the 
November ballot. Mr. Mosley clarified that this measure has no bearing on the general fund 
deficit discussion. If approved by voters, any such tax would be a dedicated revenue source 
restricted to a specific purpose, similar to existing dedicated taxes, and could not be used to close 
the general fund gap.

City of Greeley Historic Preservation Commission 
Greeley Historic Register 
 
Plaque Mounting Policy 
 
The bronze plaques awarded for each property listed on the Greeley Historic Register shall be mounted 
according to the following guidelines: 
 
Owners of designated properties have three options for mounting their plaques, including mounting the 
plaques on the designated property, on a monument by the sidewalk/street or on a pedestal by the 
sidewalk/street. 
 
A. Guidelines for mounting plaques on the designated building : 
 
1.  For plaques mounted on the structure itself, they must be mounted in a visibly prominent position  
on the exterior, near the main entryway, between four and six feet high.  
 
2.   Mounting the plaques shall not damage the historic character or fabric of the structure.  
 
3.  Mounting devices, such as bolts, must be attached in the following manner:  
 
For brick structures, bolts must be attached in the mortar joints, in order to protect the integrity of the 
brick 
 
For frame structures: bolts must be attached securely to the structure, with at least two bolts (an 
upper and lower on one side) attached to a stud (or other supporting member).  Care should be taken 
to do the least amount of damage to the siding, sheathing (or lath) and studs (or other supporting 
members as possible). 
 
B. Guidelines for mounting plaques on a monument by the sidewalk : 
 
1.  For plaques mounted on a monument by the sidewalk, the monument should be colored concrete, 
sandstone or granite. 
 
2. The monument should have rough-cut sides, a smooth sloping face on which the plaque will be 
mounted, and should be 10 inches high on the high end, 6 inches high on the low end of the sloping 
face, and 12 inches by 14 inches on the base.  The plaques should be mounted in the center of the 
sloping face, with a two inch border surrounding it.  An example of a plaque on a concrete 
monument is located at 1303 9th Avenue. 
 
 
3. If the owner is interested, they may get a concrete foundation with a four inch  border around the base 
of the monument.  The dimensions of the foundation should  be 20 inches by 22 inches. 
 
4.  Placement in the yard: The monument should be placed next to the sidewalk to the right of the 
sidewalk leading to the house.  
 
5.  If there is a fence, the monument should be placed outside the fence, so the plaque is readable 
from the sidewalk. 
 
The Historic Preservation Commission will cover the cost up to, but not to exceed, the cost of the 
standard plaque.  All additional costs, including those for mounting on a stone or concrete monument, 
will be assessed to the property owner. All costs related to a monument will be covered by the owner of 
the designated property.  Historic Greeley, Inc. has a mold for a monument to borrow.  Contact the 
Historic Preservation Office at 970.350.9222 for more information.

C. Guidelines for mounting plaques on a pedestal by the sidewalk : 
 
1.  The plaque may be mounted on a metal pedestal that is 3 -  3.5' above the ground.  A property 
owner may decide whether the concrete foundation will show above ground (this is not necessary 
for there to be a foundation). 
 
2.  The plaque may be mounted on the pedestal by the contractor.  
 
3.  If the property owner desires the pedestal to be painted, they may choose between brown or black 
paint. 
 
4.  Placement in the yard: The pedestal should be placed next to the sidewalk to the right of the 
sidewalk leading to the house.  
 
5.  If there is a fence, the pedestal should be placed outside the fence, so the plaque is readable from 
the sidewalk.
