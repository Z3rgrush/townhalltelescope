RECOMMENDED 2027 CIP BUDGET PREVIEW 
The proposed 2027 Capital Improvement Plan (CIP) was presented as a high- level overview of a plan previously 
reviewed by City Council, with subsequent updates. Capital requests are submitted by departments in the spring, 
assessed under a common scoring framework, and reviewed by a cross-functional steering committee. The framework 
considers community impact, quality of life, public health, master-plan alignment, asset condition, operating impacts, 
and other factors, with a maximum score of 40. Scores guide discussion rather than automatically rank projects; 
readiness, legal and practical funding constraints, cash flow, operating consequences, and Council priorities also 
determine whether a project proceeds. The plan was in final review before incorporation into the recommended budget 
and subsequent public adoption process. 
 
The principal fiscal issue is that much of the City’s capital revenue is legally or operationally restricted, including utility 
revenues, grants, and the Keep Greeley Moving tax. Accordingly, the most consequential prioritization occurs within 
a comparatively limited pool of flexible capital resources, principally food tax and quality-of-life funding. That capacity 
must accommodate three large, concurrent long- term commitments: West Greeley debt repayment, downtown 
revitalization and Civic Campus financing, and the MERGE transportation project. Although outside funding, federal 
grants, and anticipated TIFIA financing support MERGE, a substantial local contribution remains necessary. The

The recommended CIP totals approximately $204 million across roughly 150 projects and 24 funds. More than half of 
the investment is in water, sewer, and stormwater projects, with another substantial portion in transportation; these 
investments are largely financed with restricted revenues and therefore cannot simply be redirected to other priorities. 
The program continues to fund investments across City services, including downtown stormwater and utility work, 
MERGE, playground replacement, Recreation Center pool work, parks and recreation, economic development, City-
facility maintenance, the Greeley-Weld Airport, and the first West Greeley debt payment. The Airport project includes 
a $7 million food-tax investment in 2027. 
 
To balance the five- year capital plan, the recommendation includes a temporary 25 percent reduction in annual 
maintenance and replacement funding for three years. In 2027, this reduces annual funding from about $5.6 million 
to $4.2 million, a reduction of approximately $1.4 million. Departments prioritized remaining dollars toward the highest 
operational risks, but some HVAC replacements, facility improvements, irrigation systems, and similar projects will be 
deferred or phased. Deferral reflects a timing and risk -management decision rather than a determination that the 
projects are unnecessary. Departments will continue to monitor asset condition and operational risk, while the CIP will 
be updated annually to reflect changes in construction inflation, inter est rates, revenue performance, grant timing, 
project costs, and asset needs. 
 
An additional recommendation was developed after identifying approximately $3 million of available fund balance in 
the Public Improvement Fund, largely from unrestricted investment earnings. The proposal would use this one- time 
funding for three previously  prioritized deferred projects: a Recreation Center HVAC replacement, Family FunPlex 
locker-room updates, and an Active Adult Center HVAC replacement. The approach preserves the established 
prioritization process and matches one-time resources to one-time capital uses. It does not eliminate the remaining 
deferred-project list or reverse the recommended temporary maintenance reduction. Council was expected to consider 
these additions during its budget discussion. 
 
Further discussion focused on the scale and timing of the three major debt commitments. MERGE is being modeled 
with approximately $65 million in principal financing, potentially through TIFIA, with uncertainty over whether the 
project will receive a lower rural interest rate because of limited federal availability. The project cost range was 
described as approximately $160 million to $190 million as design advances. The current planning assumption is 
roughly $5 million annually in MERGE debt service over 20 years, beginning around 2029 or 2030. Obtaining favorable 
TIFIA terms and coordinating advocacy through appropriate channels were identified as important because the 
interest rate materially affects borrowing capacity and the local funding gap. 
 
Civic Campus financing was described as approximately $84 million in debt, with capitalized interest during 
construction and no payments in 2027 or 2028. Debt service would begin in 2029 at approximately $4 million for 
several years before increasing to about $8.4 million annually over a 20-year term. West Greeley debt repayment was 
estimated at approximately $95 million, with a phased repayment structure and an eventual annual cost estimated in 
the range of $8 million to $9 million. West Greeley payments are currently modeled across food-tax and quality-of-life 
resources; absent another solution, General Fund support may eventually be required. At their peak, the combined 
annual debt-service burden for these commitments could approach $25 million, significantly limiting flexibility for large 
cash-funded capital projects. 
 
The discussion also addressed the relationship between West Greeley infrastructure and future utility growth. New 
ratepayers would contribute revenue to the utility enterprises over time, but the water enterprise has not assumed the 
current infrastructure cost because the work was viewed as being driven by the Catalyst project rather than by a 
normal, independently timed utility need. A previously considered half -cent sales -tax increase was estimated to 
generate about $16 million and could have relieved capital debt-repayment pressure, allowing more deferred projects 
to move into funded status; Council did not advance that measure. Potential future strategies discussed included 
reauthorization of Keep Greeley Moving, improved revenue performance, and careful consideration of how any future 
capacity should be allocated. 
 
The committee emphasized that the City must continue investing in renewal and replacement of existing assets rather 
than automatically shifting future revenue gains toward new programs or projects. The deferred- project list is 
maintained and can be revisited by the standing steering committee as funding changes. Cash-flow management and 
project readiness were also identified as areas for continued improvement, especially when long equipment lead times 
require funds to be encumbered well before a project can be completed. The committee was asked to provide 
observations and feedback for Council, particularly on support for the general approach and on prioritizing critical 
asset repair and replacement when additional one-time resources become available. 
 
CITY MANAGERS 2027 RECOMMENDED BUDGET PREVIEW 
A preliminary overview of the 2027 Recommended Operating Budget was provided before publication of the full budget 
document. The central objective is to address the City’s structural General Fund deficit while preserving core services 
to the greatest practical extent. Departments used service data, service- level reviews, position evaluations, and 
efficiency assessments to develop their proposals. Positions were evaluated for rehiring, deferral, or elimination, and 
voluntary separation agreements were used to give employees an opportunity to self-select while helping departments 
finalize reductions. The budget process is intended to move the City away from continued reliance on one- time 
resources and deficit spending. 
 
The financial outlook showed that recurring expenditures had exceeded recurring revenues since the 2022– 2023 
period, while reserves had been used to close the gap. The prior projection placed year -end 2026 reserves at the 
$16.7M minimum requirement. The recommended budget is expected to maintain required reserves and modestly 
improve the number in 2027, in part because the overall budget base is smaller. The presentation emphasized that 
reserve and revenue- stabilization funds should not be used to avoid ongoing spending decisions; these funds are 
intended to protect the City against extraordinary revenue shocks, emergencies, or events outside the City’s control 
rather than to sustain routine services indefinitely. 
 
Updated assumptions reflect audited 2025 actuals, the adopted 2026 budget, health- plan cost pressures, and 
department submissions. Health premiums are projected to increase about 10 percent, and a one- time $1.5 million 
transfer to the health fund is included to keep the self-insured plan solvent. The City is also reevaluating health-plan 
cost controls and the frequency and structure of self -insurance review. No recurring salary increase is assumed for 
2027; however, the recommendation includes one- time bonuses of 2 percent for regular staff and 1 percent for 
leadership to help offset health-premium impacts without increasing the ongoing salary base. Future years assume 3 
percent salary increases. The recently approved Fire Department union contract is also included. 
 
Department proposals total approximately $16.8 million to $17 million in reductions, compared with an original $18.1 
million target. Updated information indicates that the remaining approximate $1.3 million gap does not need to be 
closed. As a result, Culture, Parks and Recreation would be held to an approximately 13 percent reduction rather than 
being required to make further reductions to reach an earlier, higher target. The presentation noted that target 
percentages varied among departments and that the final package reflects differing service demands, funding 
opportunities, and departmental circumstances rather than a uniform percentage applied to every unit. 
 
The preliminary all -funds 2027 budget includes approximately $481 million in revenues, excluding transfers, and 
approximately $503.7 million in expenditures. The General Fund portion includes about $144.5 million in revenue and 
$137.7 million in expenditur es, excluding transfers. The apparent all -funds difference is not an operating deficit 
because it reflects fund-balance timing and the fact that revenues may be collected in one year and spent in another. 
The presentation stressed that enterprise and other restricted funds support substantial activity but cannot generally 
be used to solve the General Fund’s operating challenge. A clearer separation of General Fund and all -funds 
information was identified as important for public-facing budget communications. 
 
The recommended plan reduces General Fund full -time equivalents by 72.75 positions, with a net General Fund 
reduction of 67.75 after five positions are shifted to other funding sources, including Conservation Trust, stormwater, 
and water. The overall City workforce would decline from above 1,200 FTEs to approximately 1,195. The changes 
include reductions as well as revenue-supported funding shifts, and the reduction was characterized as a significant 
but necessary organizational response after substantial staffing growth in recent years. 
 
The revised multi -year forecast shows continued projected deficit spending of approximately $3.9 million in 2027, 
largely driven by one-time items. Those include $1 million for the homeless shelter, approximately $1.3 million for one-
time employee bonuses, and funding that allows some departments to avoid additional cuts, including approximately 
$800,000 for Culture, Parks and Recreation and $150,000 restored to the Police budget for the downtown precision 
contract. The forecast then shows projected surpluses of roughly $400,000 in 2028, $862,000 in 2029, and about $3 
million to $3.6 million in later years. These projections remain subject to economic, labor, health- cost, and revenue 
variables, and the presentation cautioned against treating future surpluses as immediately available for restoration of 
all prior reductions or for new ongoing commitments. 
 
The committee identified the before-and-after multi-year financial trend graphs as essential to explaining the budget: 
they show that the City is changing course, even though correction will take time and will involve difficult effects. 
Concern was express ed about scope creep during Council deliberations, particularly the possibility of adding 
unplanned expenditures without identifying offsetting reductions or sustainable funding. The recommended discussion 
structure for Council would present the package, s eek consensus on whether it materially addresses the financial 
problem, allow individual concerns to be raised, and focus deliberation on items with sufficient Council consensus. 
The budget schedule included release of the recommended budget, a Council budget retreat, public hearings, and 
adoption in October or November; Colorado law allows adoption by December 15, providing some flexibility in the 
schedule. 
 
CBAC COMMUNICATIONS SUBCOMMITTEE UPDATE 
The Communications Subcommittee reported a productive initial discussion focused on improving communication 
between the Citizens’ Budget Advisory Committee (CBAC), the City Council, and the broader Greeley community. A 
key takeaway was that CBAC members may be well positioned to serve as a trusted conduit between residents and 
City leadership by providing context about budget -related issues, listening to neighborhood concerns, and helping 
residents better understand City processes and available information. The discussion recognized that many residents 
may not know who serves on CBAC or understand its advisory role, creating an opportunity for members to increase 
awareness through everyday community conversations and more intentional outreach. 
 
Potential outreach strategies included meeting with individual City Council members, coordinating with neighborhood 
or community boards, and possibly holding quarterly public-facing meetings similar to Council coffee conversations. 
The purpose of these efforts would be to establish a more consistent and accessible standard for public engagement, 
particularly during a challenging budget year. Participants also discussed leveraging existing City communication tools 
rather than creating entirely new channels. In particular, Speak Up Greeley was identified as a valuable existing 
resource because it includes budget information and other City topics. Ideas included developing informational cards 
that CBAC members could share with residents, as well as distributing materials through realtors or newcomer packets 
to help new residents locate reliable City information. 
 
The subcommittee also received positive feedback on frequently asked questions and discussed improving their 
organization so residents can more easily find answers at the appropriate level of detail. Clear, navigable information 
would allow residents to quickly address common questions while also giving them the ability to explore budget and 
policy matters more deeply. The overall goal is to make City information more understandable and accessible without 
relying solely on formal communications or creating the perception that outreach is one-directional or promotional. 
 
The discussion emphasized that CBAC members can help add credibility and balance to public conversations. 
Members may be able to identify misunderstandings, correct inaccurate information, and provide practical context in 
a way that feels more peer-to-peer than official City messaging. At the same time, their feedback can help City Council 
and staff better understand resident concerns and make more informed decisions. Members were encouraged to 
contact their Council representatives or other relevant board r epresentatives when they want to discuss issues or 
request information, as these conversations are considered an important part of public engagement. 
 
A concern was raised regarding the appropriate scope of individual member outreach. Because CBAC is a budget 
committee, members should avoid creating the impression that they are being directed to advocate for a particular 
revenue measure, budget position,  or City-preferred outcome. Members retain their rights and responsibilities as 
individual Greeley residents and may communicate personal views to their ward representatives. However, they 
should clearly distinguish when they are speaking as private residents versus when they are conveying a formal CBAC 
position. If CBAC has reached consensus on an issue, members should accurately represent that collective position; 
where there is no consensus, members should avoid implying that their individual perspective represents the 
committee as a whole. 
 
The group acknowledged that CBAC members effectively wear two hats: one as individual residents bringing diverse 
viewpoints to the committee, and another as members of a collective advisory body that may occasionally adopt a 
shared recommendation. Dissenting or minority perspectives may also be appropriate when clearly identified. The 
Communications Subcommittee’s work remains in the early stages, with no finalized outreach plan or required action 
for members. Further discussion is needed to define what information should be communicated, establish appropriate 
guardrails, and ensure any community engagement activities are helpful, accurate, and aligned with CBAC’s advisory 
role rather than extending beyond its budget-focused purpose. 
 
CBAC BUDGET RECOMMENDATION DISCUSSION 
The committee discussed preparing a written statement for Council in advance of the B udget Retreat and as a 
foundation for its later formal recommendation. The proposed themes were to honor the budget-development process; 
recognize the substantial, difficult work completed by staff; acknowledge that adjustment is necessary because 
maintaining the  status quo is not financially sustainable; and affirm confidence in the professional analysis and 
recommendations that produced the proposed budget. Members supported explaining the reasoning behind the 
statement rather than offering only a brief endorsement. 
 
The discussion emphasized that the proposed budget seeks to distribute responsibility across the organization rather 
than place the entire adjustment on a single department, service, or employee group. Members recognized that 
impacts are not identical in every area but viewed the package as a broad organizational response developed through 
an established process. The committee also noted that the financial trajectory if no changes were made was known 
to be unsustainable, whereas the recommended changes provide a path to course correction that can be monitored 
and adjusted as experience and new information become available. 
 
Key points proposed for the statement included: maintaining fiscal discipline; avoiding additional spending unless 
corresponding reductions or sustainable revenue are identified; protecting long- term financial stability; maintaining 
reserve requirements an d revenue- stabilization capacity; and respecting Council’s fiduciary responsibility while 
cautioning against undermining the professional work and analysis underlying the budget. The committee also 
discussed the value of the financial trend graphs in helpi ng nontechnical audiences understand both the severity of 
the prior trajectory and the improvement expected from the recommended plan. 
 
Potential new revenue ideas, including sponsorships and naming opportunities, were discussed as concepts 
previously raised through internal innovation efforts, but no specific proposal had been adopted. The effect of oil and 
gas revenue was also addressed.  Oil and gas revenue is not a dominant share of the budget but is volatile; the 
forecast assumes no growth and separates oil and gas property-tax revenue from regular property tax so that different 
assumptions can be applied. The committee agreed that a draft statement should be refined, circulated for review/edit/ 
and approval via email, so it may be presented to the Council in advance of the Council Budget Retreat on September 
11, 2026, with any lack of full consensus clearly documented, if necessary.

CBAC Statement to City Council — FY2027 Budget  
 
The Citizen Budget Advisory Committee (CBAC) has strong conﬁdence in the work of City staﬀ 
and the remarkable eﬀort that went into developing the FY2027 budget. We believe the 
process was thoughtful, thorough, and necessary, and that Council should honor that work 
while recognizing its responsibility to evaluate impacts and consider adjustments as warranted.  
CBAC encourages Council to:  
• Trust the process and the work of staﬀ that developed the proposed budget.  
• Recognize that the burden is being shared across the organization and that reductions 
are based on relative priority to protect core services and minimize impact to quality of 
life to the greatest extent possible.  
• Reinforce that meaningful change is necessary; the city cannot continue with the status 
quo and that comes with supporting the hard decisions made in this budget process.  
• Exercise its ﬁduciary responsibility  by maintaining a focus on long -term ﬁnancial 
sustainability.  
• Evaluate impacts and adjust going forward when experience or new information 
demonstrates that changes are warranted.  
• Maintain ﬁscal discipline by not adding expenditures without identifying corresponding 
reductions or sustainable funding elsewhere.  
• Be mindful of the trust placed in staﬀ and avoid decisions that unintentionally undermine 
the diﬃcult work and progress made through the budget process.  
CBAC believes the FY2027 budget represents a responsible and substantial step toward 
addressing the City’s ﬁscal challenges and building a more sustainable ﬁnancial future.
