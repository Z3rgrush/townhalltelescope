Coal Creek Business Park Land (Parcel BB) 
Property Features: Approximately 5 acres. A small but high-value riparian parcel 
adjacent to Coal Creek with trail connectivity along the Coal Creek Trail. 
Property Goals: Protect the riparian nature and rich habitat of the property. Provide a 
buffer against nearby commercial development. 
OSAB Recommendation: OSAB recognizes that the property is zoned Commercial 
Business and recommends preservation of acreage adjacent to Coal Creek Trail. 
 
 
Baseline Horse Property (Parcel OO) 
Property Features: Approximately 34 acres. A large, scenic, and ecologically rich site 
that includes a barn and farmhouse on land long used for agriculture. It offers critical 
wildlife habitat, an irrigation ditch, trail connectivity options, and buffering opportunities. 
Bordered by Lafayette and Boulder County Open Spaces, which connect to Louisville’s 
North Open Space. 
Property Goals: Retain the rural nature of the property. Increase open space acreage 
and provide continuity with adjoining open space properties. 
OSAB Recommendation: OSAB recommends that the city work with Lafayette and 
Boulder County to negotiate a conservation easement, perhaps with the right to develop 
two custom home properties along the northwestern edge. 
 
 
Southwest of GHX (Parcel ZZ.1) 
Property Features: Approximately 5 acres. Bordered to the west by a powerline 
easement and Davidson Mesa Open Space. 
Property Goals: Retain the rural nature of the property. Expand Davidson Mesa Open 
Space and enhance trail access opportunities as the surrounding area becomes more 
developed. 
OSAB Recommendation: OSAB recognizes that the property is zoned Planned 
Community Commercial and recommends preservation of contiguous acreage and 
potential trail connectivity to Davidson Mesa through the development process. An ideal 
location for an additional trailhead to reduce overuse of the McCaslin trailhead.

Strategies and Tools for Open Space Property Acquisition 
12/10/2015 Revised 2/4/2026 
Summary: acquisition discussions between OSAB, City Manager Malcolm Fleming, and OS staff (with input from Louisville, Boulder 
County & Jefferson County Open Space staff)  
Tool  How it works  Advantages  Disadvantages 
Real Estate 
Retainer  
While many large open space agencies 
have staff dedicated to land 
acquisitions, this is impractical or 
unfeasible for  smaller agencies due to 
cost and infrequency of  opportunities. 
A smaller municipality could extend its 
reach  through an established network 
of real estate brokers in the  area. Real 
estate brokers tend to be the first 
individuals  with knowledge of potential 
land coming on the market for  sale, and 
may have relationships with landowners 
in the  area. A municipality could 
provide a select group of brokers with  
its target acquisition list either to  
pursue transactions, or simply to   keep 
the municipality abreast of potential 
target  acquisition opportunities. It is 
typical in the real estate  industry for a 
seller to pay all brokerage   
commissions/compensation. 
Expands the reach of smaller 
municipalities’ beyond staff 
resources at minimal additional 
costs, and  may result in 
additional opportunities. 
May result in a 
conflict if multiple 
brokers identify the 
same target  
acquisition at the 
same time, an  event 
more common in a 
smaller  municipality 
real estate market. 
Right of first 
refusal/Option 
Agreement 
The right of first refusal is a contract 
between the buyer and seller that 
specifies that the land may be acquired 
by the  buyer at a future date. This gives 
the municipality the  opportunity to 
match an offered purchase price within 
a  specified time period should a 
landowner receive a  legitimate offer to 
sell. A purchase option is simply a right  
that the municipality holds to purchase 
the land by a  specified date at a 
specified price. A right of first refusal  
and a purchase option can be either 
donated to the  municipality or sold. 
Keeps the opportunity open, 
keeps the dialogue going, 
inexpensive. 
Money paid to the 
seller for the right of 
first refusal/option is  
forfeited if the city 
cannot or will  not 
purchase the land at 
the  specified time 
and price. 
Fee Simple 
Acquisition  
Most acquisitions of open space have 
historically been fee simple purchases. 
Fee acquisition has the 
advantage of giving the city full 
control over the management 
of the  properties’ resources, 
and provides the greatest  
flexibility for decision making 
about the best  ways to address 
visitor access, agricultural  
management, ecological 
restoration, and other  
management issues. 
Most expensive of 
all acquisition tools 
Page 10 of 55
Conservation 
Easements  
Legal restriction voluntarily placed on a 
property by its owner. Enforcement 
rights are granted to a public agency or  
charitable organization. Easement is 
customized to meet  landowner needs, 
including retention of certain rights. 
Landowners can protect land in 
perpetuity while maintaining 
ownership. There are significant  
savings on taxes including 
property, income, and  estate 
taxes. Landowners are 
motivated to  donate CEs 
because Colorado allows 
transferable  tax credits for 
qualified donations. Currently, a  
CE donor can earn up to 
$375,000 in state  income tax 
credits. Under policies now in 
effect,  these credits can be 
carried forward for up to 20  
years and used as needed to 
offset state income  tax 
payments, or sold to others. 
Conservation 
easements are 
irrevocable and rights 
included are  no 
longer an option for 
the landowner. 
Trail Easements  A partial interest in a property is 
granted to allow entry onto another 
landowner’s property. Trail facilities are  
developed within a designated area, to 
allow users onto the  corridor to use the 
trail. Negotiation between trail  
managers and owners usually occurs. 
Owners may be  willing to allow access 
for a fee or donation of the  easement.  
Easement acquisition is 
usually cheaper than outright 
purchase of land. There are 
fewer  disruptions of existing 
land uses. 
Tensions can arise 
between entities 
regarding terms or 
covenants.  Term 
easements can cause   
problems if the 
landowner does not 
choose  to renew the 
easement. 
Parkland/Trail 
Dedication  
City requires developers and builders 
to dedicate park/trail lands or pay a fee 
that is used to acquire and develop 
park  and trail facilities. This exaction 
fee is a way to offset  increased 
demand for parks or trails created by  
developer/new homeowner 
Cities can conserve open 
spaces at the pace of land 
development. Developers can 
negotiate to  construct 
facilities, saving costs to both 
parties. 
Although courts 
generally uphold this 
type of exaction, it 
could result  in 
litigation for requiring   
payment/land 
dedication. 
Bargain Sale of 
Land  
An agreement is created to sell land for 
less than fair market value between City 
and landowner. 
Potential tax benefits exist 
such as charitable donations 
and a reduction in capital 
gains tax.  Sellers are often 
motivated to see land  
preserved. 
Less profit for seller 
than selling at fair 
market value. 
Conservation  value 
restraints may limit 
agency  ability to 
purchase property.

Excerpt from the Trails System Plan 
Undesignated (Social) Trails 
Going forward, the PROS Department will consider the following conditions, as outlined in 
the social trail resolution ﬂowchart, when determining which undesignated (social) trails 
should be closed and revegetated. These conditions apply only to undesignated (social) 
trails. Existing formalized trails will remain in place and will not be closed or revegetated, 
regardless of Land Classiﬁcation. 
• Undesignated (social) trails leading to, or located within, the Preserve Land 
Classiﬁcation shall be closed and revegetated as funding and resources allow. 
• Undesignated (social) trails that impact or impede City operations shall be closed 
and revegetated.  
• Undesignated (social) trails identiﬁed in a plan for future trail development, will 
remain in place until advancing to the trail design process (Section 5.2). This 
includes trails that fall into the following categories: 
o Trails identiﬁed in the Transportation Master Plan, Open Space Master Plan, 
or the Trail System Plan. 
o Trail alignments approved by City Council through a Capital Improvement 
Project or other adopted planning document.  
o Corridors identiﬁed by staff for potential future trail connections that require 
additional public input.  
• Remaining undesignated (social) trails, including those in the Protect, Visitor, and 
Other Open Land Classiﬁcations, will be further evaluated for restoration and 
revegetation prioritization based on ﬁve factors outlined in the evaluation matrix: 
safety, wildlife habitat quality, trail saturation, erosion, and invasive weeds.  
By applying a consistent process for evaluating undesignated (social) trails and using a 
quantitative method to prioritize revegetation efforts, projects will be comparable across 
the trail system and resources can be directed toward the highest-priority management 
needs.  
For example, undesignated trails that present signiﬁcant safety concerns or substantial 
resource impacts may receive a higher priority for closure and revegetation than trails with 
fewer impacts. The resulting priority ranking should inform, but not dictate, implementation 
timing. Completion of management actions will continue to depend on available funding, 
staffing, partnership opportunities, and other operational considerations. Implementation 
schedules should be established and adjusted by staff as part of ongoing work planning.

 Organize and lead education programs
 Supports Open Space operations
 Perform foot, bicycle, and vehicle patrol 
of parks and open spaces.
 Partial code enforcement commission 
with Louisville Police Department
 Limited by policy to open space and parks 
sections of the Municipal Code.
 Directed by Council and CMO for an 
“educational” approach.

By the Numbers
In 2025:
 More than 375 miles of foot patrol
 About 834 miles of bike patrol
 More than 10,000 friendly “public relations” 
contacts with trail users and visitors to parks 
and open spaces.
 About 830 law enforcement or emergency 
response contacts.
 21 education programs and 10 booths bringing 
1,050 attendees.

Value for the PD?
 Rangers divert enforcement calls from parks and open 
space that would otherwise go to code or patrol officers
 Act as eyes and ears on trails away from areas easily 
accessed by vehicle.
 Can assist officers with:
 Transporting bikes, bulky items, etc.
 Information about wildlife, OS issues
 Liaising with CPW
 Missing person, SAR calls
 Found property reports for items on OS and parks
 Staffing road/lane closures for traffic incidents or events
 Other case/incident reports on parks and OS
 Escaped livestock

Support from the PD?
 Rangers receive shift support from 
Police supervisors for enforcement-
related matters.
 Equipment – radios, light bars, 
Bodyworn cameras, etc.
 Use of the Police Department’s 
records and property and evidence 
management systems
 Officers provide cover and emergency 
response for rangers in the field

Successes and Challenges
 Program Success
 Generalist nature of the position lends 
itself to great support of team 
objectives
 Work closures – grazing, trail 
maintenance, etc.
 Education
 Reduction in staff harassment 
 Formalizing the program – Ranger Policy 
Manual
 Positive communication – resource for 
the community
 Increased protection of Natural 
Resources 
 Trail-based patrol is more effective than 
code or police patrolling from vehicles
 Appeal to the public of education 
programs with uniformed staff
 Program Challenges
 Roles & program objectives can change 
with political bodies
 High turn-over position 
 Non-ranger managers need to quickly 
learn the legal and safety components 
of the job
 Relationship with PD & Emergency 
Response
 Balancing a diverse and wide set of 
duties
 Safety concern for rangers making 
enforcement contacts with more limited 
defensive tools
