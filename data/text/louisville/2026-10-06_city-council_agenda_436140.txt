A RESOLUTION APPROVING A FIRST AMENDED EMPLOYMENT AGREEMENT 
FOR CITY MANAGER WITH DIANA LANGLEY 
 
WHEREAS, by that Employment Agreement for City Manager dated February 11, 
2025, the City engaged Diana  Langley’s services as City Manager and Ms. Langley 
began her employment with the City on March 31, 2025; and  
 
WHEREAS, the City desires to continue to employ the services of Diana Langley 
as the City Manager of the City of Louisville, Colorado; and  
  
 WHEREAS, the City and Ms. Langley desire to set forth certain specifics 
concerning her employment by the City as City Manager, and to establish conditions of 
employment related thereto, as set forth in the First Amended Employment Agreement 
that accompanies this resolution; and  
 
 WHEREAS, by this Resolution the City Council desires to approve the Amended 
Agreement.    
 
 NOW, THEREFORE, BE IT RESOLVED BY THE CITY COUNCIL OF THE CITY 
OF LOUISVILLE, COLORADO: 
 
 Section 1. The City Council hereby approves the First Amended Employment  
Agreement for City Manager by and between the City of Louisville and Diana Langley (the 
“Amended Agreement”) in substantially the same form as the copy of such Amended 
Agreement accompanying this Resolution.   
 
 Section 2. The Mayor is hereby authorized to execute the Amended Agreement 
on behalf of the City, and the Mayor is hereby further authorized to negotiate and approve 
such revisions to the Amended Agreement as the Mayor determines are necessary or 
desirable for the protection of the City, so long as the essential terms and conditions of 
the Amended Agreement are not altered.

Role Review - Overview
 Quasi-Judicial: A “Quasi Judge” role where there is determination of rights,
duties or obligations of a specific individual or entity. Facts are developed at a
hearing and decisions are based on the application of current standards and
policies to the facts at the hearing. Quasi-judicial matters have specific
requirements and procedures, and B/C members must be a neutral and impartial
decision maker, applying current standards to facts in the record.
 Advisory: Advisory to Council via general rules or policies within your subject
area. These should be forward-looking, policy-making recommendations that are
generally applicable to and affect a large group of community members and/or
properties.

Agenda Item 8A – Model Traffic Code Ordinance
Question: The local amendment to Section 1101(1) replaces the model code’s
requirement to drive at a speed that is “reasonable and prudent” under existing
conditions with a prohibition on exceeding the maximum lawful speed limit. It
obviously makes sense to prohibit exceeding the limit, but I am curious why we would
want to remove the reasonable and prudent language, which seems to be an additional
safeguard. It also appears to me that state law may limit our ability to modify this
language—section 1102(2) provides that "A local authority shall not alter the basic
rules set forth in section 42-4-1101(1) . . ."
Answer (City Prosecutor Tricia McCarthy): “Reasonable and prudent is not
defined by law and is difficult for a court to determine what would qualify.
 
Speed limits are set by traffic engineers based upon concrete data and adding
this language eliminates that inconsistency.
 
 I disagree with the council's analysis
that 1102(2) prohibits changes to speed limits.
 
 That section actually allows it.”
Question: The same section would establish an unposted speed limit of 25 mph in
residential districts and 30 mph in business districts. The state defaults in 1101 appear
to be the reverse: 30 mph residential and 25 mph business.
Answer (Melinda Culley):  I reviewed the City’s prior ordinances adopting the
Model Traffic Code (MTC), and the 30 mph speed limit in business districts has
been in place since at least 2003. 
 
State law expressly allows municipalities to
alter the default speed limits, which is what the City has chosen to do in the
past. 
 
Note that the default speed limit only applies when speed limit signs are
not posted, so this may not affect that many streets. 
 
I don’t object to changing
the speed limit, but it may be helpful to discuss this further with Public Works
and the PD before making the change.
 
 
  
Question: I understand that the ordinance adopts the published 2024 edition of the
model code, subject to our local amendments. It looks like some subsequent state-law
updates were considered as part of our amendments (e.g., HB25-1112). I am curious as
to whether other updates to state law were also considered, especially the following
safety updates that may be relevant to us: (Answers from Melinda Culley)
SB24-065 (mobile devices) - Response: We can add this change as a second
reading amendment. 
HB26-1237 (bike lane protections) - Response: This law only recently became
effective, but we can get it added as a second reading amendment. 
HB26-1318 (school zones) - Response: We don’t think it’s necessary to address
this in the MTC ordinance right now.  The law exempts existing school zones so
long as they’re at least 200 feet from a school.  Public Works and the PD may
want to review existing school zones to ensure they are sufficient. 
SB25-281 (careless driving involving serious injury or death) - Response: The City
Prosecutor indicated it’s fairly unlikely this type of charge would be brought in
Municipal Court.
SB26-035 (repeat and excessive speeding) - Response: Yes, the intent is to
change the misdemeanor threshold from 20 to 25 mph for the reasons
referenced in the Council memo.  After the packet was published, we realized
the ordinance in the packet was not the most current draft. I believe the
ordinance has been updated now.  Sorry for the mistake!
 
I am just looking for clarification on whether these were considered as part of the
update, and any context around that consideration.
 
Question: The memo says the misdemeanor threshold would move from 20 to 25 mph
over the limit, but proposed Municipal Code Section 10.04.060.D.2 appears to retain
the 20 mph threshold. Is that right? I likely favor keeping 20 but want to make sure I am
understanding the ordinance correctly.  
Answer (Melinda Culley): Yes, the intent is to change the misdemeanor threshold
from 20 to 25 mph for the reasons referenced in the Council memo.  After the
packet was published, we realized the ordinance in the packet was not the most
current draft. I believe the ordinance has been updated now.  Sorry for the
mistake!
 
Agenda Item 5E – Development Code Update
Question: It will not surprise you that I have some sticker shock on the $1M / 4,916-
hour proposal for the development code update. May we please see the other
responses to the RFP?
 
While I understand there are probably important differences in scope, I found the
following comparison points online:
 
Palm Desert, CA — 2024 (population approximately 51,000): $428,080 and 2,120
hours for a Clarion unified development code update, including analysis,
drafting, development standards, testing and adoption.
Evans, CO — 2022 (population approximately 22,000): $240,000, including
contingency, and 1,514 hours for a land-use code update and housing market
study.
Colorado Springs, CO — 2019–2023 (population approximately 479,000): a
reported $369,940 Clarion budget for its zoning and subdivision code rewrite.
 
I also noticed that approximately $350,000 and 1,906 hours (nearly a full person-
year) are allocated to the first two phases (i.e., before code drafting begins). Have
we taken a hard look at ensuring this does not duplicate efforts already completed
through the Comprehensive Plan, Housing Plan, or Community Survey?
 
Answer (Rob Zuccaro): Our base budget is $500k, similar to some of the example
budgets provided by Councilmember Hoefner.  The main reason the budget is
this high is we were able to get two grants focused on Affordable Housing and
Sustainability that brought the budget up significantly.  Because of these grants,
we have asked the consultants to have additional subconsultants and expanded
scope dedicated to each topic area covered by the grants.  Meeting the grant
obligations will take significantly more work than a more typical code update,
and we hope the grants will add value to the project overall in meeting the City’s
Affordable Housing and Sustainability goals.  We are also keeping a contingency
of $136k, so actual tasked scope is $864,000. 
 
Staff spent significant time in reviewing the proposals and interviewing the
different consultants understanding that several recent consultant projects
have not resulted in what we found to be acceptable work products.  We
also worked with Clarion to modify their scope and bring on a preferred
Sustainability consultant to further refine what we felt was the best overall
proposal and consultant team.  Staff’s recommendation is based on the
consultant’s significant experience in drafting code updates, their willingness
to adjust the scope to match the City’s desired outcomes, and the consultant
demonstrating the most flexibility and local knowledge to make the project
successful. Some of the proposals and consultant teams did not demonstrate
the type of flexibility desired during the interview process or demonstrate
adequate focus on Affordable Housing and Sustainability compared to the
Clarion proposal. We also negotiated the fee down from their original
proposal to have a contingency that may or may not need to be spent. 
 
We have also had multiple direct discussions with Clarion about building off
of and not duplicating the Comp Plan, Housing Plan, and community input
that was received through these recent efforts and believe they have a good
understanding of this expectation that is reflected in the scope.  Staff’s
experience with community and Council expectations is that a high touch
approach is needed to get targeted input to build off of these foundational
plans, including working with Council, boards, commissions, focus groups,
and the public as part of this project that likely requires a higher budget as
compared to other comparison code updates.  We feel that the base scope
and budget reflect the high touch approach, plus the contingency is available
to supplement outreach if needed or directly by Council.    
 
Answer (Jeff Hirt): For context, the nine proposals we received were:
 
Clarion Associates: $1,001,000
PlaceMakers: $999,000
Logan Simpson: $994,000
Code Studio: $975,000
TPUDC: $970,520
Rundell Ernstberger: $969,000
Kimley-Horn: $937,000
Multistudio: $825,000
Kendig Keast: $715,000
 
The proposals themselves are probably the best comparison for what the
market considers a reasonable cost for Louisville’s particular scope. Seven of
the nine were between $937,000 and $1 million. As Rob noted, we
subsequently negotiated Clarion’s committed scope down to $864,000, with
the remaining $136,000 retained as contingency rather than committed
upfront.
 
I also think there are several important differences between our project and
some of the code updates cited. We are not just rewriting the Development
Code; we are also comprehensively updating four separate sets of
supplemental design standards and guidelines, which adds a substantial
amount of drafting, coordination and testing. We also intentionally built in
three iterations of each major code deliverable, rather than a more typical
draft/final approach, because we expect substantial review and refinement
will be necessary to get the regulations right.
 
Another significant/unique component is Louisville’s legacy zoning
framework. The scope includes evaluating how the new code will interact
with existing PUDs, GDPs and other legacy approvals, and potential
approaches such as City-initiated rezoning or transitioning older zoning
frameworks. That is a substantial issue that many code updates leave to
implementation after adoption, which can result in significant
nonconformities and administrative complexity.
 
The housing and sustainability grants also expand the scope in substantive
and unique ways. Each has specific deliverables and requires additional
market analysis, sustainability analysis, testing and specialized consultant
expertise. The project also includes a high level of interaction with Council,
Planning Commission and targeted focus groups, along with multiple
opportunities to review and refine the draft regulations. As Rob noted, the
intent is to build from and not revisit the policy direction and community
engagement completed through the Comprehensive Plan and Housing Plan.
 
One useful recent comparison is Evanston, Illinois, which selected Code
Studio in 2026 for a zoning rewrite at approximately $900,000. Evanston
staff specifically noted that a lower-cost competing proposal included less
engagement, scenario testing and fewer code iterations. While the projects
are not identical, it illustrates how the level of testing, iteration and
engagement can materially affect the cost of a comprehensive code rewrite.
 
Overall, I think the combination of the nine proposals we received and the
scope differences above provides good context for why our project is
budgeted at this level.
 
Please let me know if you have any additional questions.

Dear Mayor Leh and Members of City Council, and Staff,
I am writing to request a full economic analysis and additional community input be
provided PRIOR to approving a lease or purchase of a portion of 1155 Pine St lot, and
the Rail Cars.  
While no one doubts Mr. Ramos’ passion for this project, being unable to ‘afford it’ is
not a valid reason for the city to proceed.  I am requesting full and transparent
analysis, and open dialog.   Many in our community are facing severe financial
pressures, and this is an unequitable and dangerous precedent to set. 
First, by the Staff’s evaluation, the 0.26 entire property, including the house and
railcars, is valued between $443,000 to $537,000, and was purchased by the Ramos
family in 2023 for $493,000 (see attached Parks and Public Landscaping Board Packet
Oct 1, 2025).   This begs the question - how was the $396,000 purchase price
derived?  The proposal is to purchase 36% of the land, and railcars.  The seller would
retain the home, and 64% of the land.  The math isn’t working.   
Second, while the railcars may evoke sentimental value for some, they do not meet the
minimum requirements for restoration by the Historical Preservation Commission, as
recently ruled on September 15, 2025.   Specifically, the structures “do not meet the
standards for social or architectural significance” AND “the current location provides a
false sense of historical significance to Louisville”.  (See attached staff
recommendations from Historic Preservation Commission Packet Sept 15, 2025). 
 Sentiment is not reason enough to pull $396,000, or 3.3% of the balance, from the
general fund.  Our city has many needs.  A full cost/benefit is required.   Do residents
want to pay for railcars, or more open space fire mitigation?  Let’s have a discussion. 
Third, while this proposal is limited to purchasing the railcars and triangle of land, the
results are long-term commitment of taxpayer funds for preservation and
maintenance.  By the seller’s own estimate, the cost to renovate the rail cars is more
than $355,000 (see attached Grant Request for railcar renovation submitted to the
Historical Preservation Commission on Sept 15, 2025).    Some work is complete,
however it’s evident that the rail cars need a great deal more attention.
Fourth, the purchase also includes unspecified costs for a survey, plat, subdivision and
closing costs, including title insurance, fees and taxes.  In addition, this doesn’t
account for additional costs to landscape, irrigate and maintain this new public space,
signage, insurance and security.   Other than the sidewalk which dead-ends on Hwy
42, this is not a pedestrian friendly area and offers no public parking lot.   How much
additional unbudgeted money will be spent from the General Fund to cover these
costs?
Lastly, my home on Pine Street is just west of 1155 Pine.  It is subject to the same
zoning rules/requirements.    I also own an adjacent property on Pine St. as well.
The current zoning and intended use of East Pine Street area is crystal clear, as is the
intent of the Proposed Comprehensive Plan and Highway 42 Revitalization Area
Comprehensive Plan Amendment.  As an owner of 2 properties on East Pine, I fully
support East Pine Street zoning as a “Neighborhood Center”.   This last-minute
proposal to purchase the rail cars and land, and retain them in this location, is in direct
conflict with every single plan and policy.
I stayed silent during previous public hearings because I trusted that the established

1.  Purpose 
This memorandum documents the engineering judgment supporting the design of the Polk Avenue / West Dahlia 
Street corridor between West Pine Street and Cherry Street: the problem it addresses, the constraints under which 
it was designed, the alternatives considered, the guidance relied upon, the departures knowingly accepted and 
their mitigations, and the corridor’s performance in service. It renders no legal opinion; questions of immunity and 
liability are reserved to the City Attorney. Quantitative statements are drawn from the City records cited in Section 
9. 
2.  The Problem and the Adopted Goals 
The corridor is a 5,300 ft residential collector — 42 ft flowline to flowline (38 ft of pavement plus two 2 ft gutter 
pans) — with continuous house-front driveways, on-street parking on both sides, Fireside Elementary School and 
park frontage on the north side of West Dahlia Street, two regional trail crossings, and a 25 mph posted limit with 
a 20 mph school zone. 
By 2023 the record before the City included: two pedestrian-involved incidents at corridor crossings; a 2020 
resident petition (51 signatures) seeking three to five additional crosswalks between Pine and Cherry; sustained 
resident correspondence on speed and cut-through traffic; measured 85th percentile speeds of 27.3–31.7 mph and 
95th percentile speeds of 29.4–33.6 mph against the 25 mph posting (February–April 2023 counts, ADT 2,949–
2,973); and an origin-destination analysis indicating approximately 54 percent of corridor traffic neither began nor 
ended in the neighborhood. The corridor is also a designated connection in the City’s adopted 2019 Transportation 
Master Plan bicycle network. 
Council-endorsed project goals: (1) increase crosswalk safety at Dahlia/Madison; (2) increase driver awareness; (3) 
slow speeds; (4) reduce cut-through traffic; (5) provide bicycle facilities within a cross-section constrained by 
residential front-facing on-street parking on both sides, without removing a resident’s ability to park in front of 
their home. Goal 5’s parking condition was an adopted policy constraint on the engineering, not an engineering 
conclusion.

The 14 ft centre lane was a documented selection among conflicting published guidance. FHWA’s Small Town 
and Rural Multimodal Networks (FHWA-HEP-17-024, 2016) states a preferred centre width of 13.5–16 ft, which 
“permits two passenger cars to pass within the center lane at modest speeds without encroaching into the 
advisory shoulder”; the Minneapolis Street Design Guide specifies 14–19 ft; NACTO’s Urban Bikeway Design Guide 
specifies 10–15 ft. Dutch CROW guidance, as reported in the Edge Lane Road Design Guide, disfavors 12.5– 15.8 ft. 
The City selected 14 ft with knowledge of the conflict, for a stated reason: motor vehicle volume on this corridor 
far exceeds bicycle volume, and the American guidance range serves the dominant user while avoiding advisory 
lanes of 7 ft or more, which NACTO cautions invite use as travel or parking lanes. Choosing among conflicting 
national guidance for documented reasons is the ordinary exercise of engineering judgment.

6.  Regulatory Context at the Time of the Decision 
• Advisory bike lanes were not a standard MUTCD treatment in 2023 and are not today. During project 
development the City inquired with FHWA regarding the experimentation program and was advised that 
applications for this treatment were not being accepted at that time. The City proceeded following that 
inquiry, not in ignorance of the federal process. 
• The treatment was in contemporary use by peer agencies: Vail, Colorado (trial 2020; permanent 2021, in 
service today); Fort Collins, Colorado (under an approved FHWA experiment, first lane opened October 2022, 
in service today); and more than ninety installations reported across North America. Published national design 
guidance was available and relied upon (NACTO; FHWA-HEP-17-024; Minneapolis). 
• Subsequent developments corroborate the judgment: AASHTO’s Guide for the Development of Bicycle 
Facilities, 5th Edition (December 2024), added advisory bicycle lanes (§9.8) —  national engineering guidance 
that did not exist at design now addresses the treatment. The corridor’s design choices, including the buffer 
and centre-lane width, fall within the ranges of the guidance now published.  
• Polk Avenue and West Dahlia Street are local streets under municipal jurisdiction; no CDOT approval applies.  
7.  Performance in Service 
The City committed to post-installation monitoring and has performed it: counts in December 2023, March 2024 
and October 2024; a resident survey in late 2024; and crash surveillance with the Police Department. Results, 
reported for the treatment package as a whole: 
• No reported crashes on the corridor in approximately 2.7 years of service (November 2023 –  August 2026). 
Both pre-project pedestrian incidents occurred at crossings and preceded the installation; the crossing 
locations received the corridor’s priority treatments (raised crosswalk at Madison; a second raised crossing at 
Polk/Hackberry under the 2026 project; curb-extension crossings at the trail and school locations). 
• Traffic volume declined 9.5 percent (Polk south of Hackberry: 2,949 to 2,669) and 19.4 percent (Dahlia north 
of Chestnut: 2,973 to 2,397) and the reduction has persisted — consistent with the design intent of deterring 
through traffic (Goal 4). 
• 85th percentile speeds declined 0.7 and 2.3 mph at the like-for-like stations, with the largest reductions in the 
first months after installation. 
• The 2024 corridor-resident survey found the raised crosswalk and speed cushions viewed favorably and the 
advisory lanes viewed unfavorably by many corridor-fronting respondents. Resident acceptance is monitored 
and informs the refinements below; it is noted that the surveyed population bears the corridor’s costs directly 
while the volume reduction benefits the wider neighborhood. 
8.  Reinstatement at the 2026 Resurfacing 
The 2026 Street Resurfacing Project reinstates the corridor with its markings, rebuilds the Madison raised 
crosswalk, and adds a raised crosswalk at Polk/Hackberry. The basis for reinstating the design is its record: no 
crashes in service, a durable volume reduction responsive to the corridor’s principal complaint, crossing 
improvements at the locations of the original incidents, and the absence of any documented crash increase 
attributable to this treatment nationally. Removing an in-service treatment with a clean record would itself require 
engineering justification the record does not supply.

10.  Conclusion 
The Polk/Dahlia corridor design was a reasonable exercise of engineering judgment. It responded to a documented 
problem; it operated within an adopted policy constraint; it considered the available alternatives and selected the 
only one satisfying the adopted goals; it relied on published national guidance and, where guidance conflicted, 
chose among the conflicts for stated reasons; it identified its departures and mitigated them; it was accompanied 
by an education program and a monitoring commitment that was kept; and in 2.7 years of service it has produced 
no reported crashes and a durable reduction in through traffic. Reinstatement at the 2026 resurfacing, with the 
refinements described in Section 8, is supported by that record.
