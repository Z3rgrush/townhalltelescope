Item 5F: Resolution Setting Certain Fees, Rate, and Charges for 2027
Question: Did RAB consider a resident rate for golf course memberships?
Answer (Adam Blackmore): RAB did not consider this option in any level of significant detail. The board reviewed
a variety of potential pricing options and the impacts each option will prospectively have on the Golf Fund which
included annual pass pricing and/or the removal of annual passes from the fee type offerings at Coal Creek. They
elected to maintain the pass options at a more viable price point and remove the “maximum” number to be sold
restriction that was put into place in 2026.
 
A reminder that Coal Creek does not offer memberships or have a membership structure. A membership
structure would typically include an initial lump sum membership investment, annual dues for course access
and capital investment, and a food & beverage/merchandise minimum spend requirement. This is an
important distinction when evaluating how the course operates and provides services to the community.
 
The 2027 fee schedule includes an all you can play annual pass option for weekdays or weekends. These pass
programs offer significant discounted per round value for those wishing to purchase the pass and play the
course on a regular basis. Staff’s opinion is that with nearly 55,000 rounds being played annually, and on-going
high demand for golf, there is not a need to further incentivize discounted play; particularly with an “all you
can play” option at an enterprise fund cost center. That being said, as found in the proposed fee schedule and
in response to council member feedback, the course is prepared to offer daily play resident discounts for the
first time in 2027.

Clerk, a portion is now being allocated to Courts/Public Safety (sub program 216).

you look to the Program level, you’ll note tax revenue increases by 2-3% in the O/S fund in total.
Question: Under budget subprogram 532 why do the expenses for services decrease so much in 2027 and 2028?
Answer: The Ice Rink was previously budgeted in the Recreation Fund in 2026, it was shifted to the General Fund
in 2027 and 2028, sub program 553, within the newly created division, citywide events and contracts.
Question: Under budget subprogram 553 why do the expenses for services increase so much in 2027 and 2028?  
Answer: Sub Program 553 contains newly created division, citywide events and contracts, which centralized many
of our city event and contracts, this includes the $68.5k Chamber of Commerce contract shifted here (previously
in Prog 65), Increase of 27k for Spec events, ice rink moved here 110k (previously Rec 532), and Sundance set
aside contingency $100k
Question: Under budget program 65 why do the expenses for services decrease so much in 2027 and 2028?
Answer: This decrease is driven by the one time vacancy bonus 100k (Kroger, one time in 26, not in 27/28)  and
Chamber of Commerce contract 68,500 shifted to 553, which contains newly created division, citywide events
and contracts..

Dear Barb, Caleb, Chris, Deb, Dietrich, and Judi, 
Here are some thoughts on our 2027 work plan. 
Regarding the process of crafting our 2027 work plan . . .
Since there will not be any changes on Council for 2027, one might expect our work plan to
largely carryover and continue from 2026. While I expect this to be largely true, I appreciate the
opportunity for wholesale reevaluation of the work plan, weighing all carryover and continuing
items against all new items in some comprehensive way. Such reevaluation should take place
with full and proper input from the public, boards, and commissions. Since we meet with
boards and commissions throughout the year, I would prefer that their staff liaisons provide
them with an explicit opportunity to advise on our work plan. 
Regarding the structure of our 2027 work plan . . .
As I recall, when developing our 2026 work plan, staff suggested that we might restructure this
work plan around the new Comprehensive Plan’s core values. I believe that the suggestion
entailed replacing the current four priorities and two lenses with the six core values, perhaps
categorizing work plan items under the headings of the core values or tagging work plan items
with the relevant core values. Since we did not adopt the new Comprehensive Plan until March
2026, this restructuring was put on hold.
I do not necessarily object to setting priorities for the coming year and categorizing work plan
items under these headings. Our priorities for any one year might not fully encompass the core
values, and our priorities for any one year might not fully encompass public input; neither of
these situations is necessarily problematic. Moreover, there will often be work plan items that
do not neatly fit into a categorization by priorities and work plan items that fit into more than
one categorization by priorities; the same is true for a categorization by core values. Also, it
may happen that in any one year we do not have work plan items that fall under one (or more)
of the core values; again, this is perhaps only a problem of optics. 
I actually find the work plan structure that Council used until a few years ago—with items
principally coded by overall priority during the year—preferable and more utilitarian. This
structure does not have the categorization issues mentioned above. This structure also shows
the public (and staff) how Council has prioritized its work. If we want to set overarching
priorities for the year, then we can always tag work plan items with these priorities; we can—
and should—do the same for the core values. (I like the idea of using—and showing our use—
of the new Comprehensive Plan.) Moreover, I recall that the work plan used to have a separate
section for unprioritized items that simply required Council’s attention during the year. The
work plan could also have a separate section for items that are public-facing but largely staff-
led projects during the year. 
Finally, despite staff’s efforts, I do not feel that the two lenses function adequately to bring
consideration of environmental sustainability and diversity, equity, and inclusion. An
alternative structure, like the one discussed just above, will not necessarily resolve this issue;
rather, I think that we need to develop a framework for such consideration, which might be a
work plan item in itself. 
Regarding specific work plan items . . .
I am interested in considering the following additions to the work plan. 
A program to incentivize the deed-restriction of existing (likely smaller and older) homes
as affordable rentals or owned units potentially using historical preservation funds
Further revision of historical preservation tax spending to advance environmental
sustainability and diversity, equity, and inclusion
Exploration of City-owned properties (excluding open spaces) as potential affordable
housing locations
Comprehensive climate adaptation and resilience planning and action
Investigation of a comprehensive volunteer program (to supplement not replace staff)
I am also interested in considering the following additions to the work plan, but I do not believe
that I have sufficient support for consideration.
Adoption of the DRCOG building code or the State’s model low energy and carbon code
Phase-out of sale and use of gasoline-powered landscaping equipment
Protections for mature trees on public and private property
Restrictions on use of pesticides on public and private property
Update of weed ordinance
Thanks for your consideration.
Best,
Josh

Founded in 1973, BVHC's mission is to provide high-quality, equitable reproductive and 
sexual healthcare, abortions, and education to all people, especially those facing 
barriers to care. Clinical services include STI testing and treatment, pregnancy testing, 
contraception, annual exams, cancer screenings, menopause care, and 
gender-affirming care. As the only Title X provider in Boulder County, BVHC serves a 
vital role in the healthcare ecosystem. Title X is the U.S. federal family planning program 
established in 1970. It provides grants to community-based clinics, such as BVHC, to 
deliver contraception, reproductive health counseling, and other preventive services to 
low-income, uninsured, and otherwise underserved populations. This funding has 
historically enabled BVHC to offer a sliding fee scale, reducing costs to $0 for eligible 
patients. BVHC's FY26-29 strategic priorities focus on organizational sustainability, 
workforce development, compassionate and innovative care, and stronger community 
partnerships and trust. Grounded in a reproductive justice framework, we reduce 
barriers through affordable services, protecting patient privacy, and fostering an 
affirming environment. BVHC assesses need through patient demographic and 
utilization data collected in our Athena EHR system, patient feedback, community 
partnerships, and trends identified by our clinical team. These sources help identify 
emerging needs, address service gaps, and keep programs responsive to need.
Funding will help sustain BVHC's overall capacity to provide affordable, high-quality 
reproductive healthcare to Louisville residents. Support will help offset the costs of 
clinical staffing, patient navigation, care coordination, medical supplies, technology 
systems, and other essential operational expenses that enable patients to access care 
regardless of income, insurance status, or background. In 2026, 68% of BVHC’s 
patients were low-, very low-, or extremely low-income, 44% were LGBTQIA+, 17% 
were transgender, nonbinary, or another identity, and 37% were under age 25. Based 
on current service volume projections, BVHC is on track to serve 28% more Louisville 
residents than in 2025, demonstrating both growing community needs and increased 
utilization of our services. We maintain an income-based sliding fee scale that reduces 
eligible patients' costs to as little as $0. Through this support, BVHC will continue 
providing a comprehensive range of reproductive healthcare services, including 
contraception counseling and birth control services, STI testing, treatment, and 
prevention, gender-affirming care, cancer screenings, resource navigation, and other 
preventative care. This funding is an investment in BVHC’s ability to serve the most 
vulnerable, providing the flexibility needed to navigate an uncertain funding landscape 
and increasing costs for delivering care. The services BVHC provides helps address 
health concerns early, improve health outcomes, and stay engaged in care.

By June 2026, BVHC delivered 57 health services to 41 unique Louisville residents. In 
recent years, BVHC made a concerted effort to expand services related to menopause 
care, gender-affirming care (GAC), and PrEP. While BVHC served Louisville residents 
in all three areas, we made the greatest impact in gender-affirming care services, which 
accounted for 28% of all services provided. This is an 8% increase in GAC service 
utilization by Louisville residents, indicating our efforts on this service over the past two 
years have been successful. Of the 223 BVHC patients who completed a survey on their 
GAC service experience in 2026, 91% reported a positive experience with our team. In 
addition to GAC services, 23% of Louisville patients sought birth control services, while 
18% sought STI testing, treatment, and prevention services. This data tells us that 
BVHC experienced strong demand for several core reproductive healthcare services 
from Louisville residents, reflecting growing community needs and utilization.  
One challenge faced in 2026 was patient volume being 6% below projections between 
January and May. Data analysis suggested rising living costs were contributing to 
patients delaying care. For example, of the Louisville residents served, 68% were 
extremely low-, very low-, or low-income based on Boulder County area median income 
standards. Three-quarters of these individuals fell within the extremely low-income 
category. In response, BVHC expanded community outreach efforts, strengthened 
online visibility, and increased participation in community events to improve awareness 
of available services and engagement with priority populations. These efforts 
contributed to reproductive health service utilization exceeding projected volume by 4% 
in June 2026. 
In addition to providing clinical services based on the utilization trends observed among 
Louisville residents this year, we plan to continue strengthening our patient navigation 
and care coordination services to address the economic strain facing the overwhelming 
majority of our Louisville patients. Of the 44 unique Louisville residents who completed 
SDOH screenings, 59% identified one or more needs beyond the scope of our clinical 
services. Through coordinated efforts to provide resource referrals, assistance 
accessing social services, and individualized support navigating healthcare systems, 
BVHC aims to reduce barriers that can prevent patients from obtaining timely care. We 
will continue using patient feedback, referral outcomes, and social determinants of 
health data to inform service improvements and ensure our programs remain responsive

At present, we have 8 people serving on our Board of Directors, none of whom live in 
Louisville. Our current members live in Longmont, Denver, Lyons, Boulder, Arvada, and 
Lafayette.
BVHC currently employs 25 staff. Of these, 20 are full-time, meaning 32 hours or 
above each week. We have 5 part-time employees, ranging from 16-31 hours each 
week. In addition, BVHC contracts 2 abortion providers, who each work 8 hours per 
week. None of our employees currently live in Louisville. 
BVHC currently utilizes volunteers to help with outreach activities, serve on our 
Inclusion & Equity (I&E) Committee, and assist with fundraising events.  Volunteers, 
including our Board of Directors, participate in onboarding and training specific to the 
services they will provide and are partnered with the department they will support. We 
estimate that volunteers will contribute approximately 750 hours of service this year.
Our Clinic Director and CEO/Chief Medical Officer will oversee the implementation, 
evaluation, and quality improvement of the organization’s services supported through 
this request. Clinical staff will provide direct patient care, and our Patient Navigator will 
support patients through care coordination, resource referrals, and assistance 
navigating healthcare and social service systems.

BVHC acts as a collaborative partner alongside several organizations providing similar 
services, including RISE Collective, The Crane Center, Planned Parenthood, and 
Boulder Community Health. These organizations offer unique specialties within the 
same field and serve many of the same populations as BVHC.  BVHC actively refers 
patients to these partners based on clinical needs, organizational capacity, and service 
availability, ensuring access to the most appropriate and timely care. Together, these 
partnerships foster a referral network centered on prioritizing patients’ needs. 
Additionally, BVHC maintains collaborative relationships with organizations that 
provide different types of support but serve many of the same priority populations, 
including Rocky Mountain Equality, Our Center, Sister Carmen, EFAA, ViVe Wellness, 
and YWCA. These organizations have deep connections within communities such as 
LGBTQIA+ individuals, immigrants, and people of color, helping cultivate trusted 
referral pathways to BVHC’s services. Through referrals, care coordination, and 
ongoing collaboration, BVHC works in tandem with local organizations to ensure 
residents have access to an effective continuum of care.
BVHC is requesting increased funding to respond to the growing healthcare and 
support needs of Louisville residents, as well as the rising costs of delivering 
high-quality, affordable care. As healthcare costs continue to increase, maintaining 
access for patients with limited financial resources requires additional local investment. 
As noted elsewhere in this application, BVHC's patient data highlights significant 
economic challenges within the community. Of the 68% of Louisville residents served 
who are classified as low-, very low-, or extremely low-income based on Boulder 
County Area Median Income standards, 71% are extremely low-income. Through our 
patient navigation program, we have identified additional barriers that affect healthcare 
access and overall well-being, including unemployment or underemployment, debt, 
food insecurity, difficulty affording basic necessities, housing instability, mental health 
concerns, and social isolation. 
As the cost of living continues to rise, these financial and social pressures increase the 
likelihood that residents will delay or forgo needed healthcare. Increased funding will 
help BVHC meet growing demand for affordable sexual and reproductive healthcare 
while addressing the social and economic factors that influence the health and 
well-being of Louisville residents.
