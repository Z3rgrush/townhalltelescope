Dear Mayor, Council Members, and Historic Preservation Commissioners,
Ahead of tonight's joint meeting, I wanted to share a few thoughts about Louisville's historic
preservation program, where we've been successful, and where I believe we have an
opportunity to do something even more meaningful.
First, I want to recognize the remarkable program Louisville has built. Our preservation
incentives, landmark designation process, and commitment to Historic Structure
Assessments represent a substantial investment in the history and character of our
community. The HPC, City staff, and successive Councils deserve considerable credit for
this accomplishment.
As an architect who has worked on Historic Structure Assessments, Landmark applications,
restoration projects, and preservation incentives in Louisville, I've seen firsthand what this
program makes possible. I can also attest that much of its success is directly tied to its
community-first approach: preservation is voluntary, participation is encouraged through
meaningful financial incentives, and the overall process is welcoming, collaborative, and
supportive of property owners. Rather than relying primarily on regulation or restrictions,
Louisville has created a program that encourages people to see preservation as an
opportunity. I believe this key approach has been fundamental to earning community trust
and encouraging participation, and it is something we should be especially careful to
preserve as the program evolves.
Through this work, I've also come to appreciate that the most interesting part of a historic
building isn't always its architecture. Often, it's the people who lived there, the lives they
led, and the ways the building has changed alongside the community. This is where I see
an opportunity to build upon the program's existing strengths by engaging more residents in
understanding and sharing Louisville's history.
I believe Louisville has a preservation program worth celebrating. But even the best
programs cannot sustain themselves on autopilot. They need fresh ideas, continued
engagement, and a continued willingness to evolve. My hope is that tonight's meeting will
be more than a review of accomplishments and will instead begin a conversation about
what the next chapter of historic preservation in Louisville might look like.
I recently read “The Relevancy Guidebook: How We Can Transform the Future of
Preservation,” by Bonnie McDonald of Landmarks Illinois. It's an insightful examination of
how preservation organizations can become more relevant to the communities they serve.
One of its most compelling ideas is that historic preservation should be about people first,
and buildings second. That perspective raises a question I think we should be asking more
often: Who gets to decide what history is important enough to preserve?
Traditionally, preservation has emphasized architectural significance, prominent individuals,
and buildings that retain much of their original character. Those considerations are
important, but they tell only part of Louisville's story. Our history is also found in the modest
homes of mining families, neighborhood businesses, gathering places, and buildings that
have changed and adapted over generations. Some of the most meaningful places in our
community may not be architecturally remarkable at all.
I would like to see us broaden the conversation about what makes a place historically
significant and invite more residents to participate in telling those stories. This doesn't mean
abandoning preservation standards or professional expertise. It means recognizing that the
people of Louisville hold a great deal of historical knowledge that deserves to be part of the
process.
I also believe there's an important distinction between saving history and preserving history.
Saving a historic building generally means preventing it from being lost. That's essential
work, but preservation should go further. Preservation is about carrying history forward. It
means allowing historic buildings to remain useful, accommodate new generations, and
continue contributing to our community while retaining the qualities and stories that make
them significant.
Louisville's history didn't stop when the coal mines closed, when businesses changed
hands, or when families modified their homes to meet new needs. Each generation has
added another layer to our story. Our preservation program should recognize that history is
ongoing rather than treating historic buildings as artifacts frozen in amber.
With these ideas in mind, I'd like to suggest ten opportunities for the HPC and Council to
consider. Some are straightforward improvements, while others would require additional
collaboration or longer-term planning.
1. Improve the City's historic preservation website. Make information about landmark
designation, preservation grants, eligibility, and application procedures easier to find
and understand. The website should also serve residents who simply want to learn
about Louisville's historic places, not just those applying for grants.
2. Strengthen the relationship between the HPC and Historical Museum. The HPC
focuses on preserving physical structures, while the Museum documents the people
and stories associated with them. These missions are inseparable, and closer
coordination could enrich both preservation decisions and public education.
3. Create a publicly accessible historic landmark register. Each landmark could have a
dedicated webpage containing photographs, designation information, Historic
Structure Assessments, architectural surveys, and available Museum social histories.
We've invested substantial public resources in producing this information, and it
should be more accessible, subject to appropriate privacy considerations.
4. Restore historic property information to the City's GIS mapping system. An interactive
map linked to the landmark register would allow residents to discover the history of
buildings in their neighborhoods and could support walking tours and other
interpretive activities.
5. Better connect residents with state and national preservation resources. The City
could provide clearer information and links to History Colorado, the State Historic
Preservation Office, the National Park Service, and applicable grants, technical
assistance, and tax incentives.
6. Proactively identify and engage owners of significant historic properties. Rather than
relying exclusively on property owners to initiate landmark applications, the HPC
could work with the Museum and interested residents to identify important buildings,
review existing surveys, and reach out to owners about voluntary preservation
opportunities.
7. Introduce public-facing interpretive plaques. In addition to Louisville's existing
landmark plaques, consider a second bronze plaque near the sidewalk that briefly
tells the story of the building and the people associated with it. Imagine how much
more meaningful a walk through Old Town could become if these stories were readily
available.
8. Connect preservation with housing affordability. I've previously proposed a Market-to-
Affordable Acquisition Program that would acquire existing homes and establish long-
term affordability protections. There may be an opportunity to combine affordable
housing resources with preservation grants to rehabilitate eligible historic homes
while creating permanently affordable housing. This would require careful review of
funding eligibility, but it could advance two important City priorities simultaneously.
9. Better celebrate Louisville's preservation program. I suspect many residents don't
realize how substantial our preservation incentives are or understand what the
program has accomplished. Sharing restoration stories, before-and-after
photographs, and the history behind landmark designations could help demonstrate
the broader public value of this investment. A broader celebration and recognition of
the program could include guest speaker opportunities at historic preservation and/or
planning conferences.
10. Broaden how we define and communicate preservation. Preservation should not be
understood simply as preventing change. It should help historic places remain
relevant as Louisville evolves, recognize stories that may have been overlooked, and
encourage compatible adaptation. Our goal shouldn't be to preserve a single, fixed
image of the past, but to carry the many layers of Louisville's history into the future.
I recognize that the HPC is a volunteer commission with considerable responsibilities. I
don't believe these recommendations should simply become more work for commissioners
or City staff. In fact, I think one of our greatest opportunities is to invite more residents to
help.
There are many people in Louisville who care deeply about our history, architecture, and
preservation efforts. Some have lived here for generations and carry stories that may never
have been formally documented. Others bring experience in architecture, research,
photography, mapping, writing, or community outreach. I suspect many would gladly
contribute their time if given a meaningful opportunity.
I'd like to propose a Historic Preservation Community Working Group consisting of one or
two HPC commissioners and approximately eight to twelve interested Louisville residents.
This wouldn't be another regulatory board or decision-making body. It would be a volunteer
group working in support of the HPC, providing additional ideas, perspectives, and the extra
horsepower needed to pursue some of these initiatives.
The group could begin with a few manageable projects, such as helping organize the online
landmark register, researching the stories associated with historic buildings, developing
interpretive plaque content, or identifying places that deserve additional historical research.
It could work alongside the Museum and City staff while leaving the HPC's formal
responsibilities and authority unchanged. The exact structure would need to fit the City's
procedures, but I would favor a practical, project-oriented approach rather than creating
another layer of bureaucracy.
Just as importantly, this group could bring more voices into the discussion about what
history deserves recognition. The HPC brings valuable expertise, but the community itself
holds an enormous amount of knowledge about the people and places that have shaped
Louisville. Giving residents a meaningful role in this work would make preservation more
inclusive and help build a stronger sense of shared ownership.
I hope tonight's discussion can move beyond the annual list of accomplishments and
consider a few achievable priorities for the coming year. Establishing a community working
group, beginning a publicly accessible landmark register, and strengthening collaboration
with the Historical Museum would be meaningful first steps. Council can play an important
role by supporting these priorities, encouraging coordination, and helping create
opportunities for residents to participate.
Louisville has already demonstrated its commitment to historic preservation through
substantial financial incentives and the dedication of its commissioners, staff, and property
owners. We have a strong foundation, and I believe we're ready to build on it.
Ultimately, I would like to see preservation become something more residents feel
connected to and take part in. Historic preservation shouldn't simply be something the City
does for the community. It should be something the community does together, with the
City's support.
I support Ballot Question 2B along side fellow local architect, Peter Stewart.  Let's continue
saving the historic buildings that make Louisville special, but let's also make sure we're
preserving the stories, the people, and the living history that make those buildings worth
saving in the first place.
Thank you for your consideration and for your continued commitment to Louisville's historic
preservation program.
Sincerely,
Andy

Dear Mayor and Councilmembers,
I am writing in advance of the joint City Council and Planning Commission meeting to
encourage both bodies to make the upcoming Development Code Update a defining priority
for 2027 (and 2028). I also believe this presents an important opportunity to reconsider the
relationship between Planning Commission and City Council, and to establish a more
meaningful role for the Commission in shaping Louisville’s land-use policies.
First, I want to recognize again the tremendous work undertaken by City Council, Planning
Commission, staff, and the community in developing Louisville’s updated Comprehensive
Plan. The process established a thoughtful, forward-looking vision for our city. However, a
Comprehensive Plan is only as effective as the zoning regulations and decision-making
processes that allow it to be implemented.
Louisville’s zoning system has its origins in the early 1960’s, a period when American land-
use regulations were frequently rooted in exclusion, separation of uses, and the
preservation of established development patterns. The racial and economic exclusion
associated with twentieth-century zoning is a history we should acknowledge as we
reconsider the assumptions underlying our current regulations. More importantly, we should
ask whether a system originating in that era remains appropriate for the community we
aspire to become. We should not confuse the longevity of a regulation with its
effectiveness.
As an architect practicing in Louisville, I regularly encounter the inefficiencies and
unnecessary complexities of our development review process. Our regulations are difficult
to understand, time-consuming to administer, and too often create obstacles to the very
outcomes our Comprehensive Plan encourages. Even modest improvements to our built
environment can require disproportionate amounts of time, money, and procedural effort.
The resulting uncertainty discourages investment, incremental development, and creative
solutions to our community’s evolving needs.
Perhaps most concerning is the effect this system has on our Planning staff. Louisville is
fortunate to have intelligent, capable, and dedicated planning professionals. Yet we have
created a regulatory environment that consumes much of their time administering
complicated and somewhat Byzantine zoning requirements rather than allowing them to
focus on the more meaningful work of planning. Planning should be about shaping the
future of our community, not simply managing the limitations of its past.
The Development Code Update should therefore be much more than an administrative
exercise in reorganizing chapters, updating definitions, or simplifying isolated procedures. It
should fundamentally reconsider our zoning districts, permitted uses, development
standards, and approval processes. Without getting into the granular details, we should
strive for a regulatory framework that encourages incremental growth, supports diverse
housing choices, promotes walkable neighborhoods, and allows our community to evolve
thoughtfully over time.
Our goal should be straightforward: Create a system where good development is the
easiest development to accomplish. That means clear and understandable regulations,
predictable outcomes, greater opportunities for by-right development, and a streamlined
review process that allows staff to focus their expertise where it provides the greatest public
benefit.
Equally important is the opportunity to reconsider how Planning Commission participates in
shaping Louisville’s land-use policies. I find it somewhat ironic that we have a commission
of dedicated residents who volunteer their time specifically to focus on land use and
planning, yet their influence on consequential land-use decisions often appears limited to
forwarding recommendations to City Council. Too often, Council’s subsequent deliberations
do not appear to meaningfully build upon the discussions, analysis, and conclusions
developed by Planning Commission.
I want to emphasize that this observation is not directed at our current Council. Rather, it
reflects an institutional habit that has developed over many years and through multiple
Councils. Councilmembers run for office to provide leadership, establish priorities, and
make important policy decisions on behalf of our community. They are not expected to
become land-use professionals. Planning Commission exists, in part, to provide the
sustained attention, deliberation, and specialized focus that these complex issues deserve.
Yet we do not appear to fully capitalize on that resource.
Planning Commission should have more skin in the game than simply forwarding a
recommendation to Council. Its work should meaningfully shape policy, inform decisions,
and contribute to the long-term direction of our community. Council should be able to
demonstrate how the Commission’s deliberations influenced its decisions, even when
Council ultimately reaches a different conclusion.
I would encourage two specific changes as part of the Development Code Update:
1. Give Planning Commission a leadership role in establishing the direction of the
Development Code Update.
Planning Commission should be actively involved in defining the fundamental objectives,
principles, and measures of success before staff and consultants begin drafting regulations.
The Commission should not simply be asked to react to a substantially completed
document. It should help establish what the new zoning system is intended to accomplish,
identify the shortcomings of our current approach, and evaluate whether proposed changes
genuinely advance the Comprehensive Plan. This would make Planning Commission a
meaningful participant in shaping the reform rather than simply another step in the review
process.
2. Establish a stronger relationship between Planning Commission recommendations
and Council decisions.
Council should give substantive consideration to the Commission’s deliberations, not
merely receive its recommendations as a procedural requirement. When Council reaches a
different conclusion on a significant land-use matter, there should be an expectation that
the reasons for that departure are clearly articulated, including acknowledgment of the
Commission’s analysis and the policy considerations that led Council to a different decision.
This would not diminish Council’s authority or responsibility. Rather, it would strengthen the
decision-making process, provide greater transparency, and reinforce the value of the
Commission’s work. If we ask residents to volunteer their time and energy to serve on
Planning Commission, we should ensure their contributions genuinely matter.
The Development Code Update may be one of the most consequential planning initiatives
Louisville undertakes in a generation. It deserves the time, resources, and ambition
necessary to produce meaningful reform. I encourage Council and Planning Commission to
make this work a central priority of their 2027–2028 agendas, with clear objectives, an
established schedule, and a collaborative process that meaningfully engages the
Commission, staff, residents, and professionals who regularly work within our development
regulations.
Success should not be measured simply by how many sections of code are rewritten or
how efficiently the project is completed. It should be measured by whether the resulting
system makes Louisville a better place to live, work, invest, and build. We should emerge
with a development framework that is simpler, more predictable, more equitable, and better
aligned with the aspirations of our community, along with a stronger Planning Commission
whose contributions are meaningfully reflected in the decisions that shape Louisville’s
future.
Louisville has already articulated a compelling vision through its updated Comprehensive
Plan. Now we need the courage to change both the regulations and the decision-making
processes that will determine whether that vision becomes reality.
Thank you for your consideration and for your continued service to our community.
Sincerely,
Andy

Services Department to enable a seamless integration.
