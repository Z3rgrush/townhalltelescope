Summary:  
The applicant requests landmark designation for the Louisville Methodist Church 
located at 741 Jefferson Ave. 
 
  
 
Background / Prior Discussions: 
Information from Louisville Historical Museum 
The Louisville United Methodist Church was the second church building in Louisville 
and is the oldest church still meeting in its original building. The date of construction of 
the church has been well established as having been 1892, but it should be noted that 
the church building does appear in the correct location on the 1909 Drumm’s Wall Map 
of Louisville and on the Methodist Church Map of Louisville that was made in circa 
1923-25. The property in question is outside of the boundaries of the Sanborn Maps 
that were done for Louisville in 1893, 1900, and 1908 (they focused on the downtown 
business district and La Farge Avenue only). 
The church was established by, and appealed to, the many English pioneer families 
who came to Louisville in its earliest years. Many of the men were miners who had 
Page 3 of 150
come from mining areas of England. Tom Carlton, who was a miner and preacher 
(though not ordained, apparently), is generally recognized as having been the force 
behind the establishment of the church. 
The Methodist Church had a strong affiliation with the Iliff School of Theology in 
Denver. The Iliff School provided student pastors for the church. According to local 
historian Carolyn Conarroe, writing in 1991, “[o]f the total 60 ministers who have served 
Louisville Methodist Church, 57 are thought to have been students.” It was true for at 
least some of these student pastors that they would come up from Denver and stay 
over the weekend in a small room in the building. One of these student ministers was 
Ralph Mulhollen, who was assigned by the Iliff School of Theology to be the pastor for 
the Louisville Methodist Church from 1923 to 1925. It was likely during this time frame 
that he made a map of the Louisville parish that shows the location of not only streets 
but also houses and businesses at the time. His name appears on the map. This map 
was found in a home at 700 Lincoln that until recently was still owned by the Thomas 
family and was donated to the Louisville Historical Museum. Mary Thomas, the home’s 
former resident, was among the founders of the church, according to a Thomas family 
history. (She and her husband, Nicholas Thomas, were also associated with 733 Pine 
in Jefferson Place.)  
Jefferson Place Historic Survey 
The Jefferson Place Survey identifies the Louisville United Methodist Church at 741 
Jefferson Avenue (5BL924) as an important historic resource within the Jefferson Place 
neighborhood. The survey identifies the building historically as the Methodist Episcopal 
Church and states that it was constructed in 1891. It further notes that the building 
remains extant and that the congregation is now known as the Louisville United 
Methodist Church. 
The survey discusses the church within the broader historic context of religious and 
community institutions in Louisville. It identifies three early religious congregations 
located within Jefferson Place: St. Louis Catholic Church, the Methodist Episcopal 
Church, and the Pillar of Fire congregation. Regarding 741 Jefferson, the survey states 
that the Methodist Episcopal Church was built in 1891 and remains in use by the 
Louisville United Methodist Church.  
Association with Jefferson Place's Immigrant Community 
The survey also discusses the church in the context of the ethnic and social history of 
Jefferson Place. It explains that social and cultural ties among neighborhood residents 
were formed through schools, churches, and fraternal organizations. Within this 
discussion, the survey states that the Methodist Episcopal Church at 741 Jefferson 
primarily served families from the British Isles.  
This discussion occurs within the survey's broader treatment of English and other 
European immigrants who settled in Jefferson Place, including immigrants associated 
with Louisville's coal-mining industry. Accordingly, the survey places the church within 
Page 4 of 150
the social and cultural development of the historic neighborhood rather than treating it 
solely as an architectural resource.  
Architectural Significance and State Register Eligibility 
The survey specifically evaluates 741 Jefferson Avenue (5BL924), Methodist Episcopal 
Church, as eligible for listing in the Colorado State Register of Historic Properties under 
Criterion C for Architecture, identifying the building as a Gothic Revival style church.  
In its discussion of individually eligible resources, the survey states that five resources 
within Jefferson Place were considered eligible for the State Register. The Methodist 
Episcopal Church at 741 Jefferson was one of those five resources. The survey lists 
the property as: “741 Jefferson 5BL924 Methodist Episcopal Church: Eligible to State 
Register Criterion C, Architecture, Gothic Revival style church”  
The survey also illustrates the property in its discussion of non-residential architecture, 
identifying 741 Jefferson as the Methodist Episcopal Church and as an example of a 
Gothic Revival style church.  
Survey Inventory Data 
The survey's property inventory identifies the resource as follows: 
 Address: 741 Jefferson Avenue 
 Current Building Name: United Methodist Church 
 Historic Building Name: Methodist Episcopal Church

The inventory table also records the property as meeting several survey 
eligibility/contributing classifications.  
Thus, the Jefferson Place Survey provides supporting documentation for the historic 
significance of 741 Jefferson Avenue in two principal respects. First, it places the 
Methodist Episcopal Church within the early social, religious, and immigrant history of 
Jefferson Place, specifically noting its association with families from the British Isles. 
Second, it identifies the building as an architecturally significant example of the Gothic 
Revival style and finds it eligible for the Colorado State Register under Criterion C 
(Architecture).  
The survey therefore provides evidence relevant to both the historical associations of 
the property and the architectural significance of the church building. The State 
Register eligibility determination reported in the survey is specifically based on 
architecture; the survey's discussion of the congregation and British immigrant 
community provides additional historical context but is not identified in the report as the 
basis for that State Register eligibility finding.

ARCHITECTURAL INTEGRITY 
The Historic Structure Assessment for 741 Jefferson Avenue, prepared by DAJ Design 
in 2024, identifies the Louisville United Methodist Church as a wood-frame vernacular 
structure constructed around 1892. The wood-frame construction is characteristic of 
late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century construction techniques found in Louisville. 
The HSA notes that many of the building’s original materials are consistent with 
materials and details found on other Louisville buildings of a similar age, while some 
construction techniques and details are unique to the church, with no known precedent 
elsewhere in Louisville.  
Figure 3. Historic development of property

The primary façade of the church faces east toward Jefferson Avenue. Although the 
building has undergone several modifications and additions, the form of the original 
church remains similar to that documented in the earliest historic photographs. Early 
changes included construction of a small addition on the south side, removal of the 
original entrance, and construction of a new entrance and bell tower on the north side. 
The HSA dates the south altar addition to sometime between approximately 1892 and 
1910 and the north seating area, entrance, and bell tower to approximately 1910. A 
larger addition was constructed to the west of the original church in 1941, followed by a 
two-story west addition in 2000.  
Despite these changes, the HSA finds that many original materials remain, including 
structural members, decorative elements, exterior siding, doors, and windows. Several 
elements removed during early alterations also appear to have been repurposed 
elsewhere in the building, including components of the original entrance and bell tower. 
The original steeply pitched gable roof and overall massing also remain apparent when 
the building is viewed from all directions, notwithstanding the subsequent additions.  
DAJ Design concludes that “741 Jefferson has the potential to be restored to a high 
degree of architectural integrity when compared to historic photos.” The HSA further 
characterizes the original church as essentially intact, with very few modifications since 
the early phases of construction during the late 1800s and early 1900s. Overall, the 
church is described as well maintained, although the assessment identifies structural 
and maintenance items requiring prioritization.  
Based on the HSA, the building retains substantial physical evidence of its early 
construction and development. The later additions document the continued use and 
expansion of the church, while the original church form and numerous original materials 
and architectural features remain identifiable. 
HISTORIC STRUCTURE ASSESSMENT 
A Historic Structure Assessment (HSA) was completed for the property in 2024 by DAJ 
Design, with structural evaluation provided by GFE Structural. The assessment 
documents the building's construction history, existing materials, architectural features, 
structural systems, condition, and recommended repairs. 
The HSA identifies six primary phases in the physical development of the building: the 
original church, constructed around 1892; the south altar addition, constructed between 
approximately 1892 and 1910; the north seating addition and new entrance and bell 
tower, constructed circa 1910; the middle addition constructed in 1941; and the west 
addition constructed in 2000.  
The assessment finds that the original church is substantially intact and retains 
numerous historic materials and features. However, the HSA also identifies structural 
deficiencies that require attention. Of particular concern is the roof framing associated 
with the original church and the 1941 addition. The original 1892 roof framing and north 
Page 8 of 150
addition were evaluated as being in fair to poor condition, with evidence of water 
damage and rot and several unsupported rafters in the bell tower area. The 1941 roof 
framing was evaluated as being in fair condition, but the assessment identified 
improperly supported ceiling-joist splices and other framing conditions requiring 
additional structural support.  
GFE Structural concluded that the existing roof framing contains multiple deficiencies 
warranting further evaluation and repair. The structural engineer recommended that the 
roof structure over both the original church and the 1940s addition be further evaluated 
and that necessary repairs be implemented as soon as possible. The assessment also 
cautions that significant portions of the building's structural systems were concealed by 
finish materials and therefore could not be observed. Thus, additional deficiencies may 
become apparent when those areas are exposed. 
Recommended structural work includes further evaluation of the original roof and 
ceiling support system, which includes; additional support for the 1941 roof framing; 
installation of diagonal braces, purlins, beams, and potentially new foundation support; 
and evaluation of concealed portions of the roof and floor systems by a licensed 
structural engineer. The HSA recommends that structural repairs be designed by a 
licensed structural engineer and reviewed by the City prior to construction.  
The HSA also identifies several preservation and maintenance recommendations. 
These include restoring historic windows and their wood sash, frames, counterweights, 
hardware, and stained glass; repairing or restoring exterior siding, window and door 
trim, frieze boards, corner trim, decorative barge-rafter brackets, and decorative 
bargeboards; addressing deteriorated painted wood elements; and evaluating and 
repairing the historic chimney as necessary. The report generally recommends repair 
and restoration of existing historic materials rather than wholesale replacement.  
Site drainage is also identified as an important maintenance issue. The structural 
assessment recommends directing water away from the building because poor 
drainage could contribute to water infiltration, foundation movement, and deterioration 
of wood framing. Existing gutters and downspouts were evaluated as generally being in 
fair condition, although not all downspouts have extensions sufficient to direct water 
away from the foundation.  
Overall, the HSA finds that the church retains a substantial amount of its historic 
building fabric and that the original church remains essentially intact. At the same time, 
the assessment identifies structural deficiencies, particularly within the roof framing, 
that warrant further engineering evaluation and repair. The recommended work is 
primarily directed toward stabilization, repair, restoration, and continued maintenance of 
the existing building and its historic materials, rather than reconstruction of the historic 
structure.

History of 741 Jefferson (Louisville’s Methodist Church) 
This report is intended to supplement and update the 2000 survey that was conducted with respect to 
the history of the Methodist Church at 741 Jefferson. This church was the second church building in 
Louisville and is the oldest church still meeting in its original building. 
When Charles Welch conveyed this property in 1891 to the “Methodist Episcopal Church” for the 
construction of a church building, it likely made sense for it to be located on Spruce, which was 
developing into the major east-west street in downtown Louisville. Maps and firsthand accounts 
indicate that Spruce was at the time a through street to the west, and Pine was not (whereas today, Pine 
is a through street, and Spruce is not.) By the early to mid 1900s, Spruce as it passed through the 
Jefferson Place Addition included the offices of the Louisville Times at 800/804 Spruce, a very nearby 
store at 805 La Farge, the William Austin store at the northeast corner of Spruce and Jefferson, the 
Methodist Church at 741 Jefferson, and the Louisville grade school. Farther west on Spruce were the 
brick school house at 801 Grant and the German Lutheran Church at Spruce and McKinley.  
The following photo shows the relationship of the Methodist Church (on the left) to the Louisville Grade 
School across Spruce to the north; it was taken in 1961, not long before the school was demolished: 
 
Page 17 of 150
As noted in the 2000 survey, this church was established by, and appealed to, the many English pioneer 
families who came to Louisville in its earliest years. Many of the men were miners who had come from 
mining areas of England. Surnames of families who belonged to this church included (but are not limited 
to) the following prominent Anglo families who lived in Louisville: Carlton, Hutchinson, Dalby, Jenkins, 
Lawrence, Thirlaway, Thomas, Thompson, Ferguson, Robinson, Hilton, Cable, Pickett, and Henning. 
Tom Carlton, who was a miner and preacher (though not ordained, apparently), is generally recognized 
as having been the force behind the establishment of the church. He was born in 1824 in the village of 
Flimby in Cumberland, which is on the Irish Sea near Maryport and near the border with Scotland. He 
married in 1844 to Jane McLean (1818-1891) and they had several children. English census records show 
that he was a “colliery viewer,” which was a position similar to that of a foreman. The Carlton family 
immigrated to the US in the 1870s. By 1880, Tom Carlton was a mining engineer in Gold Hill in Boulder 
County, and by 1885, he was living in Louisville with his family. Records indicate that he was a preacher 
for Louisville’s English settlers who met in private homes prior to the building of the Methodist Church 
and that he personally raised $800 for the building. He died in January 1892, not long after the death of 
his wife, before the Methodist Episcopal Church could be opened.  
The following two photos are strongly believed to show Jane and Tom Carlton based on the available 
evidence and writings on the backs of the photos. The photo of Jane Carlton was taken by a local 
Colorado photographer, while the photo of Tom Carlton was taken in Maryport, England (near his home 
village of Flimby):
 
The earliest available photo of the church shows a central entrance on the east and a bell tower, 
although records indicate that there may not have ever been a bell installed in that particular tower.  
Page 18 of 150
The photo would likely have been taken between 1892 and 1910 (when some modifications were 
made). In these earliest years, the pews faced south and the pulpit was in the south alcove. 
The Methodist Church had a strong affiliation with the Iliff School of Theology in Denver. The Iliff School 
provided student pastors for the church. According to local historian Carolyn Conarroe, writing in 1991, 
“[o]f the total 60 ministers who have served Louisville Methodist Church, 57 are thought to have been 
students.” It was true for at least some of these student pastors that they would come up from Denver 
and stay over the weekend in a small room in the building.  
One of these student ministers was Ralph Mulhollen, who was assigned by the Iliff School of Theology to 
be the pastor for the Louisville Methodist Church from 1923 to 1925. It was likely during this time frame 
that he made a map of the Louisville parish that shows the location of not only streets but also houses 
and businesses at the time. His name appears on the map. This map was found in a home at 700 Lincoln 
that until recently was still owned by the Thomas family and was donated to the Louisville Historical 
Museum. Mary Thomas, the home’s former resident, was among the founders of the church, according 
to a Thomas family history.  (She and her husband, Nicholas Thomas, were also associated with 733 Pine 
in Jefferson Place.) 
The date of construction of the church has been well established as having been 1892, but it should be 
noted that the church building does appear in the correct location on the 1909 Drumm’s Wall Map of 
Louisville and on the Methodist Church Map of Louisville that was made in circa 1923-25. The property 
in question is outside of the boundaries of the Sanborn Maps that were done for Louisville in 1893, 
1900, and 1908 (they focused on the downtown business district and La Farge Avenue only).  
The Ladies Aid Society of the Methodist Church was a very active group of women who are credited with 
starting Louisville’s Labor Day Fall Festival that is to this day a crowd-drawing annual event that has 
played a large role in Louisville’s reputation as a festival town. It quickly grew out of an annual church 
bazaar that was started in 1932. The Ladies Aid Society had almost identical members as the Pioneer 
Club and the Saturday Study Club, which operated the Louisville Public Library for decades. The 
following photo, dated 1925, shows the Ladies Aid Society of the Methodist Church: 
 
Page 19 of 150
The woman seated on the farthest right was Jane Carlton, the daughter-in-law of Tom and Jane Carlton. 
She was the widow of their son, David Carlton, and was very active in the Ladies Aid Society and in the 
Methodist Church. As noted by local historian Carolyn Conarroe, in her booklet “A Cloud of Witnesses” 
written for the centennial anniversary of the Methodist Church in 1991,”Some men of the church were 
merchants who put in long hours at their businesses. Many men were coal miners in the early years 
before the mines closed in the 1940’s. Their workloads were so heavy, the hours so long, there were no 
hours left for church work. And the women took over.” 
Carolyn Conarroe further stated in her booklet “A Cloud of Witnesses” that the Methodist Church was 
also used for public education in that it provided space for overflow classes from the public grade school 
across the street. She wrote, “Photographs and the Louisville Times history features tell that the north

2.0 HISTORY AND USE 
 
The following report is taken from the Colorado Cultural Resource Survey, Cultural Resource Re-evaluation

History of 741 Jefferson (Louisville’s Methodist Church) 
 
This report is intended to supplement and update the 2000 survey that was conducted with respect to the 
history of the Methodist Church at 741 Jefferson. This church was the second church building in Louisville and 
is the oldest church still meeting in its original building. 
 
When Charles Welch conveyed this property in 1891 to the “Methodist Episcopal Church” for the construction of 
a church building, it likely made sense for it to be located on Spruce, which was developing into the major east -
west street in downtown Louisville. Maps and firsthand accounts indicate that Spruce was at the time a through 
street to the west, and Pine was not (whereas today, Pine is a through street, and Spruce is not.) By the early to 
mid 1900s, Spruce as it passed through the Jefferson Place Addition included the offices of the Louisville Times 
at 800/804 Spruce, a very nearby store at 805 La Farge, the William Austin store at the northeast corner of 
Spruce and Jefferson, the Methodist Church at 741 Jefferson, and the Louisville grade school. Fart her west on 
Spruce were the brick school house at 801 Grant and the German Lutheran Church at Spruce and McKinley.  
 
The following photo shows the relationship of the Methodist Church (on the left) to the Louisville Grade School 
across Spruce to the north; it was taken in 1961, not long before the school was demolished:

As noted in the 2000 survey, this church was established by, and appealed to, the many English pioneer 
families who came to Louisville in its earliest years. Many of the men were miners who had come from mining 
areas of England. Surnames of families who belonged to this church included (but are not limited to) the 
following prominent Anglo families who lived in Louisville: Carlton, Hutchinson, Dalby, Jenkins, Lawrence, 
Thirlaway, Thomas, Thompson, Ferguson, Robinson, Hilton, Cable, Pickett, and Henning. 
 
Tom Carlton, who was a miner and preacher (though not ordained, apparently), is generally recognized as 
having been the force behind the establishment of the church. He was born in 1824 in the village of Flimby in 
Cumberland, which is on the Irish Sea near Maryport and near the border with Scotland. He married in 1844 to 
Jane McLean (1818-1891) and they had several children. English census records show that he was a “colliery 
viewer,” which was a position similar to that of a foreman. The Carlton family imm igrated to the US in the 1870s. 
By 1880, Tom Carlton was a mining engineer in Gold Hill in Boulder County, and by 1885, he was living in 
Louisville with his family. Records indicate that he was a preacher for Louisville’s English settlers who met in 
private homes prior to the building of the Methodist Church and that he personally raised $800 for the building. 
He died in January 1892, not long after the death of his wife, before the Methodist Episcopal Church could be 
opened.

and writings on the backs of the photos. The photo of Jane Carlton was taken by a local Colorado 
photographer, while the photo of Tom Carlton was taken in Maryport, England (near his home village of Flimby):

The earliest available photo of the church shows a central entrance on the east and a bel tower, although 
records indicate that there may not have ever been a bell installed in that particular tower.  
 
The photo would likely have been taken between 1892 and 1910 (when some modifications were made). In 
these earliest years, the pews faced south and the pulpit was in the south alcove.  
 
The Methodist Church had a strong affiliation with the Iliff School of Theology in Denver. The Iliff School 
provided student pastors for the church. According to local historian Carolyn Conarroe, writing in 1991, “[o]f the 
total 60 ministers who have served Louisville Methodist Church, 57 are thought to have been students.” It was 
true for at least some of these student pastors that they would come up from Denver and stay over the 
weekend in a small room in the building. 
 
One of these student ministers was Ralph Mulhollen, who was assigned by the Iliff School of Theology to be the 
pastor for the Louisville Methodist Church from 1923 to 1925. It was likely during this time frame that he made a 
map of the Louisville parish that shows the location of not only streets but also houses and businesses at the 
time. His name appears on the map. This map was found in a home at 700 Lincoln that until recently was still 
owned by the Thomas family and was donated to the Louisville Historical Museum. Mary Thomas, the home’s 
former resident, was among the founders of the church, according to a Thomas family history. (She and her 
husband, Nicholas Thomas, were also associated with 733 Pine in Jefferson Place.)  
 
The date of construction of the church has been well established as having been 1892, but it should be noted 
that the church building does appear in the correct location on the 1909 Drumm’s Wall Map of Louisville and on 
the Methodist Church Map of Louisville that was made in circa 1923-25. The property in question is outside of 
the boundaries of the Sanborn Maps that were done for Louisville in 1893, 1900, and 1908 (they focus on the 
downtown business district and La Farge Avenue only). 
 
The Ladies Aid Society of the Methodist Church was a very active group of women who are credited with 
starting Louisville’s Labor Day Fall Festival that is to this day a crowd -drawing annual event that has played a 
large role in Louisville’s reputation as a festival town. It quickly grew out of an annual church bazaar that was 
started in 1932. The Ladies Aid Society had almost identical members as the Pioneer Club and the Saturday 
Study Club, which operated the Louisville Public Library for decades. The fol lowing photo, dated 1925, shows 
the Ladies Aid Society of the Methodist Church:

2.1 ARCHITECTURAL SIGNIFICANCE & CONSTRUCTION HISTORY 
 
The commercial property at 741 Jefferson, known as the Louisville United Methodist Church, was built 
sometime around 1892. The church is a typical wood frame vernacular structure of the Louisville area. The 
wood frame construction style is typical of late 19th and early 20th century construction techniques found in the 
Louisville area. Many of the original materials remain and are consistent with other building materials and 
details used on other structures built around the same time in the Louisville area. However, some of the 
construction techniques and details are unique to the church with no known precedent found elsewhere in 
Louisville. 
 
The primary façade faces east to Jefferson Avenue and the form of the church is similar to that seen in the 
earliest photographs of the church with several modifications and additions having taken place over the years . 
Early modifications to the original church include a small south addition, the removal of the original entrance, 
and a new entrance and bell tower constructed on the north side of the original structure. A large addition to the 
rear (west) of the original church occurred in 1941 and another two-story west addition was added in 2000. 
 
Many of the original construction materials remain including structural members, decorative elements, exterior 
siding, doors, and windows. Several original items that were removed early on appear to have been repurposed 
elsewhere in the building including pieces from the original entrance and the original bell tower. 
 
741 Jefferson has the potential to be restored to a high degree of architectural integrity when compared to 
historic photos. Overall, the church is well maintained, with items that require prioritization, as outlined in the 
analysis of this report. The original church is essentially intact with very few modifications since the early 
phases of construction during the late 1800’s and early 1900’s.  
 
741 Jefferson is not listed on the National, State, or Local Register.
