Background / Prior Discussions: 
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND: 
Information from Gigi Yang, Louisville Historical Museum 
The history of 832 McKinley contributes to our knowledge of Louisville’s earliest families 
and immigrant groups from England, Germany, and Wales. Of note is the early 
ownership by the Fischer family, who were well-known in the Louisville area as early 
settlers and as the builders of many homes and buildings in town. The history of the 
Fischer and Lawrence families illustrates a typical Louisville family network with children 
and siblings living close by. The Watts family exemplifies Louisville’s family life in the 
1950s with two working parents, social organizations, and the transition away from coal 
mining.  
 
Mary and Henry Fischer Ownership, 1907-1914 
Mary Fischer was the first owner of this property, having purchased it in 1907 for $130. 
Her husband Henry Fischer and his sons established themselves as reputable builders 
in Louiville having constructed the addition to the original grade school, the State 
Mercantile building, the Baptist Church, Rex Theater (currently Waterloo restaurant), 
Lackner Saloon (currently Azul Fuego), and 740 Front St; in addition to some residential 
homes. It is assumed that Henry and Mary constructed the house at 832 McKinley in 
1907 and lived there with their young children. Boulder County records list the date of 
construction as 1907 and the Drumm’s Wall Map of 1909 shows a house on the 
property as further evidence of construction in 1907. Henry Fischer died in 1912 at the 
age of 42. Mary and their children moved in with her in-laws on Spruce St. Mary sold 
the property to her tenant, who owned the property for 4 years.  
 
Ben & Hannah Lawrence Family Ownership, 1918-1954 
Benjamin and Hannah Lawrence owned 832 McKinley Avenue for 36 years and were 
both English immigrants. During the Lawrence residence, the property was identified by 
the earlier addresses of 333 McKinley, and 820 McKinley, before it became 832 
McKinley in the 1950s. Benjamin came to the Louisville/ Marshall area and worked as a 
coal miner in Louisville’s earliest mines, including the Acme, Ajax, and Leader Mines. 
 
Page 8 of 37
Marvin & Margaret Watts Ownership, 1954-1985 
Marvin and Margaret Watts purchased 832 McKinley from Hannah Lawrence’s children 
in 1954. Over his time in Louisville, Marvin had worked for the coal mines and for the 
Public Services Company as a fire man and pump operator until he retired in 1966. He 
was active in the Louisville Fire Department rescue team and was a member of the 
Masonic Lodge of Lafayette, United Mine Workers of America, and International 
Brotherhood of Electrical Workers. Margaret also worked in town as a checker at 
Dalby’s supermarket (located where the State Mercantile is today). She was a member 
of the Saturday Study Club and hosted many meetings at this house and was also 
involved in the Board of the Public Library as it transitioned from being a volunteer run 
organization to a city owned department between 1921-1981. Marvin Watts passed 
away in 1979 and Margaret continued to live at 832 McKinley until she moved to 
Boulder in 1985 and sold the property to Andrew M. and Mary L. Horton. 
 
Current Ownership 
In 1985, Andrew M and Mary L Horton purchased the property from Margaret Watts. 
They maintained the property as a rental house until they sold it to the current owners, 
David and Jeanette Bangs in 2013.

Summary:  The history of 832 McKinley contributes to our knowledge of Louisville’s earliest families and 
immigrant groups from England, Germany, and Wales. Of note is the early ownership by the Fischer 
family who built many commercial buildings in Louisville. The home is also notable for having only two 
other owners over the next 67 years. The history of the Fischer and Lawrence families illustrates a typical 
Louisville family network with children and siblings living close by. The Watts family exempliﬁes

 Development of the Louisville Heights Addition  
The Louisville Heights Addition was ﬁrst developed in 1904 by the Colorado Mortgage & Investment Co. 
Ltd. In 1905, the company conveyed many of the lots in the Louisville Heights subdivision to Louisville 
residents John Aﬀolter and Dr. Charles Wolfer. Most of the lots remained undeveloped for decades.  
 
Mary Fischer Ownership, 1907-1914 and Oakley Lawton Ownership, 1914-1918 
Mary Fischer was the ﬁrst owner of this property, having purchased it from Charles Wolfer and John 
Affolter in 1907 for $130. Within the Fischer family, there were several Mary (Marie) Fischers but it is 
assumed that the Mary Fischer who purchased the property was Mary Margaret Teten Fischer 
(1872-1936), the wife of Henry Fischer (1870-1912). The Fischer family was well-known in the 
Louisville area as early settlers and as the builders of many homes and buildings in town.  
Herman Henry (H.H.) Fischer (1845-1931) and his wife Marie Brasse (1848-1944) were German 
immigrants who came to the US in 1869 – ﬁrst to Illinois, Missouri, and Nebraska, arriving in 
Colorado in 1887 and then to Louisville in 1896. They had nine children and moved to Louisville with 
Page 15 of 37
three of their adult children (Guy, Paul, and Marta) and settled on Spruce St. on the western edge of 
town.  
Their oldest son, Henry, had married Mary Teten in Nebraska in 1899 and was farming in Araphoe 
County during the 1900 census. It is presumed that shortly after, Henry and Mary moved to 
Louisville and purchased the Louisville Heights Addition property. By then, H.H. and his other sons 
Guy and Paul had established themselves as reputable builders and had already constructed the 
addition to the original grade school, the State Mercantile building, the Baptist Church, Rex Theater 
(currently Waterloo restaurant), Lackner Saloon (currently Azul Fuego), and 740 Front St; in addition 
to some residential homes. 
It is assumed that Henry and Mary (or at least the Fischer family) constructed the house at 832 
McKinley in 1907 and lived there with their young children – Frank (b. 1901), Carolyn (b. 1906) and 
Alice (b. 1910). Boulder County records list the date of construction as 1907 and the Drumm’s Wall 
Map of 1909 shows a house on the property as further evidence of construction in 1907. 
 
1909 Drumm’s Map of Louisville showing Lots 19-20, Block 5 Louisville Heights Addition. 
Henry Fischer began working as a manager at the McAllister Lumber Yard and the 1910 census 
shows the Fischers also taking in Oakley Lawton as a boarder. Lawton worked with Henry at the 
lumber yard. Henry Fischer died in 1912 at the age of 42. Mary and their children moved in with her 
Page 16 of 37
in-laws on Spruce St. and it is possible that Oakley Lawton continued to live at the house as a 
renter.  
In 1914, Mary Fischer sold Lots 19-20, Block 5 to Oakley Lawton for $800. A few months later, 
Lawton married Grace Johnston in Lafayette. It is unclear if the Lawtons lived at this address since 
the 1916 City Directory shows them renting a house on Grant St. south of Hutchinson. Oakley 
Lawton was working as the manager at McAllister Lumber. By 1918, the Lawtons had plans to move 
to California with Grace’s father, A.B. Johnston, and sold the house on McKinley to Ben Lawrence. 
 
Ben & Hannah Lawrence Family Ownership, 1918-1954 
Benjamin and Hannah Lawrence owned this property for 36 years. Benjamin Lawrence (1865-1933) and 
Hannah Lawrence (1865-1953) were both English immigrants. Upon arriving in the US, Benjamin lived 
ﬁrst in Illinois before coming to Colorado. Discrepancies in reported dates confuse the family timeline 
but in the 1880s, Benjamin came to the Louisville/ Marshall area and worked as a coal miner in 
Louisville’s earliest mines, including the Acme, Ajax, and Leader Mines. It is unclear when Hannah 
emigrated from England as census records have noted 1870 and 1880 arrivals; nor is it known how she 
came to Colorado. However, she met and married Benjamin Lawrence in 1885 and they lived in Marshall 
with their daughter Mary (b. 1886 in Illinois), until the birth of their oldest son, Fred in Marshall in 1890. 
After moving to Louisville, they lived near the Ajax and Acme Mines (Lot 16, Block 5 of Johnson’s First 
Addition) and in other locations around Louisville prior to purchasing 832 McKinley in 1918. They had 
two more sons, Arthur (b. 1891) and Albert “Lob” (b. 1893), and three more daughters, Eva (b. 1896), 
Violet (b. 1901), and Thelma (b. 1903).

were living with them. Their sons had married and lived in the surrounding neighborhoods. Arthur

custodian at the Louisville grade school for almost 30 years. In 1933, Ben Lawrence passed away and his 
funeral was held at this house. Hannah continued to live there on her own and later with her son Albert 
until her death in 1953.

SUMMARY 
The applicant is requesting approval to demolish the existing single family home, 
garage, and detached guesthouse at 181 Roosevelt Avenue. As the structure was 
constructed prior to 1955, the demolition review must be reviewed by the Historic 
Preservation Commission as per Louisville Municipal Code (LMC) Section 15.36.020. 
The applicant intends to rebuild the home on the property without preserving or 
rehabilitating any potentially historic aspects of the existing home.  
 
Louisville Municipal Code (LMC) Section 15.36.020 defines demolition as an act that 
Page 21 of 37
removes fifty percent or more of the roof area as measured from directly above, fifty 
percent or more of the exterior walls of a building as measured contiguously around the 
building, or alteration on any exterior wall facing a street. The purpose of demolition 
reviews, according to Section 15.36.200, is as follows:  
 
The purpose of the review of permit applications for demolition, moving, 
and removal of buildings constructed in or before 1955 is to prevent the 
loss of buildings that may have historical or architectural significance. The 
purpose of this chapter is also to provide the time necessary to initiate 
designation as an individual landmark or to consider alternatives for the 
building. 
Under Section 15.36.200 of the LMC, if the commission finds that the building may have 
historical significance under the criteria, the commission may place a stay on the 
demolition and, “no permit for demolition, moving or removal shall be issued for a period 
not to exceed 180 days from the date the permit application was accepted.” In 
addition, Section 15.36.200 states, “The commission will make all reasonable efforts to 
expedite resolution of the application or request.”  
 
HISTORICAL BACKGROUND: 
Information from Gigi Yang, Louisville Historical Museum 
 
The history of 181 Roosevelt is notable for its unique evolution as a property on the 
rural edge of Louisville city limits and proximity to several early mines. Central to its 
history is the unresolved date of construction and the fire that destroyed what is 
considered to be the original home. Peter and Leah Blankette sued the Public Service 
Co. for damages and their case set precedents for future Colorado lawsuits. The 
property also contributes to the immigration story of English, Italian, and French families 
in Louisville and illustrates the lifestyle of coal-mining families into the mid-20th century. 
 
Early Ownership 
Johnson’s 1st Addition was platted in 1890 by Mahlon V. Johnson. It was the second 
residential subdivision to be platted, after the Jefferson Place Addition was platted in 
1880. The earliest known deed for Lot 15, Block 1, is to Lawrence W. Spangler who 
purchased the land from the Ajax Coal Mining Co. in 1893 for $65. Not much is known 
about Lawrence Spangler other than that he was in the Louisville area between 1893 
and 1906. The Ajax Mine operated for a short time from 1889-1892, and it is unclear 
whether the house on this property was originally part of the Ajax Mine or was built or 
moved in later. In 1897, Lawrence Spangler sold the property to Joseph H. Fearn and it 
is assumed that Fearn lived here, making the likeliest date of construction for the house 
to be between 1892-1897. John Blake purchased the property from Joseph Stengel in 
1905 and owned it for almost twenty years, however very little is known about him.  
 
The Fire and Peter and Leah Blankette (Blanketti) 1923-1952 
Peter and Leah Blankette (Blanketti) owned the property between 1923-1952. In March 
of 1928, the Blankette house caught on fire during a windstorm and burned down. The 
report of the fire in the Rocky Mountain Daily describes a “six-room house…at the edge 
Page 22 of 37
of Louisville.” This description implies that there was an original, larger house on the 
property and that the current house was not identified as 181 Roosevelt until after 1928. 
The Drumm’s Wall Maps from 1909 and 1925 both show two structures on this lot and it 
is not known which structure was the principal family home that burned down. No 
additional records, photos, or floorplans were discovered that show what the original 
buildings may have looked like. In December of 1928, Peter and Leah Blankette filed a 
lawsuit against the Public Service Company of Colorado claiming that the destruction of 
their home was due to faulty wiring that caused the fire. During these years, the 
Blankettes no longer had a house and the 1930 census shows them living at either the 
Monarch Coal Camp #2 or Coalton Camp. Peter continued working at the local mines 
and likely moved into company housing.  
 
Boulder County’s current online records give 1892 as the house’s date of construction. 
However, the date offered by the County could not be verified by other documents, 
including the 1948 County Assessor card which does not list any date of construction 
but does show the house on the property with the address of 341 Roosevelt. As was 
sometimes done, the date of construction may have been copied from other houses in 
the neighborhood which were constructed roughly between 1892-1915. The style of the 
house is in keeping with other miner’s homes in Louisville during this time period. It is 
worth considering whether the current house at 181 Roosevelt was moved from one of 
the mine camps since the design of the current building is a traditional style for miner’s 
housing and it may have been more economical for the Blankettes to purchase and 
move an older house than to rebuild or build a new house. However, both the 1909 and 
1925 Drumm’s map show two buildings on the property and it is also possible that the 
current house was the second building that was already on the lot, potentially left from 
the Ajax mine era. A building from the Ajax Mine, being one of the earliest mines, could 
explain the assigned 1892 date of construction for a building left on the property. 
 
Recent Ownership 
Several other families lived at the home after the Blankette's with limited social 
significance. From 1977 to 1983, the house was used as a rental property or left vacant. 
In 1980, Louisville City Council annexed the parcel to the City of Louisville and changed 
the house number from 341 Roosevelt to 181 Roosevelt to align with other properties 
on the same block. Joslin sold 181 Roosevelt to William and Elizabeth Keane in 1983. 
The Keanes lived at this address until 1988 when they sold it to George Campbell who 
lived there until 2010.

ARCHITECTURAL INTEGRITY: 
181 Roosevelt Avenue is not listed in the City's architectural survey, PaleoWest “Stories 
in Places” (2018) residential context.  181 Roosevelt Avenue was not surveyed as part 
of PaleoWest’s “100 Architectural Inventories" (2023). The property is located outside

Year of Construction: circa 1892-1897 
Summary: The history of 181 Roosevelt is notable for its unique evolution as a property on the rural 
edge of Louisville city limits and proximity to several early mines. Central to its history is the unresolved 
date of construction and the ﬁre that destroyed the original home. Peter and Leah Blankette sued the 
Public Service Co. for damages and their case set precedents for future Colorado lawsuits. The property 
also contributes to the immigration story of English, Italian, and French families in Louisville and 
illustrates the lifestyle of coal-mining families into the mid-20th century. 
Development of Johnson’s First Addition 
Johnson’s 1st Addition was platted in 1890 by Mahlon V. Johnson. It was the second residential 
subdivision to be platted, after the Jeﬀerson Place Addition was platted in 1880. (It consists of two 
separate sections that do not border one another, though both abut Roosevelt.)  
Mahlon V. “Ajax” Johnson was a Denver-based coal mine operator who started the Ajax Coal Mining Co. 
in 1889 and served as its president. According to 1889 articles in Denver newspapers, the original intent 
was to establish a new town by Louisville to be called “Ajaxville” with proximity to the new Ajax Mine. 
However, instead, Johnson platted this subdivision that became part of the town of Louisville. The Ajax 
Mine operated from 1889-1892. 
 
Early Property Ownership 
The earliest known deed for Lot 15, Block 1, is to Lawrence W. Spangler who purchased the land from 
the Ajax Coal Mining Co. in 1893 for $65. The Ajax Mine operated for a short time from 1889-1892, and 
Page 30 of 37
it is unclear whether the house on this property was originally part of the Ajax Mine or was built or 
moved in later. 
Boulder County’s current online records give 1892 as the house’s date of construction. However, the date 
oﬀered by the County could not be veriﬁed by other documents, including the 1948 County Assessor 
card which does not list any date of construction but does show the house on the property with the 
address of 341 Roosevelt. As was sometimes done, the date of construction may have been copied from 
other houses in the neighborhood which were constructed roughly between 1892-1915. The style of the 
house is in keeping with other miner’s homes in Louisville during this time period. 
Not much is known about Lawrence Spangler other than that he was in the Louisville area between 1893 
and 1906. He is listed as a member of the Knights of Pythias in Louisville in 1894. Around this time, 
Mahlon V. Johnson was the superintendent for the Fox Coal Mining Co., which operated the Fox Mine in 
Marshall. Johnson also opened the Ajax Mine in Louisville in 1889. Spangler is noted in the local 
newspaper as being from Marshall in 1895 during the birth of his son, so it is likely that he spent time 
working at the Fox and Ajax mines. He borrowed funds from Eliza Autrey and from the Lockwood Trading  
Company between 1896-1906. Lockwood Trading was a mining supply store located in Louisville in the 
vicinity of the Ajax Mine. Whether Spangler built or lived at the house on Roosevelt is unknown. In 1897, 
Lawrence Spangler sold the property to Joseph H. Fearn and it is assumed that Fearn lived here, making 
the likeliest date of construction for the house to be between 1892-1897. 
Joseph and Elizabeth Fearn Ownership, 1897-1904 
Joseph and Elizabeth Fearn moved to 181 Roosevelt with their one-year old son, Ernest in 1897.  Joseph 
Fearn (1869-1936) was born in England and came the US with his parents Henry and Anna Fearn in 1877 
at 8 years old, along with his younger sister Mary Ann. The family ﬁrst went to Illinois where Joseph’s 
father found work as a coal miner and by age 12, Joseph was also working in the coal mines. The family 
continued moving west and in 1895, Joseph met and married Elizabeth Bush (1870-1957) in Nebraska. 
In 1895, Joseph and Elizabeth came to Colorado, possibly following his sister Mary Ann who had married 
Isaac Robbins and was already living in Louisville by then. The Fearns daughter, Lela May, was born in 
1900 and both Ernest and Lela May lived their early years at the house on Roosevelt.  The family sold the 
house in 1904 and were living on Second St. (Main St.) by 1910 when Joseph Fearn ran for Town Trustee 
on the Socialist ticket. The Fearns moved to Nebraska around the time of the 1920 census.  
Joseph Stengel and his wife Mary Dornberger Stengel were German immigrants who brieﬂy owned the 
property from 1904-1905. The Stengels already had seven children when they purchased the lot, and it is 
not known whether they lived at this address. However, they farmed in Louisville and the surrounding 
area for multiple generations with descendants still in the area today.  
 
John Blake Ownership, 1905-1923 
Although John Blake purchased the property from Joseph Stengel in 1905 and owned it for almost 
twenty years, very little is known about him. An obituary for John S Blake in 1930 lists several members 
of the Stengel family as his pallbearers so it is possible that this John Blake is the same one that owned 
Lot 15, Block 1 of Johnson’s First Addition. He is identiﬁed as a carpenter from Boulder, and also as 
Page 31 of 37
working in diﬀerent mines. However, no John Blake appears in any of the Louisville directories from 
1916-1926 and there is no mention of him in Louisville newspaper articles so it cannot be determined 
that he actually lived on the property or in Louisville. It is possible that the house was used as a rental 
property or left vacant. During this time period, this address was on the rural outskirts of town and not 
speciﬁcally listed in any directories. In fact, other Johnson’s First Addition homes were sometimes 
incorrectly listed as being part of Kimberly Place which was a more developed subdivision just to the 
north. In 1923, Blake sold the property to Pete Blankette. 
 
Peter and Leah Blankette (Blanketti) Family Ownership, 1923-1952 
Peter Blankette (1886-1950) was an Italian coal miner who came to the US in 1910 at age 24. Although 
he was born in Italy, his father was Italian and his mother was French and Peter’s native language was 
French. Upon arriving in the US, he traveled ﬁrst to Pennsylvania and then came to Colorado where he 
worked as a digger on the Moﬀatt Coal lines in Routt County.  
Leah Wattelet Brunet (1884-1949) was born in France and married Leorence Brunet (b. 1881) in 1902 
when she was 18. They already had two daughters, Lea (b. 1902) and Bertha (b. 1904) when they sailed 
for the US in1905. Leorence worked as a coal miner in Illinois where their son Leon was born in 1906 and 
the family then moved to Colorado by 1909 where their youngest daughter Elsie was born. Leorence 
Brunet died quite young prior to 1920. It is unknown exactly how or when he died but Leah is listed as a 
widow in the 1920 census, living only with Leon (age 13) and Elsie (age 11) who were both attending 
school. In 1920, Peter Blankette moved to Louisville where he met and married Leah Brunet. They lived 
on Front Street until they purchased the house on Roosevelt in 1923. In 1927, Peter and Leah’s daughter 
Jacquiline was born.  
In March of 1928, when Jacquiline was only an infant, the Blankette 
house caught on ﬁre during a windstorm and burned down. The report 
of the ﬁre in the Rocky Mountain Daily describes a “six-room house…at 
the edge of Louisville.” This description implies that there was an 
original, larger house on the property and that the current house was 
not identiﬁed as 181 Roosevelt until after 1928.  
The Drumm’s Wall Maps from 1909 and 1925 both show two structures 
on this lot and it is not known which structure was the principal family 
home that burned down. No additional records, photos, or ﬂoorplans 
were discovered that show what the original buildings may have looked 
like. In the early 1900s, Johnson’s First Addition was outside of Louisville 
city limits and consequently not as well documented. City directories 
and census records did not include street names or listings other than 
just “Louisville” for residences in this area.  Both maps show how 
separate Johnson’s Addition is and how Lot 15, Block 1 is even further 
separated from the northern addition which was frequently mis-labeled 
as being part of the Kimberly Addition.

In December of 1928, Peter and Leah Blankette ﬁled a lawsuit against the Public Service Company of 
Colorado claiming that the destruction of their home was due to faulty wiring that caused the ﬁre. They 
sued for $4,325 in damages with the house valued at $2,000 and contents at $2,325.  According to the 
Boulder Daily Camera report, “It is charged that the loss of the Blankette home by ﬁre March 25, last, 
was caused by carelessness and negligence on the part of the company in allowing lines, poles, wires, 
conduits, and other appurtances near the Blankette home to remain in dangerous condition.” 
It is notable that Peter was only formally educated through the 3rd grade, and Leah through 8th grade and 
yet they hired a lawyer to ﬁle their suit and were able to see it through the long legal process. Their case 
did not go to trial until June of 1929 after which it was dismissed for failure to show enough evidence to 
prove their claim. The Blankettes appealed the decision, bringing the case to the State Supreme Court 
where the judgement was reversed and remanded. Blankette v. Public Service Co. of Colorado set a legal 
precedent in Colorado law that deﬁned “reasonable care” and required public utility companies to show 
“highest degree of care” in installing and maintaining equipment.  
During these years, the Blankettes no longer had a house and the 1930 census shows them living at 
either the Monarch Coal Camp #2 or Coalton Camp. Peter continued working at the local mines and 
likely moved into company housing. Both of these mine camps were located on the borders of Superior 
near the current Redtail Ridge development and the Flatiron Crossing Mall.
