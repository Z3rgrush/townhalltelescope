Chapter 18.58 of the Golden Municipal Code establishes the criteria for Certificate of 
Appropriateness review. The Board evaluates the historic, geographic, and 
architectural value of the structure, its materials and features, its relationship to 
surrounding buildings, and the compatibility of the proposed work in terms of materials, 
scale, size, height, design, placement, and use.
The following discussion evaluates the proposed alterations in relation to these criteria.
1. Historic, Geographic, and Architectural Value
The property is located within the East Street Historic District, and the rear unit is 
identified by the applicant as a contributing structure.
The applicant’s letter provides information regarding the structure’s construction history 
and the condition of the existing siding. The applicant asserts that the structure has 
undergone substantial reconstruction and that the existing siding is a later replacement 
rather than historic material.
While this information provides context for the proposed alterations, the Board’s review 
should consider the structure’s designation and the available evidence regarding its 
historic integrity. The proposed work should maintain or enhance the building’s 
contribution to the district, as applicable, and avoid unnecessary loss of historic 
character.
The proposed replacement of deteriorated siding is intended to rehabilitate the existing 
structure without changing its overall form or placement.
2. Texture and Materials
The existing structure is clad primarily in horizontal siding. The proposed elevations 
identify replacement of the existing horizontal siding on the north and south elevations 
in kind.
The east and west elevations are proposed to receive vertical clear cedar siding. The 
use of vertical siding introduces a different texture and orientation from the existing 
horizontal siding.
The proposed material change is limited to the east and west elevations and is 
intended to distinguish these portions of the structure from the remaining horizontal 
siding.
The use of wood siding is generally compatible with the material vocabulary of 
Golden’s historic residential districts. The proposed vertical cedar siding introduces a 
contemporary interpretation of a traditional material rather than attempting to replicate 
a historic siding profile.
 Page 5
The Board should consider whether the proposed vertical siding is appropriate in 
relation to the existing structure and the surrounding district, particularly with regard to 
its texture, finish, and visual prominence.
The applicant’s letter identifies the proposed siding as natural cedar. The submitted 
elevations do not establish a specific stain or finish. The final material and finish should 
be consistent with the submitted design and should not introduce an overly reflective or 
visually prominent treatment.
3. Relationship to Surrounding Structures and the Historic District
The proposed alterations retain the existing building footprint, roof form, and general 
massing. The work does not propose an increase in building height or a change in the 
structure’s placement on the property.
The use of horizontal siding on the north and south elevations maintains the existing 
siding orientation on those portions of the building. The proposed vertical siding on the 
east and west elevations creates a variation in material texture while retaining a wood-
based exterior treatment.
The proposed alterations are generally consistent with the scale and utilitarian 
character of an accessory residential structure.
The Board should consider the visibility of the east and west elevations from public 
streets and adjacent properties, as well as the relationship of the proposed siding to 
the primary residence and nearby contributing structures.
The proposed work does not appear to introduce a substantial change in the building’s 
overall relationship to the surrounding neighborhood.
4. Scale, Size, Height, Design, and Placement
The submitted elevations indicate that the existing building form and roof configuration 
will remain.
The proposed alterations are limited to exterior materials and windows. No change to 
the building footprint, overall height, or placement is proposed.
The retention of the existing building form helps maintain the structure’s relationship to 
the surrounding historic district.
The proposed siding changes are limited to the exterior walls and do not appear to 
alter the overall architectural composition of the building.
The Board should evaluate the proposed window alterations to ensure that the size, 
placement, proportions, and general character of the openings remain compatible with 
the structure and the historic district.
5. Compatibility with Historic Preservation Principles
The proposed work is intended to rehabilitate an existing structure while retaining its 
overall form and placement.

The applicant’s approach includes retaining the existing horizontal siding profile on the 
north and south elevations while introducing vertical cedar siding on the east and west 
elevations.
The proposed work does not attempt to replicate a specific historic architectural style. 
Instead, it uses traditional wood siding materials in a manner intended to maintain 
compatibility with the surrounding district.
The proposal is generally consistent with the objective of preserving the overall 
character of the historic district while allowing alterations that accommodate the 
continued use and rehabilitation of existing structures.

Based on the submitted application materials and the applicable Certificate of 
Appropriateness review criteria in Chapter 18.58 of the Golden Municipal Code, staff 
finds that the proposed alterations to 2211 Ford Street, Unit B, are generally

massing. The replacement of the existing horizontal siding and the introduction of 
vertical cedar siding are limited to the exterior materials and do not substantially alter 
the building’s overall form or relationship to the surrounding neighborhood.

Background of Property:
Location
The property is located at 622 Water Street on the northeast corner of 
Jackson Street and Water Street. The parcel extends  north toward 10th 
Street and is situated within a mixed-use area of downtown Golden.
Across Jackson Street from a public park, the prope rty is surrounded by 
residential, commercial, and mixed-use development. The main residence 
occupies the center of the parcel, while the north duplex is near the 10th 
Street frontage.
The property historically occupied a prominent posi tion within the early 
development of Golden. Its location near Clear Creek and downtown Golden 
reflects the development of the community during th e transition from a 
transportation and mining-support center to an established agricultural and 
industrial community.
Historical Background
The Barnes-Peery Residence was constructed in 1865 by David Barnes, a 
flour merchant who established the Golden Mill near  Clear Creek. The 
residence was constructed as a substantial two-stor y brick dwelling 
incorporating Italianate architectural features.
An addition was constructed in 1867, extending the residence north toward 
Jackson Street. Subsequent additions and alterations included a two-story 
bathroom addition constructed around 1938 and later one-story additions. 
The residence was originally constructed as a single-family dwelling and was 
subsequently converted to multiple residential unit s. The building has 
continued to serve residential uses, including rental apartments.
The north duplex was constructed later and appears on the 1886 Sanborn 
fire insurance map. The building has undergone nume rous alterations, 
including changes to its footprint, windows, and ex terior materials. The 
property has been associated with several owners, including David Barnes, 
the Quaintance family, William Llewellyn, and the Peery family. The Peery 
family acquired the property in 1920 and maintained ownership for several 
decades.
 Page 5
The property was designated as a historic site by the City of Golden through 
Ordinance No. 1194. The main residence was subseque ntly listed in the 
National Register of Historic Places in 2001 as the Barnes-Peery Residence.
Architecture
The main residence is a two-story brick building designed in Italianate style. 
Its architectural characteristics include:
• A low-pitched hipped roof.
• Widely overhanging boxed eaves.
• A paneled wood cornice with paired brackets.
• Tall, narrow, double-hung windows.
• Segmentally arched window openings and decorative lintels.
• A stone water table and foundation.
• A symmetrical south-facing façade with a central entrance.
The 1867 addition continues the architectural featu res of the original 
residence, including the brick construction, roof d etailing, and window 
arrangement. The later additions are primarily loca ted at the rear of the 
building and are less visible from the principal Water Street frontage. The 
National Register nomination identifies the residence as a rare surviving 
example of the Italianate architectural style in Go lden and notes that the 
building retains sufficient historic integrity to c onvey its significance. The 
north duplex is a one-and-a-half-story brick building with a hipped roof and 
later additions. The National Register nomination i dentifies the duplex, 
constructed in 1875, as noncontributing due to alterations to its footprint and 
architectural features.
NATIONAL REGISTER OF HISTORIC PLACES
National Register Listing
The Barnes-Peery Residence was listed in the National Register of Historic 
Places in 2001 under Criterion C, which recognizes properties that embody 
the distinctive characteristics of a type, period, or method of construction; 
represent the work of a master; possess high artistic values; or represent a 
significant and distinguishable entity.
 Page 6
The National Register nomination identifies the pro perty’s areas of 
significance as:
• Architecture.
• Community Planning and Development.
The period of significance is 18651867. The nomina tion identifies the 
residence as one of the earliest surviving dwellings in Golden and a rare 
example of the Italianate style. The building’s arc hitectural features, 
substantial brick construction, and association with the early development of 
Golden contribute to its historic significance. The nomination also identifies 
the north duplex as a noncontributing building due to alterations that have 
affected its historic integrity.
What National Register Listing Means
The National Register of Historic Places is the federal government’s official 
list of properties recognized for their historical or architectural significance. 
Listing recognizes the property’s importance to the  history, architecture, 
archaeology, engineering, or culture of the nation, state, or community.
National Register listing provides formal recognition of a property’s historic 
significance and may provide access to certain pres ervation incentives, 
including federal historic rehabilitation tax credi ts when applicable 
requirements are met. However, National Register listing does not, by itself, 
prohibit a private property owner from altering, se lling, or demolishing a 
property.
For privately owned properties, National Register listing generally does not 
impose direct federal restrictions on property owne rs unless a federal 
undertaking, funding source, permit, or other federal involvement triggers 
applicable review requirements. The National Register listing also does not 
independently establish local land-use or demolition restrictions.
In this case, the property is additionally designated as a historic site by the 
City of Golden. The City’s local historic designation and Chapter 18.58 of the 
Golden Municipal Code provide the basis for the Cit y’s review of the 
demolition request.
 Page 7
The Board’s review should therefore focus on the City’s applicable historic 
preservation requirements, the property’s historic significance, the proposed 
demolition, and the potential for alternatives that would preserve the historic 
resource.
Local Historic Designation
The property was designated as a historic site by the City of Golden through 
Ordinance No. 1194. The local designation establish es the property as 
subject to the City’s historic preservation review process. The applicant is 
therefore required to obtain a Certificate of Appropriateness for demolition of 
the designated historic structures.
The National Register listing and local designation are separate recognition 
mechanisms. The National Register listing recognize s the property’s 
significance at the federal level, while the local designation establishes the 
City’s authority to review proposed changes in accordance with the Golden 
Municipal Code.
The north duplex’s noncontributing status in the National Register nomination 
does not independently remove it from local review.  The Board should 
consider the scope of the City’s local designation and applicable code 
requirements when reviewing demolition of the duplex.

September 21, 2026, sealed by David Watanabe, P.E., is enclosed, and we are putting its findings in front 
of the Board directly. 
As highlighted in the report, the main house is structurally sound. The basement stone foundation is in 
excellent condition for the age of the structure, the floor joists are in very good condition, and the 
masonry cracking observed “could be remedied with patchwork brick replacement and mortar 
repointing.” The north structure is likewise reported to be in good condition. Structural failure is not the 
basis for this application. 
The property does carry significant deferred maintenance predating our ownership: the roof, the boiler 
system, aging plumbing fixtures, a sewer line that requires constant maintenance, and an on-going 
rodent infestation issue. These are maintenance, utility, and building-systems conditions. They bear on 
the cost of any path forward rather than on the soundness of the structure. 
What We Have Studied 
Working with our architect, development consultants, and civil engineer, we have run this site against 
four product types, each tested with the historic house in place: 
• Multifamily, including an affordability component, with ground-floor retail 
• Hospitality, with ground-floor retail 
• Hospitality, without ground-floor retail 
• Townhomes, and residential condominiums with a hospitality component 
We also studied relocating the house to a different position on the lot. The estimated weight of the 
structure is well over 1,000,000 pounds, which makes moving it technically challenging and prohibitively 
expensive. In every configuration, the location of the structure has prevented a workable relationship 
between parking, access, and building placement. The constraint is the position of the building, not its 
condition and not its merit. 
Where This Stands 
We have not closed the door on adaptive reuse, and we are continuing to test whether a project that 
retains the 1865 house can be made to work here. We do, however, require this application to preserve 
maximum flexibility while this work continues.  
Carrying the Site’s History Forward 
Whether or not the 1865 house remains standing, we honor this property’s history and are committed 
to working collaboratively with the Board through this application process for options and alternatives 
as may be possible. We seek to develop these options alongside the project rather than fix them now, 
and we welcome the Board’s input through this application process. 
The North Structure 
The 2001 National Register nomination assesses the north duplex as noncontributing, noting that “the 
building’s overall footprint has changed several times over the years and because of numerous 
alterations.” We ask the Board to consider this structure separately from the 1865 house.

DESCRIPTION
The Barnes-Peery Residence is situated on the northeast corner of Jackson and Water Streets, on the 
north side of Clear Creek. It is located in a mixed-use neighborhood with an apartment and a 
commercial building to the south and east, respectively. A city park is immediately to the west across a 
small parking lot and the four-lane 10th Avenue lies to the north. When viewed from the principal 
elevation on the south, you see an imposing house positioned on a large open lot with mature 
landscaping. The building is a two-story brick house with simple Italianate features set on a stone water 
table and foundation. Despite the symmetrical southern fa9ade, a walk around the building indicates the 
house has an irregular plan showing four distinct stages of construction as follows:
1) the original 1865 two-story red brick rectangular building facing south onto Water Street. It has 
a low-pitched, hipped roof and widely overhanging boxed eaves with a paneled cornice and 
paired brackets. The roof is covered in corrugated metal. The tall, narrow, double-hung sash, 
wood frame windows have stone lugsills. The windows on the south side are surmounted by 
segmentally arched wooden lintels with molded trim, while those on the west and east sides have 
segmentally arched stone lintels. There is a central porch on the south side.
2) an 1867 two-story red brick rectangular wing extending north and facing Jackson Street to the 
west. It has a low-pitched hipped roof and widely overhanging boxed eaves with a paneled 
cornice and paired brackets, similar to the original building, only slightly smaller in height. The 
roof is covered in sheet metal. The tall, narrow, double-hung sash wood frame windows have 
stone lintels and lugsills.
3) a circa 1938 two-story, variegated brick addition within the ell formed by the two earlier sections. 
It has a shed roof covered in rolled asphalt that slopes to the east and two jack arch, wood frame, 
double hung sash windows with brick lugsills. This addition contains the house's bathrooms.
4) a one-story wood frame addition at the back, on the north and east sides, wrapping around the 
bathroom addition. Apparently built in two sections, it has an asphalt-covered shed roof that 
slopes east, horizontal siding, fixed sash ribbon windows, and is used for storage. Construction 
dates for these frame additions have not been determined.

SIGNIFICANCE
The Barnes-Peery Residence is eligible under criterion C for its significance in the areas of Architecture 
and Community Planning and Development. The house is representative of a type and period of 
construction. With its low pitched roof, widely overhanging eaves supported by large elaborate brackets, 
tall narrow windows, and curved decorative window crowns, the house is a rare extant example of the 
Italianate style in Golden. The Barnes Peery Residence is also one of the earliest extant dwellings in 
Golden, dating to 1865. Possibly the first two-story brick house in Golden using a coherent architectural 
style, newspaper reports of the day considered the house one of the finest residences in the town. This 
house has an intimate association with the early community development of Golden and its owner made 
a concerted effort to introduce a distinctive architectural style in the earliest years of Golden's 
development. The building has survived remarkably well with its Italianate features and streetscape 
appearance relatively unchanged and additions placed discretely at the rear of the building. While there 
are other houses in Golden showing an Italianate influence, most have undergone alterations that have 
obscured or eliminated many of the style's characteristic features. One notable exception is the 1879 
Kelly Mansion at 920 Twelfth Street (located within the National Register listed Twelfth Street Historic 
Residential District). However, the Barnes-Peery Residence pre-dates this building by 14 years and may 
have even served as its inspiration.
In the mid-1860s, the town of Golden was witnessing a transition from a transportation hub servicing the 
Clear Creek gold towns to a settled community with its own agricultural and industrial economy. 
Wealthy merchants, like David Barnes, contributed to the growth of this community by investing in their 
residences and businesses. Barnes constructed his home at 622 Water Street as a symbol of his growing 
prosperity. Barnes chose to build using Italianate elements common in the more developed parts of the 
country but possibly unseen in a frontier town like Golden in the mid 1860s. Barnes likely intended the 
styling and size of the building to impress to other residents of Golden. His efforts appeared successful, 
as evident by an article in the Rocky Mountain News of 28 February 1866 which stated that "When 
finished, this [house] will be one of the most elegant and convenient residences in Colorado. It is of 
brick, two stories high, and containing all the modern conveniences of a first-class residence, and will be 
one of the chief ornaments of the town." Barnes' construction of his large brick home using a coherent 
architectural style was a statement of his presence and permanence in this community. As a recent 
immigrant to Colorado from the East coast, Barnes may have wanted to introduce an Eastern sense of 
culture and sophistication to Golden, using his mansion as a tangible statement of his own good taste. 
While the newspaper articles suggest this attitude, it is impossible to determine if Barnes held these 
opinions or if these comments were the stylistic manner of a newspaper reporter.
Barnes was the owner of a large flour mill, the Golden Mill, located just one block away on the banks of 
Clear Creek at Ford Street. A Pennsylvania native, Barnes came to Colorado to Russell Gulch in 1861. 
He returned to the East to bring his wife Sarah and family to Colorado, where he built a flour mill on 
Bear Creek. In 1864, he relocated to Golden, where he proceeded to build a large flour mill on the banks

claim of territorial capital to upstart Denver.
The transition of Golden from a transportation center supplying the gold towns of Clear Creek to an 
emerging agricultural and industrial center was the result of a growing merchant class investing in the 
community. David Barnes was a leader in this group contributing a sense of permanence and prosperity 
to Golden. His attempts to bring Eastern culture and refinements, such as the popular Italianate styling 
and even a picket fence, to this frontier town were observed and imitated by other citizens. This house 
served as a standard bearer in this community's search for authenticity and permanence.

Historical Background
In a warranty deed dated 28 August 1865, William A. Loveland and his wife sold to Sarah Barnes 
(David Bames's wife) Lots 1 through 4 of Block 34, Golden City, the north side of Clear Creek for 
$100. A Rocky Mountain News article in November 1865 reported that Barnes was erecting "an elegant 
brick dwelling" and by February the following year, the newspaper commented on the near completion 
of the building. The rectangular plan (approximately 26x30), two-story home using a simple Italianate 
design, was soon enlarged. In 1867, Barnes constructed a two-story addition using the same 
architectural features. The Golden Transcript noted this construction hi an article published on August 
21, 1867. The house with its additions appears on the 1873 Strobridge birds eye view map of Golden 
and on the 1878 Willits Map of the city. Sanborn Insurance Maps from 1886 [the earliest available] 
through 1911 identify the house with its addition as 109 Waters Street, while subsequent maps give the 
address as 622 Waters.
When Barnes moved to Loveland, ownership of the property (and the flour mill) transferred to A.J. 
Quaintance in 1878. Sanborn Insurance maps of 1886, 1890 and 1895 show no changes to the building 
since the construction of the north wing in 1867. The 1900 Sanborn map shows the presence of a one- 
story addition located within the ell formed by the original building and the north wing. In 1901, 
ownership of the property transferred to William Llewllyn. A Sanborn map of 1906 shows the addition 
of a front porch and a small square addition to the northeast corner of the original building. This 
footprint of the building remains unchanged on subsequent Sanborn Maps. [The last available map is a 
1919 corrected to October 1938.]
In 1920, the Peery brothers, William Wynn and Ira Walter, bought the home, and these two brothers and 
their relatives held on to the property for more than six decades. The 1932 city directory shows a George 
F. Peery owning and living in the house. From at least the late 1930s through the late 1940s, the house 
was subdivided into rental apartments. The 1938 city directory has four families living in the building, 
and yet the October 1938 Sanborn Map does not show the two-story brick bathroom addition. It is 
assumed that this four-bathroom addition must have been built shortly after in order to accommodate the 
four families residing in the house. Three families were living at the address in 1941, and there were 
three individual renters in 1947. The 1959 directory lists a Minnie Peery and one renter at the address.
Ford Street Management acquired the property in 1984, succeeded by the Adolph Coors Company in 
1986. The current owners, William and Roberta Law, acquired the property in 1993 and that same year, 
the Golden Historic Preservation Board locally designated the Barnes-Peery Residence.
