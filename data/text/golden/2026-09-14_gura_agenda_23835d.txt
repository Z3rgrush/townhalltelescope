1. New Junction Plan Area Update – Financial Feasibility Report 
Attached to this memo is the Financial Feasibility Report prepared by GURA’s chosen 
consultant, Pioneer Development Company, which is evaluating the proposed Junction 
Urban Renewal Plan, a 51.28-acre light industrial business park on the west side of State 
Highway 93. The Conditions Survey identified five statutory blighting factors on the 
property, including a coal mine subsidence hazard across roughly half the parcel, 
expansive soils, and a complete absence of public infrastructure, that have kept the land 
vacant and undeveloped. The project engineer's estimate places required public 
improvements at just over $59 million, or approximately $118 per square foot of 
programmed building area, a cost that consumes more than half of the project's estimated 
build-out value and cannot reasonably be recovered through private financing alone. 
The report's core analysis, prepared with underwriting support from the development 
team’s consultant, Piper Sandler, compares the Metropolitan District's bonding capacity 
with and without the Authority's participation via TIF financing. Relying solely on its own 
debt service mill levy, the District could generate only about $14.2 million toward 
infrastructure costs, leaving a $44.9 million gap that would require rents 45–53% above 
what the Golden/Jefferson County industrial market will support, effectively making the 
project infeasible. With the Authority's proposed tax increment pledge, the District's 
Pkt Pg 2
bonding capacity more than doubles to $35.1 million, narrowing the private funding gap 
to $23.9 million, an amount Evergreen has committed to finance based on a track record 
of similar public-private partnerships exceeding $90 million in delivered infrastructure 
across the region. Notably, because the parcel currently generates zero property tax, this 
pledge does not redirect existing revenue from any taxing entity; it allocates only future 
increment that would not exist but for the development itself. 
Staff recommends that the Board review this analysis  and consider approaches to 
negotiate with the taxing entities. The report’s recommended revenue-sharing structure 
protects the Jefferson County School District's Bond Redemption and Mill Levy Override 
revenues (an estimated $13.4 million preserved over the Plan's term), and the Impact 
Report confirms low-level fiscal impacts across all affected taxing entities. In exchange, 
the completed project would deliver approximately 690 permanent jobs at a blended 
wage near $76,800/year, roughly $52.8 million in annual payroll, a safer State Highway 
93 roundabout, and a grade- separated pedestrian bridge serving the North Table 
Mountain Trailhead, which are all public benefits that persist regardless of future tenant 
mix. Staff supports authorizing tax increment financing and entering into the cooperation 
and revenue-sharing agreements described in the report, conditioned on the developer's 
firm commitment to close the remaining funding gap and deliver the committed public 
improvements.

Executive Summary 
The Junction Urban Renewal Plan Feasibility Report: 
This report evaluates the financial feasibility of The Junction, a proposed 499,000 square foot light 
industrial business park on a 51.28 acre parcel within the proposed Junction Urban Renewal Plan area in 
Golden, Colorado. The parcel was recently annexed into the City, sits on the west side of State Highway 
93 directly across from the North Table Mountain Park West Trailhead, and is bounded by the Brickyard 
Business Park to the south. Jefferson County Open Space sold the parcel through a competitive process in 
December 2025, and Evergreen Development Company (Evergreen) now leads the development. The City 
has identified the site as one of Golden's last large-scale employment opportunities. 
The parcel is vacant today because it is expensive to activate. The Junction Conditions Survey documented 
five statutory blighting factors, including a coal mine subsidence hazard covering roughly half of the 
parcel, expansive soils, steep topography, a si ngle unimproved highway access, and a complete absence 
of public infrastructure. Sanitary sewer requires a lift station because the site sits below the City's 
treatment plant. Xcel Energy has indicated it can serve only the first phase of the program, and full 
electrical capacity will require several million dollars of dry utility improvements. The City has also made 
a grade separated pedestrian and bicycle bridge across State Highway 93 a priority for trailhead safety. 
The Service Plan for The Junction Met ropolitan District estimates these public improvements at 
$59,050,236 in current dollars, before any vertical construction, tenant improvements, or soft costs. 
The public financing strategy pairs the proposed  Urban Renewal Plan with an approved  Metropolitan 
District. The Authority would pledge property tax increment to the District, and the District would 
combine that increment with its own debt service mill levy to issue tax exempt bonds that fund the eligible 
improvements. Piper Sandler prepared two financial plans at PDC's request: Plan N.3 with the Authority's 
tax increment and Plan N.4 without it. The comparison is the core of this report. Without the Plan, the 
District's net bond proceeds available for infrastructure total approximately $14.2 million, roughly 24% of 
the total public improvement cost. With a tax increment pledge equal to 81% of the overlapping mill levy, 
net proceeds rise to approximately $35.1 million, roughly 59% of the cost.  The developer has committed 
to finance the remaining $23.9 million privately, allowing the project to proceed as envisioned. 
This report finds that The Junction is not feasible without the Urban Renewal Plan and its tax increment 
financing, and that the project becomes feasible with them. The remaining private obligation with the 
Plan is significant but manageable for an experienced developer. The obligation without the Plan, nearly 
$44.9 million or about $90 per square foot of building area, cannot be recovered at rents the Golden and 
Jefferson County industrial market will support.

Key Findings 
1. Extraordinary infrastructure costs arrest development. The parcel requires $59.05 million in public 
improvements before any building can be occupied. That equals ~ $118 per square foot of programmed 
building area and about $1.15 million per acre, more than half of the program's estimated $113.5 million 
value at build out. Costs of this magnitude are the direct result of the blighting conditions catalogued in 
the Conditions Survey, and they explain why the parcel has remained vacant. 
2. Without the Plan, the project is not feasible. Relying on its own debt service mill levy alone, The 
Junction Metropolitan District can generate approximately $14.2 million in project funds across two bond 
issues. That leaves $44.9 million, or 76% of the infrastructure cost, to be financed privately. Carrying that 
cost would require rents roughly 45% above the top of the market's supportable range. No asset class at 
this location could absorb that burden. 
3. With the Plan, the project becomes feasible. A tax increment pledge of 73.318 mills, 81% of the current 
90.668 mill overlapping rate, increases the District's project funds to approximately $35.1 million. The 
remaining gap falls to $23.9 million, about $48 per square foot of building area, which Evergreen has 
committed to finance privately. This private share is consistent with the land and site basis that industrial 
developers routinely carry, and the developer's record of delivering more than $90 million of public 
improvements through similar partnerships supports its ability to close it. 
4. The pledge is proportionate and the fiscal impacts are low. The Impact Report found low level impacts 
on every taxing entity. The recommended revenue sharing agreements return the Jefferson County School 
District's Bond Redemption and Mill Levy Override levies to the District, an estimated $13.4 million over 
the Pl an's term, and pledge only the levies that the Plan's development would create on land that 
generates no property tax today. 
5. The completed project delivers lasting community and economic benefits. At build out the program 
supports roughly 690 permanent jobs at a blended wage of about $76,800 per year, approximately $52.8 
million in annual payroll, and an estimated $56,000 per year in City and County sales tax from workforce 
spending. The public improvements, including the roundabout and the pedestrian bridge, will serve 
residents, trail users, and the regional highway corridor regardless of who occupies the buildings. The Plan 
will achieve the GURA’ s goals for rem ediating blight, attracting private investment, s purring community 
and economic benefits, and providing new public spaces and amenities.

Scope of the Review 
To evaluate the proposed project, PDC reviewed and provided independent research regarding the 
following: 
• Review of the public improvement program and its estimated cost as documented in the Service 
Plan for The Junction Metropolitan District and updated by the development team. 
• Comparison of the Metropolitan District's bonding capacity with and without the Urban Renewal

• Projection of property tax increment at the revenue sharing agreements recommended in the

• Evaluation of the developer's capacity to finance the remaining funding gap privately. 
• Quantification of the community and economic benefits the completed project will deliver to the 
City of Golden and Jefferson County. 
 
Methodology 
A conventional gap funding analysis compares a project's return on investment against market 
benchmarks and calculates the incentive required to yield returns above market benchmarks . That 
approach requires a detailed development budget and operating pro forma. The Junction Plan’ s 
development program is still conceptual, only the fuel and convenience use is committed, and the tenant 
mix of the twelve remaining buildings will be set by the market over next few years. What is known is the 
cost of the public infrastructure the parcel requires and the revenue the project can generate to pay for 
it. 
This report therefore evaluates the project's public financing strategy rather than its vertical economics. 
Feasibility is defined as the ability to fund the public improvements that unlock the parcel. The analysis 
compares the estimated cost of those impr ovements against the net bond proceeds The Junction 
Metropolitan District can raise under two scenarios . The first  scenario is to  rely on the district’s debt 
service mill levy alone, and the second scenario is combining that levy with  the project’s property tax 
increment pledged by the Authority. The remaining difference in each scenario is the private funding gap. 
The developer's ability to carry that gap, tested against the market and the developer's record, determines 
whether the project can proceed.

Phasing and the Power Constraint 
The program is delivered in two phases, and the phasing is driven by infrastructure rather than by market

collections in 2032. The two year lag between construction and collection is why the Series 2027 bonds 
carry capitalized interest through 2030.

Market Analysis 
A feasibility finding requires two things: a market that can quickly absorb the product, and a cost structure 
the market can carry. This market analysis evaluates the first test, whether the market can support the 
proposed use a s forecast . PDC's Market Analysis, prepared in August 2026 from CoStar data and 
independent research, found that The Junction enters one of the strongest industrial submarkets in the 
Denver region. The full analysis is available as a supporting study to the Urban Re newal Plan; its findings 
are summarized here. 
Market Conditions 
The Denver metro is working through a supply correction, with vacancy near 9% concentrated in large 
speculative distribution buildings delivered since 2021. The West Denver submarket that contains Golden 
is the opposite case. Vacancy is 3.5%, the construct ion pipeline is barely 1% of inventory, developable 
industrial land is scarce, and demand from small and mid sized manufacturers, trades, and service 
businesses is durable. Jefferson County has absorbed roughly 237,000 square feet of industrial and flex 
space per year for 27 years while holding average vacancy below 5%.

Competitive Set and Rents 
The strongest indicator of what new development at The Junction can achieve is the established business 
park immediately south of the Plan area . 1260 Brickyard Road, a 26,320 square foot manufacturing 
building completed in 2020 in the adjacent Brickyard Business Park, signed a lease in January 2026 at

Program Fit 
The 499,000 square foot program averages roughly 100,000 square feet per year over its delivery window, 
about 40% of the county's long term absorption, an achievable capture rate for the only entitled business 
park in Golden. The Phase 2 small bay flex buildings match the tenant profile West Denver demand favors, 
and the existing 1980s stock cannot adequately serve. The two large Phase 1 buildings carry the program's 
concentration risk and should be delivered against anchor commitments or substantial preleasing. On 
those terms, the market supports the program, the Financial Plan's valuation assumptions, and the 
revenue foundation of the Urban Renewal Plan. 
What the Market Cannot Carry 
The market supports the product. It does not support the parcel's infrastructure burden. If the $44.9 
million of infrastructure left unfunded without the Urban Renewal Plan were financed privately at an 8% 
yield on cost, the annual carry would be roughly $3.6 million, or about $7.20 per square foot across the  
development program. If that expense were added to the current market of rents ranging between $13.50 
to $16.00, the project’s required rent would exceed $20.70 to $23.20 per square foot, roughly 45% to 53% 
above the market range and well above any rate achieved in Golden or the West Denver submarket. No 
tenant base in this market will pay that premium, and no lender will underwrite it.  
MARKET FINDING.  The Junction enters a submarket with 3.5% vacancy, an aging competitive stock, 
and a 27 year record of steady absorption. A light industrial business park at this location is 
warranted, provided the public infrastructure cost is reduced to a level the mark et can support. The 
Urban Renewal Plan is the mechanism that makes that reduction possible.

Reconciliation to the Financial Plan 
Piper Sandler's Financial Plan N.3 pledges the same 73.318 mills but sizes the bonds on a more 
conservative revenue stream. It applies the statutory 25% commercial assessment rate rather than the 
27% equalization ratio, discounts collections to 90%, and co unts pledged revenue only through the 2051 
collection year. On that basis the Financial Plan counts $45,925,265 of pledged increment against debt 
service, compared with $56.5 million in PDC's forecast. The bond sizing also treats the District's own 35 
mill debt service levy and specific ownership taxes as the primary pledge, with the TIF revenue as the 
addition that expands capacity. This report adopts the Financial Plan's conservative figures for all 
feasibility conclusions.

How the Partnership Works 
The Junction's public improvements will be built by the developer and financed by The Junction 
Metropolitan District, which was approved by the City of Golden in 2026. The District will issue tax exempt 
bonds and repay them from two revenue streams. The fi rst is the District's own debt service mill levy, 
modeled at 35 mills against a Service Plan cap of 50 mills, plus specific ownership taxes. The second is 
property tax increment generated within the Urban Renewal Plan area and pledged to the District by the 
Authority through a cooperation agreement. Because the parcel has a base value of $0, every dollar of 
increment is new revenue created by the development itself. 
The Authority's pledge does not redirect existing revenue from any taxing entity. It allocates a share of 
future taxes that would not exist without the Plan, for a defined period, to the infrastructure that creates 
them. When the Plan sunsets in 2051, the full assessed value of the business park, estimated at more than 
$36 million by that year, returns to the tax rolls of every entity at its full mill levy.
