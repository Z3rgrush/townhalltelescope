F. Extension/Update of Agreement for Administrative Services to the DDA by the City -   
From the inception of the DDA, the City has, by contract, provided administrative, financial, 
and executive director support to the DDA in a manner similar to how services are provided 
to GURA since 2015.  The current agreement dates from early 2023, and renews 
automatically each year, unless updated or terminated.   Under the current agreement, 
administrative, support and operational services are provided by the Economic Development 
Manager and Economic Development Specialist (Tim Dwyer and Abdullah Saquib). Financial 
and auditing services are provided by the City Finance Department.  There are other 
miscellaneous services provided by the City but not listed.   The Executive Director position 
has been held by Steve Glueck since the DDA establishment.   
 
The administrative fee charged to the DDA for the services has been fairly stable over the 
years and has been based on estimates of time devoted to the DDA by City staff as 
opposed to other job duties.  The current fee has been $74,000 annually, purposely below 
estimated costs due to the mutual benefits to City and DDA of our partner efforts. More 
recently, staff has done a more complete calculation and determined that the actual cost for 
the services provided is likely closer to $130,000 per year.  Based on the on-going 
partnership between DDA and City, staff is recommending that the administrative fee be 
increased only to $80,000 per year for the foreseeable time.  While it is conceivable that the 
DDA could contract with a third party for some of these services (not necessarily the finance 
support, due to state statute requirements) staff research shows that option to be quite a bit 
more expensive, and not as conducive to partner projects, programs, and activities as the 
current structure.   The DDA board may wish to note that while staff also recommended an 
$80,000 annual fee to the GURA board, on November 9th, they voted to increase the fee 
paid to the City to $100,000.  Staff believes that the partnership benefits to both parties 
warrant the lower amount, but that is a DDA board policy decision. 
 
The other proposed change to the agreement is the transition of the Executive Director 
position from Steve Glueck to Tim Dwyer, effective February 1st, 2027, with Steve Glueck 
taking on more of an advisory role to Tim and Abdullah.    
 
Staff requests that the DDA board consider an amended agreement as attached in January 
2027, which would need to also be approved by City Council as the current agreement was.  
The November discussion is intended to be an introduction and to answer board questions.   
 
      Board discussion is requested.

Re: Downtown Parking Management Program Update 
Purpose of Memorandum: 
The City hired Interstate Parking of Colorado to operate the parking management system 
including permits, enforcement, adjudication and communications.  Interstate began 
operations on May 1, 2024.  City staff regularly monitors the overall parking system and 
contractor performance as well as communication strategies and responses to community 
input.  City staff is requesting Downtown Development Authority feedback on the 
downtown managed parking program. 
Background: 
The City of Golden’s experience with active neighborhood and downtown parking 
management dates to the initial neighborhood permit parking (NPP) zones for the area 
near 12th Street north of Mines and the areas near Golden High School.  Over the years, 
these permit areas have expanded by neighborhood request and new areas have been 
added. The permit systems for these areas included resident and employee permits 
issued by the City and enforcement by administrative citation.  In addition, the more active 
management of the downtown area was instituted in 2017 in an effort to discourage Mines 
students, staff, and patrons from utilizing free downtown public parking to avoid campus 
parking fees.  The downtown system today includes time limits on streets, paid parking in 
surface lots and garages, free downtown employee permits, and paid downtown resident 
permits.  
The City solicited proposals from managed parking vendors in late 2023 and selected 
Interstate Parking of Colorado to operate the City’s paid and permit parking, violation 
adjudication and parking communications/marketing efforts.  Interstate assumed parking 
management duties on May 1, 2024.  The current contract includes revenue sharing: the 
City receives an initial $250,000 payment and 44 percent of subsequent revenues.  The 
City’s revenue sharing percentage has decreased from 50 percent as City Council has 
approved contract amendments for additional neighborhood permit parking areas.

Interstate submits monthly revenue and customer service experience reports.  These 
reports along with other data and public and City staff feedback are the basis for evaluating 
the managed parking program. 
Parking Utilization, Violation and Revenue Data Reporting 
City staff collected parking utilization rate data in 2022 and 2025 for downtown and the 
surrounding areas.  This data will also be collected in 2027 to provide long-term trend 
analysis.  A parking utilization rate is the amount of parking in use compared to the total 
number of parking spaces expressed as a percentage.  Since most on-street parking is not 
individually marked, we utilize the standard parking space dimension from the zoning code 
to determine the total number of spaces.  Data is collected on pre-summer (before May 1) 
and summer weekdays and weekends at multiple times from early morning to evening. 
The data is point-in-time but helps City staff understand overall parking demand trends. 
The summary 2022 and 2025 parking utilization rate graphics for downtown Golden and 
surrounding neighborhoods are in Attachment 1 and 2.  City staff note several trends in 
downtown Golden: 
 Both Parking Garages Usually Have Capacity.  On-street and surface lot
parking utilization rates are often higher, peaking during weekend
afternoons.  The two parking garages along Jackson Street often have
capacity during these times, though will become full during special events
and during peak visitation times.
 Free Clear Creek Lots Mainly Used on Summer Weekends.  The free
parking lots east of Ford Street provide additional capacity to serve
downtown Golden.  However, parking utilization rates are typically lower
except on summer weekend days when creek visitation increases.  City staff
did not collect data in 2026 when creek visitation spread into more
weekdays, but anecdotally saw parking utilization increase on weekdays.
 High Evening Parking Demand Mainly North of Clear Creek.  Parking
demand exceeds 75% in the evening hours and often peaks at 90-100%
along Water and Jackson Streets.  Golden Mill is the predominant business
in this area and remains busy during evening hours.  There are also a larger
number of residential units with some off-street paid parking options, leading
to regular parking demand across different land uses.  Proximity to Clear
Creek and downtown businesses likely also play a role along with parking
enforcement ending at 5pm.
City staff note several trends for the entire managed parking program based on Interstate’s 
monthly revenue reports.   
 Overall Compliance Rates are High. Interstate tracks the number of registered
and unregistered vehicles to develop a compliance rate. The compliance rate
has been maintained in low 90 percent in the monthly performance reports.
While the rate varies by area, day and time, the City and Interstate strive to
maximize compliance and behavior change.

 Violation Revenue Continues to be Higher Than Paid Parking Revenue.  
Violation revenue regularly exceeds paid parking revenue by a substantial 
amount.  While there is no target revenue split, the City and Interstate will 
continue to adjust communication strategies to maximize compliance and 
behavior change. 
 10th Street Remains the Main Violation ‘Hot Spot.’  While initial violation 
numbers were more split, 10th Street is currently responsible for the majority of 
violations.  This corridor includes 10th Street, the Farmer’s Market lot, Kayak lot 
(converted to ADA and drop-off only during the summer) and the Community 
Center lots. 
 Violation Revenue Largely Driven by Non-Golden Residents.  
Approximately 90% of violation revenue is paid by an address outside of the two 
‘Golden’ zip code, varying monthly between 84-89 percent.  While Interstate is 
able to track payment by postal zip codes, both ‘Golden’ zip codes extend far 
outside of city limits.  Violation revenue paid by City of Golden residents is likely 
far lower than these numbers. 
 
Previous Downtown Businesses Listening Session Feedback 
 
An intended benefit of a parking management system is to create regular turnover of 
parking spaces to ensure access to local businesses.  The downtown paid parking 
regulations were developed in 2017 in partnership with the business community to 
address Mines student parking impacts.  This partnership has evolved as downtown has 
grown, the number and popularity of special events has increased, and year-round 
visitation has become the norm. 
 
City staff held two listening sessions for downtown business owners in September 2024 
and February 2025.  An online survey was included concurrent with the February session.  
Approximately 10 and 3 business owners attended the September and February 
sessions, respectively.  An online survey released with the February session had 19 
responses, a 7 percent response rate.  A summary of business feedback is below: 
 
 Downtown Business and Employee Engagement. City staff heard concerns 
from the downtown business community that they were unaware of the new 
parking contractor. Economic Development had provided email contacts via the 
B2B system which covers most businesses. Interstate connected with 
businesses via email and in person visits to register owners for the employee 
permit system where they can directly manage permits rather than submit paper 
applications as done previously.  Interstate conducted a second round of 
business outreach in 2025 and included neighborhood permit zone businesses. 
Business community feedback, particularly for the direct-manage permit 
system, has improved in the past year. 
 
 Continue Regular City/Businesses Coordination.  Appreciate being part of 
the process, like having regular avenue to discuss concerns with City staff and 
Interstate.

Expansion of ‘Public Parking – Privately Managed’ Lots 
There are multiple privately owned and managed parking options around Downtown 
Golden.  Some lots are formally managed (e.g. Calvary Church garage, Golden Hotel 
garage) while others function as employee or building owner parking.  Interstate (outside 
of the City’s contract) manages several of these lots.  ParkGoldenCO.com acts as a single 
point of information for parking in Downtown Golden and the surrounding neighborhoods. 
City staff has added these ‘public parking – privately managed’ lots to the Map n’ Park 
webpage to ensure residents, businesses, employees and visitors are aware of all parking 
options.   
Clayworks began regulating their parking lots in early February 2025.  This includes the 
10th and Ford lot, behind the old mountaineering center and the Gold Office building (nights 
and weekends only).  Parking is managed outside of the City’s vendor and rates differ.

including acceptance of the Golden Resident permit.  There are no impacts to the

city parking signage has been added including A-frame signs to delineate the two halves 
of the parking lot.  City staff negotiated unsuccessfully to extend the previous agreement 
and remain open to a new agreement.

Re: GDGID Introduction and Update 
Purpose of Agenda Item: 
To brief City Council on the fiscal status of the Golden Downtown General Improvement 
District (GDGID). Council had requested such a review in 2025, and this additional 
discussion may aid new councilors. 
Background: 
The current boundaries of the Golden Downtown General Improvement District  (GDGID) 
are as depicted on the attached graphic and have been unchanged since property near 
11th and Arapahoe Streets was added in 1998. The GDGID was established in 
September 1963 by Ordinance 521. The district was organized pursuant to Chapter 89, 
Article 4, CRS 1953, as amended. This section has been reorganized in the current state 
statutes. Powers of the district are defined in current state statutes pertaining to General 
Improvement Districts. City Council sits as the Board of Directors of the district, and the 
Mayor is President of the Board.  
Ordinance 521 stated that the improvements which the district intended to construct 
included “parking and off-street parking facilities for the area within the district”.  Bonds 
were issued in 1965 to acquire the first lots purchased by the district. Public surface lots in 
downtown are a combination of city ownership and district ownership. In 2005, the district 
entered into an agreement with the City and GURA whereby, the district lots at 13th and 
Jackson Streets were conveyed (along with the City lots at 12th and Jackson Streets) to 
GURA for the parking structure and future development projects.  The parking structure at 
1250 Jackson Street and GURA’s partial ownership interest in the north parking garage 
were conveyed to the City at the conclusion of the GURA downtown project in 2015. All 
on-street parking in the downtown area occurs on City owned and controlled streets. 
Accordingly, the GDGID directly controls very little of the public parking within its 
boundaries but rather is used as a management entity for such off-street public parking. 
For much of its history since the 1980’s, the GDGID was a rather inactive entity, collecting 
its small property tax levy and making a very small maintenance payment to the City. With 
the enactment of a “cash in lieu of parking” option in 2004 (see GMC 18.52) it was 
SS Item No. 3
pkt pg. 21
decided that any cash in lieu contributions in the district boundaries would be deposited 
into the GDGID. For the period between 2004 and the transfer of the garage ownership 
interests to the City in 2015, such cash contributions were transferred to GURA to partially 
off set the investments in the two garages. With the transfer of ownership to the City, any 
contributions remained in the GDGID budget.  Two larger contributions associated with 
the buildings connected to the 1250 Jackson Street garage caused the GDGID fund 
balance to start to grow substantially in about 2015. With the advent of paid parking 
downtown in 2017, the fund balance began to grow even more, in large part since the 
GDGID maintenance contribution was artificially small, and the general fund bore the cost 
of maintenance.  
Budget Structure: 
Based upon the growing reserves and the start of the Interstate Parking contract for 
management of the parking program, virtually all parking associated revenues and 
expenses are now reflected in the GDGID budget, as described below . The only costs 
covered elsewhere relate to staff time to manage parking/Interstate, and pavement 
management for on- street parking spaces which could not easily be segregated from 
overall street paving. 
R
evenues:    The GDGID has only a few main revenue sources: 
• Pursuant to its establishment and state statutes, the GDGID has historically levied a
property tax of 2.352 mills on real property in the district. However, GDGID revenue
growth is limited both by the TABOR amendment to the state constitution and a
statutory limit of 5.5% revenue growth. As a result, every year in recent years, the
actual mill levy has been “temporarily reduced”. The actual mill levy for 2024 taxes,
payable in 2025, was 1.752 mills or about 2% of total property taxes for properties in
the district. The actual mill levy for 2025 taxes, payable in 2026, was 1.654 mills.
The projected amount for 2026 is $51,500, with slight increases in 2027 and 2028.
• The GDGID also collects a small amount of what is labeled “Automobile Tax”.  This
tax is more accurately called the “Specific Ownership Tax” and is collected by the
county annually with vehicle registrations. The GDGID share is a portion of the
amount collected for vehicles registered within district boundaries. The balance is
paid to other taxing entities.
• Prior to contracting with Interstate Parking, the GDGID collected all downtown pay
station revenues (starting in 2017) but none of the enforcement (violation) revenues
which went to the General Fund to cover some costs. Currently, the City receives a
monthly payment from Interstate Parking that covers our share of downtown paid
resident permits, pay station revenue, and violation revenue based on the
negotiated formula whereby the city receives an annual amount of $250,000 plus
46% of all revenues above that threshold. Staff calculates the portion of revenues
received attributable to violations and revenue, based on their “pro-rata” share of the
totals. The total parking management revenues for 2026 will likely be between
$550,000 and $600,000.
pkt pg. 22
• As a result of the healthy reserves, interest revenues have grown in recent years.
Expenses:  GDGID expenses include the following:
• Starting in mid-2024, all maintenance and regular repairs for the south garage, city
share of the north garage, and surface lots west of Washington are covered by the
repair and maintenance line item, at about $120,000 in 2026, with small anticipated
increases in 2027 and 2028.
• Professional services including design for certain electrical or other improvements,
and occasionally, a contribution to a joint data collection effort with the City regarding
parking counts.
• Non-capital project expenses which vary from year to year.
• Insurance premiums for the south garage only. Insurance for the north garage is
included in the Association fees paid under maintenance.
• Capital Projects including planned investments in the south garage and surface lots.
Staff has been working on a project to improve electric service to the City half of the
Arapahoe Street lot from 12th to 13th with Xcel for some time that may occur this
year.  Future capital projects are based upon planned repairs and when unexpected
repairs are needed.
• Because the parking management revenue is generated in an area much larger
than the GDGID boundaries, starting in 2024, there is an annual transfer to the
General Fund of revenues calculated to be from outside the GDGID district. By the
end of 2026, the amount held in the General Fund for future parking investment
outside of Downtown will be about $775,000.
Budget Status:  The GDGID budget is in effect a management tool to address current 
service and infrastructure needs and grow resources for future community needs.  As 
noted below, the GDGID is a partner with the City and perhaps the DDA, but not the lead 
agency.  One recent issue that may result in a budget amendment in 2027 relates to the 
request from the other ownership entity in the north garage for a fair share revenue sharing 
for revenues generated in the jointly owned garage.  The revenues from that garage have 
not been broken out in the past.  It is premature to suggest a budget amendment at this 
time, as the dollar amounts are not yet known. 
Recent Policy Discussion: 
Both the GDGID and the General Fund Parking Trust Account each have healthy 
reserves at the end of 2026 ($1.3 million for GDGID and $775,000 for General Fund). 
These reserves and the likelihood of continued revenues in excess of expenditures allow 
the GDGID and City to consider options, as discussed in 2025.  
• Continue to seek opportunities to increase parking supply in and near downtown.
Historically, the area where most parking supply concerns are raised has been the portion of
downtown west of Washington Avenue and south of Clear Creek. Supply for customers and
pkt pg. 23
employees is limited, and demand is high. At the same time there is little to no available property 
for acquisition and asking prices for property are extremely high. Similarly, the idea of 
constructing structured parking over existing public parking would provide only a modest net 
gain in spaces (in part due to the substandard width of existing parking bays) at an extremely 
high cost. The prior policy discussions led to the conclusion that the City should seek to manage 
parking supply and utilize the typical surpluses that occur in the two garages rather than seeking 
to increase supply.  Additional parking counts in 2025  and planned for 2027 may refocus this 
discussion. 
M
ore recently, there is an indication that demand for additional parking supply north of Clear 
Creek is growing significantly. Between seasonal creek corridor visitors, anticipated employees 
and visitors to the Clayworks project, and parking demand generated by local businesses and 
Heart of Golden improvements, this area may be an appropriate focus for increased managed 
public parking supply. There may be opportunities for stand- alone parking facilities, joint 
partnerships with area developments, and the incorporation of additional parking in Council 
plans for city properties east of Ford Street. Any such opportunities for increased parking supply 
would be expected to range into the millions of dollars, however, current available resources 
would be a start. 
• P
ursue Alternative Modes and Transportation Demand Management (TDM) as a
component, but not a stand-alone solution -   Many efforts to encourage alternate modes
of travel and TDM strategies to lessen vehicle miles traveled (VMT) and parking demand are
being spearheaded by the Community Development and Public Works Departments. These
efforts are helpful but are not likely to address parking demand. The City share of the
Mines/Golden shuttle is being funded from lodging tax, while other efforts could be funded by
the City, DDA, GDGID, and possibly grants.
• P
otential increase in GDGID district boundaries -     The 2025 discussion included the
question about expanding the GDGID boundaries.  Such expansion is relatively
straightforward, but must be requested by the affected landowners, and would not necessarily
assist in achieving solutions.
• C
ity lead in policy discussions  -   While the GDGID and DDA can be valuable funding
partners in any investment solution directed by Council, neither of those entities would be in a
position to lead such an effort.  Rather, the City should be the entity to provide policy direction
on the matter of parking in and near downtown.

taff recommends that City Council (who also sit as the GDGID board) , consider the above 
information as the 2027-2028 GDGID budget is finalized and presented in December.
