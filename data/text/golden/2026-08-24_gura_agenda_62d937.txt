1. Review and Discussion of The Junction Urban Renewal Plan Area Reports
The Junction Plan Area was formally introduced to the Board as a discussion item at the
March 9, 2026 meeting, at which point staff presented background on the proposed
development and the steps needed to advance the plan area formation process.  Since
then, GURA has engaged with consulting firm Pioneer Development Company (Pioneer),
following their selection through a competitive RFP for consulting services related to the
proposed Junction Plan Area in early June.  Pioneer has completed their work and
produced the three reports required for the board to consider the adoption of The Junction
as a new target urban renewal Plan Area.   These three reports are described below and
are attached for reference. The first step in the establishment of a new Plan Area is to
assess the site area to see if the state statute defined conditions of blight are present.
The Junction – Conditions Survey – Presented to GURA on August 10, 2026.
The Junction Conditions Survey is the threshold document required under Colorado's
Urban Renewal Law (C.R.S. 31-25-103.2) before any Urban Renewal Plan area can be
established. Its purpose is to determine whether the proposed 51.28-acre parcel exhibits
the statutorily required number of  "blighting factors" conditions that impair sound
development, retard housing, or threaten public health, safety, or welfare. Colorado law
2
requires that at least four of eleven possible factors are found. This survey identified five 
factors: defective/inadequate street layout (b), unsafe/unsanitary conditions (d), unusual 
topography or inadequate infrastructure (f), conditions endangering life or property (h), 
and underutilization/vacancy (k.5). Key findings for the Board include the site's single 
unimproved highway access point, a mining subsidence hazard covering roughly 25 
acres (later refined to ~50% of the parcel in the draft Plan), expansive soils, complete 
absence of public infrastructure, and an estimated $59 million in required public 
improvements, collectively supporting eligibility for urban renewal treatment.  
The Junction – Impact Report 
The Junction Urban Renewal Plan Impact Report is the supplementary financial analysis 
required under C.R.S. 31-25-107(3.5)(I-V) and (9.5) whenever a plan intends to use Tax 
Increment Financing (TIF). Its purpose is to forecast the fiscal impact of diverting 
incremental property and sales tax revenue away from overlapping taxing entities (City, 
County, School District, and special districts) for the 25-year maximum TIF period, so 
GURA can negotiate informed revenue-sharing agreements. The Board should note this 
report models a "maximum impact scenario" assuming 100% mill levy pledges from all 
entities. Key figures include a forecasted $30.65 million taxable value at buildout, $69.9 
million in property tax increment and $2.2 million in sales tax increment over 25 years 
(present value ~$28.9 million), and roughly 690 permanent jobs with no new residents or 
students (since development is entirely commercial). All impacts were assessed as "low" 
(under the 10% significance threshold) for every taxing entity, and the report explicitly 
recommends 100% revenue-sharing with the City, County, and drainage districts, but 
only the School Finance Act levy (not Bond or MLO) with the School District. 
The Junction – Draft Urban Renewal Plan 
The Junction Urban Renewal Draft Plan is the formal governing document that, if adopted 
by City Council, actually establishes the Urban Renewal Area and authorizes GURA to 
undertake renewal activities and TIF financing.  The Conditions Survey and Impact 
Report serve as its factual and financial foundation. The Board should focus on three 
elements: first, the Plan incorporates the Conditions Survey's five blighting factors by 
reference as the statutory basis for a Council blight determination; second, it defines the 
scope of authorized activities (site preparation, grading/subsidence mitigation, a Highway 
93 roundabout, a grade-separated pedestrian bridge to North Table Mountain, internal 
streets, and utilities) without committing to specific designs, funding amounts, or 
approvals,  those remain subject to separate review; and third, it establishes the 25-year 
TIF mechanism under C.R.S. 31-25-107, specifying that participating taxing entities (City, 
County, Jefferson County School District R -1, and other levying districts) will need 
individually negotiated cooperation agreements. The Board should also note that this 
draft ties the project explicitly to the City's 2026 Comprehensive Plan goals (economic 
development, multimodal access, resilience) to strengthen the public-purpose justification 
for adoption.  The board should also note, this is a draft plan that is subject to change 
based on upcoming negotiations with taxing entities, input or direction from City Council 
3
or the Planning Commission or changes or updates to the development team’s plans. 
The draft plan will be finalized and be adopted by the GURA board, City Council and the 
Planning Commission once all details of the project and plan area are finalized.

Executive Summary 
The Junction Conditions Survey: 
The Golden Urban Renewal Authority (GURA) is evaluating a specific area for potential Urban Renewal treatment. 
This area is tentatively named “The Junction Urban Renewal Plan” (the Plan). The Plan encompasses one 51.28 acre 
parcel of vacant land located along the west side of State Highway 93, directly across from the North Table Mountain 
Park West Trailhead. The parcel was recently annexed into the City of Golden and is bounded by Highway 93 to its 
east, an existing light industrial business park to its south, and vacant land, a single family residence, and an active 
clay mine to its west. GURA and the City elected to study this area due to its development potential, its unique 
physical constraints, and the significant public infrastructure investments required to serve the site. 
GURA and the City have long identified economic development and attainable housing as local priorities. The 
Junction presents a unique opportunity to advance both goals. Jefferson County Open Space sold the parcel to a 
private entity in December 2025 through a competitive RFQ process. Evergreen Development Company proposes to 
develop the site as a light industrial business park containing 13 buildings and approximately 499,000 square feet of 
commercial, office, and retail space. At build out, the development is projected to support a daytime population of 
approximately 1,500 people. The jobs created within this business park are expected to produce higher wages, 
helping local workers afford to live within Golden’s municipal limits. The City has also zoned a portion of the parcel 
for higher density multi family residential uses, although no apartments are proposed at this time. 
This report evaluates existing physical and environmental conditions that may be arresting sound development on 
the site. Field work and a literature review identified steep topography, a mining subsidence hazard covering 
approximately 25 acres, expansive soils with high swell potentials, a defective and unsafe street layout along State 
Highway 93, and a complete absence of public infrastructure as key barriers to development. The entire parcel 
remains vacant and generates no property tax revenue. These conditions collectively arrest sound development and 
support the inclusion of this parcel for Urban Renewal consideration. 
While the property faces physical and environmental challenges, its development potential aligns directly with 
Golden’s community goals. The Plan supports the City’s efforts to attract new businesses, create meaningful 
employment, and improve traffic safety along State Highway 93. Planned public improvements include a new 
roundabout and pedestrian bridge that will serve both the subject parcel and the North Table Mountain Park West 
Trailhead. Establishing an Urban Renewal Plan for The Junction would help mi tigate existing deficiencies, fund 
required public infrastructure, and enable a project that advances community and public interests. 
Before an Urban Renewal Plan can be established, a specific geographic area within the community must be found 
to exhibit certain conditions. These conditions are known as “blighting factors” , and they must be documented 
before Urban Renewal Plan areas can be formed and urban renewal projects commence. This process is known as a 
Conditions Survey, a report that carefully analyzes a geographic area to determine the existence of conditions 
defined by Colorado’s Urban Renewal Law. In general, these are conditions that “substantially impair or arrest the 
sound growth of the municipality, retard the provision of housing accommodations, or constitute an economic or 
social liability, and are a menace to the public health, safety, morals, or welfare” (C.R.S. 31-25-103.2). 
A Conditions Survey does not create a new Urban Renewal Plan or Urban Renewal Project. The Conditions Survey is 
the first step in that process, one that is required before the City of Golden can establish the The Junction Urban 
Renewal Plan. The purpose of this study is to evaluate the proposed Plan area and determine whether it meets the 
statutorily required threshold of blighted area factors. Four (4) of these factors must be identified before an Urban 
Renewal Plan can be formally established and adopted.  
8
The proposed Urban Renewal Plan area was surveyed using a comprehensive and holistic approach. Observations 
were conducted during a field visit on July 14, 2026 to identify the presence of statutorily defined blighting factors 
throughout the area. These field observations were supplemented with a literature review of pre development 
planning, engineering, and geotechnical documents. Data from the Jefferson County Assessor, FEMA, EPA, and local 
market databases were also utilized to evaluate conditions not readily observable during field work. 
This report also reviewed City and County planning documents, engineering and geotechnical studies, GIS datasets, 
and County property records. This information was synthesized to determine which statutorily defined conditions 
are present within the study boundary. These conditions were geo -located and mapped to provide GURA and the 
City of Golden with a clear understanding of the property’s urban renewal eligibility. The Conditions Survey 
concludes that the proposed The Junction Urban Renewal Plan  area exhibits the necessary number of blighting 
factors to qualify as an Urban Renewal Plan under Colorado state statute.

Evaluation of Blighting Factors 
Defining Blighting Conditions 
Colorado Revised Statutes 31-25-103 states that for an Urban Renewal Plan area to be established, there 
must exist certain conditions known as “blighted area factors” (Blighting Factors). State statute defines 
eleven separate factors for blight. The law indicates that if four or more of these factors are found in an 
area of the municipality, that area may be declared blighted and qualify for urban renewal treatment1. 
Blighted Area Factors Defined: 
 
a) Slum, deteriorated, or deteriorating structures 
b) Predominance of defective or inadequate street layout 
c) Faulty lot layout in relation to size, adequacy, accessibility, or usefulness 
d) Unsanitary or unsafe conditions 
e) Deterioration of site or other improvements 
f) Unusual topography or inadequate public improvements or utilities 
g) Defective or unusual conditions of title rendering the title nonmarketable; 
h) The existence of conditions that endanger life or property by fire or other causes 
i) Buildings that are unsafe or unhealthy for persons to live or work in because of building code 
violations, dilapidation, deterioration, defective design, physical construction, or faulty or 
inadequate facilities 
j) Environmental contamination of buildings or property 
k) The existence of health, safety, or welfare factors requiring high levels of municipal services or 
substantial physical underutilization or vacancy of sites, buildings, or other improvements   
 
These eleven factors define the “conditions” that are arresting the municipality’s sound development. Per 
statute, these factors give “reason” to declare an area “blighted”, provided that a specific number of 
factors are identified. The presence of at least four of these conditions satisfies the state statute defining 
an area blighted. 
This Conditions Survey will unpack each blighting factor to describe various real world “conditions” that 
indicate the presence of said factor. Only one factor need be identified for a municipality to declare the 
need to establish an urban renewal authorit y. An Urban Renewal Plan or Project requires at least four 
factors identified within its boundaries. It is the culmination of four or more blighting factors which 
indicate that renewal activities should be applied within the proposed project area. 
Blighting Factors were identified based on objective analysis and urban renewal best practices. The 
following sections outline the blighting factors found within the survey area. Blighting factors that were 
not identified are excluded from this report. 
1 In addition to this list, C.R.S. 31-25-103(2) lists a twelfth condition. This final condition only applies when there is unanimous 
agreement among affected property owners that their properties can be included in an Urban Renewal Area. In this occurrence, only 
one blighting factor from the list of eleven needs to be identified to declare the area blighte d.

Factor (b) – Predominance of Defective or Inadequate 
Street Layout 
Description: 
This factor refers to surface transportation conditions throughout the survey area. Surface transportation 
conditions may include road surface quality, the location or existence of streets and sidewalks, multi -
modal improvements, traffic safety infrastructure, ingress/egress locations, and emergency access 
considerations. If surface transportation conditions exist that negatively impact sound development, 
redevelopment, or threaten safety, the survey includes this factor in its catalogue. Examples of this factor 
include: 
• Inadequate street widths 
• Lack of streets, dead ends 
• Overall faulty layouts that impede vehicular access and internal circulation 
• Inadequate Traffic Safety facilities 
• Streets that exhibit high degrees of traffic or accidents 
• Streets that are in need of repair or reconstruction 
• Poor emergency access or active transportation 
Findings 
Field observations along State Highway 93 show clear evidence of defective and inadequate street layout. The parcel 
is served by a single ingress and egress point, an unimproved dirt road that connects directly to Highway 93. This 
entrance has no intersection, no deceleration lane, and no acceleration lane. Vehicles entering or leaving the site 
must slow or merge within active highway travel lanes. This entrance is a traffic safety risk today, and it is not 
adequate to facilitate future development within the parcel. 
The parcel also contains no internal street network. All 51.28 acres consist of vacant land without a single improved 
street, sidewalk, or emergency access route. Development cannot occur without an entirely new internal road 
network, and emergency vehicle s currently have no means of reaching the interior of the site. These conditions 
impede vehicular access and internal circulation throughout the survey area. 
The street layout also creates safety problems for the North Table Mountain Park West Trailhead, located directly 
across Highway 93 from the parcel. Vehicles travelling southbound have difficulty turning into the recreation area. 
Vehicles leaving the recreation area cannot turn left to return to the City of Golden. During the July 14 th field visit, 
Pioneer Development Company representatives observed multiple drivers attempt this illegal left turn, a sign that 
the road network is unsafe and inadequate as currently constructed. 
Active transportation facilities are also missing. Highway 93 lacks adequate shoulders in this area, and cyclists and 
pedestrians arriving from the north have a difficult and often dangerous time crossing the highway into the North 
Table Mountain area. There are no sidewalks, multi use paths, or safe crossings serving the parcel or the trailhead. 
Overall, the single unimproved access point, the missing internal street network, the unsafe turning movements at 
the North Table Mountain trailhead, and the lack of active transportation facilities demonstrate a defective and 
inadequate street layout. These conditions meet the definition of Blight Factor (b).

Findings 
Field work and a literature review identified multiple unsafe conditions throughout the survey area. The former Rocky 
Mountain No. 2 Coal Mine underlies a significant portion of the parcel, creating a subsidence hazard across

Hazardous soil conditions extend across the balance of the site. A geotechnical study performed by Ninyo and Moore

The parcel’s steep topography compounds these risks. Steep slopes and drop offs from existing grade complicate 
emergency access and will require significant grading and retaining walls before the site can be safely developed. 
The combination of mining subsidence hazards, expansive soils, dangerous traffic and pedestrian conditions, and 
steep topography confirms the presence of unsafe conditions within the survey area. These findings satisfy the 
statutory definition of Blight Factor (d).

Factor (f) – Unusual Topography or Inadequate Public 
Improvements or Utilities 
Description: 
This factor refers to unusual topography or lack of public infrastructure that have the effect of arresting 
sound development in a study area. Areas that exhibit steep grades which cause development to be 
incompatible or unprofitable would fall under this factor. Properties that are lacking public infrastructure, 
or are served by deteriorating public infrastructure, would also fall under this factor. This factor shares 
aspects of factors two (2), four (4) and five (5). Conditions that justify the inclusion of this factor in an area 
may include: 
 
•  Steep slopes or unusual terrain 
• Overhead utilities in need of repair 
• Deteriorating parking lots, street surfaces, sidewalks  
• Poor storm water drainage facilities 
• Lack of central sewer or water 
• Lack of internal street network 
• Broken or inadequate street lighting 
 
Findings 
The parcel’s topography was the most obvious reason for inclusion of this factor. Steep slopes rise from Highway 93 
until they crest as a ridgeline immediately west of the parcel. These grades include steep drop offs from existing grade 
along the parcel’s frontage. Development of the site will require substantial grading, retaining walls, and stormwater 
infrastructure before any project is feasible. 
The parcel also lacks public infrastructure entirely. The site contains no internal streets, no water or sanitary sewer 
facilities, no stormwater detention, and no parks or landscaping improvements. Every category of public 
infrastructure must be constructed before development can occur. 
The cost of these improvements underscores this factor. Required public improvements include an internal street 
network, water and sewer facilities, stormwater detention facilities, grading and retaining walls, and parks, open 
space, and landscaping improvements. The total cost of these improvements exceeds $59 million in today’s dollars. 
Infrastructure costs of this magnitude arrest sound development, and without coordinated public and private 
investment the site is likely to remain vacant and unproductive. 
The combination of steep topography, the complete absence of public improvements, and the extraordinary cost to 
correct these deficiencies confirms the presence of unusual topography and inadequate public improvements or 
utilities within the survey area, satisfying Blight Factor (f).

Factor (h) – The Existence of Conditions that Endanger

Description: 
This factor refers to conditions that can endanger lives or property, including buildings that are structurally 
unsafe, but it also focuses on natural hazards. This factor shares similarities with factors two (2), four (4) 
five (5) and eleven (11). Conditions that would justify the inclusion of this factor in an area may include: 
 
• Buildings or property not in compliance with current fire codes 
• Buildings or property not in compliance with building codes 
• Areas that are in a floodplain or flood prone area 
• Areas near burn scares, in debris fans or prone to dangerous mud flow 
• Areas that exhibit a high crime rate 
• Areas with buildings or land that violates environmental regulations (this can range from 
findings of asbestos or brownfield sites.) 
 
Findings 
The former Rocky Mountain No. 2 Coal Mine creates conditions that endanger life and property within the survey 
area. The mine’s historic workings underlie approximately 25 acres of the parcel, creating a subsidence hazard zone. 
Ground subsidence can cause sudden or gradual settlement that damages structures, utilities, and roadways, 
endangering both property and the people who occupy it. Any development within this zone will require careful 
engineering and mitigation. 
Soil expansion hazards are present throughout the parcel. The 2022 geotechnical study performed by Ninyo and 
Moore found large swell potentials in the site’s soils, representing a high risk to future development. Expansive soils 
can heave and crack foundat ions, utilities, and pavements over time, endangering the property constructed upon 
them. 
These hazards are compounded by the parcel’s dangerous access conditions. The single unimproved entrance off 
Highway 93 limits emergency response to the site, and the parcel’s steep topography further restricts emergency

Impact Report Methodology 
Forecasting Future Growth 
The Junction Urban Renewal Plan’s goal is to incentivize employment generating development by remediating 
blighting conditions and spurring new investments in public infrastructure. The Plan will support the partnership 
between Evergreen Development Company, The Junction Metropolitan District, the City of Golden and GURA. 
Urban renewal authorities can use a variety of tools to realize these changes, the most powerful being Tax Increment 
Financing (TIF). TIF is a redevelopment strategy that leverages future tax revenues to incentivize urban renewal 
activities. Colorado’s Urban Renewal Law mandates that urban renewal projects that intend to use TIF for renewal 
activities submit a supplementary Impact Report that forecasts growth within a proposed renewal area and 
estimates the impact this growth will have on taxing entities within that area. The report’s purpose is to ensure that 
taxing entities that rely on property tax and, as applicable, sales/lodging tax revenues, will not be adversely impacted 
by the urban renewal project. 
Quantifying these impacts requires a forecast of probable growth throughout the project area over the project’s 
lifetime. The Junction Urban Renewal Plan is expected to last 25 years, the maximum amount of time permitted by 
Colorado’s Urban Renewal Law for the collection of TIF. 
This report forecasts the Plan’s future development by reviewing the Metropolitan District Service Plan and Financial 
Plan, the proposed development program, regional development patterns, and current market conditions. PDC also 
performed an industrial market analysis to inform its development program assumptions. That analysis evaluated 
the Jefferson County and West Denver light industrial markets, the competitive set adjacent to the site, and the 
county’s 27-year absorption and delivery record. The absorption rates and estimated values used in this report are 
derived using an income based approach. This approach required evaluating comparable properties, current 
assessments of light industrial space throughout Golden and Jefferson County, the average triple net rents this 
development could command, and its likely capitalization rates, operating margins, and vacancy rates. These findings 
informed the inputs in PDC’s Tax Revenue Forecast Model. 
The proposed development represents a significant employment opportunity for the City of Golden. The program 
includes 499,000 square feet of light industrial and flex space across thirteen buildings, delivered in two phases 
between 2028 and 2030. The marke t analysis found that the site sits in one of the metro area’s most supply 
constrained industrial submarkets, with West Denver su bmarket vacancy of 3.5% and a construction pipeline of 
roughly 1% of inventory. Jefferson County has absorbed roughly 237,000 square feet of industrial and flex space per 
year for 27 years. The program’s phasing fits comfortably within that record. 
The Authority is expected to be a key partner in this project, and TIF is vital for funding the public infrastructure that 
makes the overall development feasible. The projections made by this impact report have been informed by this 
perspective and market realities.

existing and new improvements within the plan area. Tax revenue generated by new improvements is incremental

Taxing Entity Impacts 
Assessing the Plan’s impact on partnering taxing entities requires careful consideration of the revenue, services and 
infrastructure required to achieve that Plan’s goals. The Plan’s purpose must also be considered relative to these 
potential impacts. In addition to remediating blighting conditions, an urban renewal plan is designed to bring about 
public good, either through the remediation of blighting factors, attracting new investment to an underutilized 
community area, the procurement of public amenities or infrastructure, or community-minded development, such 
as affordable housing.  
The Junction Plan is intended to remediate blighting conditions, spur new investments, and catalyze a major 
employment development. New employment space has been a key economic development priority for the Golden 
community, and this development represents one of the city’s largest remaining opportunities to attract it. The 
development would generate approximately 499,000 square feet of light industrial and flex space supporting roughly 
690 permanent jobs at build out. This coordinated effort would also deliver major public improvements, including a 
roundabout on State Highway 93 and a grade separated pedestrian crossing serving the North Table Mountain Park 
West Trailhead. 
Ensuring development feasibility, however, requires overcoming more than $59 million in public infrastructure costs, 
a subsidence hazard, and expansive soils. Interviews with the Development Team have confirmed that Tax Increment 
Financing is a vital part of the project’s capital stack, and without GURA’s assistance, the project is not feasible. To 
access tax increment financing, GURA has elected to form a new Urban Renewal Plan area around this proposed 
development. The development area was found to exhibit the requisite number of blighting factors (2026 Conditions 
Survey) to be eligible for Urban Renewal treatment. 
This partnership between the City of Golden, the Authority, its participating taxing entities, and the development 
team, needs to be weighed against the possible fiscal impacts caused by the Urban Renewal Plan on partnering 
taxing entities. Colorado’s Urban Renewal Law is clear in C.R.S. 31-25-107(3.5)I-V), when it lists the impacts an Urban 
Renewal Impact Report must evaluate on effected taxing entities: 
• An estimate on the county revenue impact, and on the cost and extent of additional county infrastructure and 
services required to serve development within the proposed urban renewal area, and the benefit of 
improvements within the urban renewal area to existing county infrastructure,  
• A statement setting forth the method under which the authority or the municipality will finance, or that 
agreements are in place to finance, any additional County infrastructure and services required to serve 
development in the urban renewal area for the period in which all or any portion of the property taxes 
described in subparagraph (II) of paragraph (a) of subsection (9) of this section and levied by a county are paid 
to the authority,  
• Any other estimated impacts of the urban renewal project on county services and revenues.

impacts to the Jefferson County School District, the City of Golden, and the Urban Drainage and Flood Control

is therefore not impacted by the Plan’s tax increment financing.

Conclusion and Recommendations 
The Junction Urban Renewal Plan area is eligible to be adopted as an urban renewal plan based on the findings 
catalogued by the 2026 The Junction Conditions Survey. This Plan is expected to leverage tax increment financing to 
overcome identified blighting conditions and facilitate new development and public infrastructure within the Plan 
area, including a roundabout on State Highway 93 and a grade separated pedestrian crossing serving the North Table 
Mountain Park West Trailhead. The Plan’s intention to utilize tax increment financing necessitated this Urban 
Renewal Impact Report as a supplementary document per Colorado’s Urban Renewal Law. The report forecast future 
tax revenues over 25 years and evaluated the Plan’s potential impacts on all taxing entities within its proposed 
boundaries. 
This report finds that the Plan will generate low level impacts on the City of Golden, Jefferson County, the 
Jefferson County School District, and the Urban Drainage and Flood Control Districts.  The Plan is forecast to 
generate $69.9 million in property tax increment and $2.2 million in sales tax increment over 25 years, with a 
combined present value of approximately $28.9 million. Because the development program is entirely commercial, 
the Plan generates no new residents or students. The program instead delivers roughly 690 permanent jobs, 
approximately $52.8 million in annual payroll, and an estimated $40,000 per year in City and $16,000 per year in 
County sales tax through workforce retail spending outside the Plan’s boundaries. These employment and sales tax 
benefits are separate from, and in addition to, the tax increment forecast by this report. This report recommends 
that the Authority negotiate TIF sharing agreements with its taxing entity partners to ensure impacts are properly 
mitigated. These TIF sharing agreements should be balanced against the project’s financial needs and community 
benefits. 
This report recommends the following: 
• The Authority and the City of Golden agree to share 100% of the project’s incremental property tax with 
the Plan. 
• The Authority and the Jefferson County School District agree to share 100% of the total program (School 
Finance Act) property tax levy with the Plan but share back the Bond and Mill Levy Override levies to the 
Jefferson County School District. 
• The Authority and Jefferson County agree to share 100% of the project’s incremental property tax with 
the Plan. 
• The Authority and the Urban Drainage and Flood Control District agree to share 100% of the project’s 
incremental property tax revenues with the Plan. 
• The Authority and the Urban Drainage and Flood Control District South Platte agree to share 100% of the 
project’s incremental property tax revenues with the Plan. 
This report only recommends TIF sharing agreements that will provide the Authority with the funds it needs to 
financially support the Urban Renewal Plan. Proper TIF sharing agreements will ensure this development remediates 
blight, attracts investment and delivers the employment space and public infrastructure that achieve community 
and economic development outcomes, without adversely impacting the Authority’s partners.
