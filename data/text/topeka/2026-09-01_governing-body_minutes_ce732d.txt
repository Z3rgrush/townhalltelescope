 DISCUSSION regarding utility rates for 2027 through 2030, amending Topeka Municipal 
Code Sections 13.05.20 through 13.05.060 and repealing original sections, was presented.  
 Dr. Robert M. Perez, City Manager, reported since June 2, 2026, the Governing Body and 
Staff have had the opportunity to receive multiple points of feedback. Staff initially recommended 
the proposed utility fee schedule which removes the base charge and brings commercial industrial 
rates more in line with the per-gallon charges assessed to residential customers. However, since 
that time Saff has received additional feedback from the Governing Body to include in the rate 
structure a delayed ramp up period for commercial industrial users. He requested the Governing 
Body provide input to reach a final agreement.  
09-01-26 
359 
 
Sylvia Davis, Utilities Director, provided an overview of the Current Proposal and 
Alternate Consideration for the 2027 to 2030 City of Topeka utility rates. Proposals were 
highlighted as follows:  
        Current Proposal 
• Elimination of customer classes and implementing a standard consumption rate over the 
next four years 
o Eliminates Multifamily and Industrial II classifications 
o Stair-step increases for Commercial and Industrial  
• Reduces increased rate impacts to residential customers 
• Rate impact is absorbed by commercial and industrial classifications 
• No increase to < 2” base charge, increase volume charges 
• Increase to Wholesale rate 
• Increase to Irrigation rate 
• Maintains current Outside City rate multiplier  
 
        Alternate Consideration:  
• Creation of two standard volume rates, one for residential customers and one for 
commercial/industrial, phased in over the next four years.  
• Increased rate impacts to residential customers in 2027, but minimal in 2028, 2029, and 
2030. 
• Reduced rate impact to commercial and industrial classifications.  
• Includes increases to all base charges, and increases to volume charges 
• Increase to Wholesale rate 
• Increase to Irrigation rate 
• Maintains current Outside City rate multiplier  
• Eliminates current practice of assessing base charges to inactive properties (formerly 
known as “readiness to serve”)

 DISCUSSION relating to the City of Topeka's legislative agenda for the 2027 legislative 
session, was presented. (Policy and Finance Committee recommended approval on August 12, 
2026) 
 Dr. Robert M. Perez, City Manager, reported Staff brought forward the drafted legislative 
agenda to the Policy and Finance Committee on August 12, 2026. During the Committee meeting 
he emphasized the addition to focus on supporting legislation for funding to support the completion 
of the City’s Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) floodplain map and flood 
mitigation projects. The Legislative Agenda will be presented for approval on October 6, 2026, 
with the plan to begin conversations with Shawnee County Delegation prior to December.  
 Cale Beam, City Lobbyist, reported that the Policy and Finance Committee discussed 
proposals to be considered for the City's 2027 Legislative Agenda at its August 12, 2026, 
committee meeting and recommended the Governing Body’s consideration for the following 
priorities. 
• Transportation: reservation of future funding allocations for critical transportation and 
infrastructure projects 
• City-County Highway Fund: To address declining motor fuel tax revenues and rising 
street maintenance costs by exploring ways to increase revenue or identify alternative 
funding sources. 
• Flood Maps: Seeking state and federal funding for flood map updates and municipal 
flood mitigation projects. 
• Protection of Municipal Rights-of-Way: Oppose legislation that seeks to erode 
municipal authority of public rights-of-way regarding franchise utilities and 
improvement projects 
• Limitation on KHRC Building Permit Fee Waivers: Support legislation to limit the 
KHRC’s authority to award points in its qualified allocation plan for municipal building 
permit fee waivers 
• Combat Human Trafficking: Support continued state-level efforts to combat human 
trafficking and child sexual abuse; and legislation that strengthens policies that enhance 
prevention and prosecution 
 
09-01-26 
364 
 
 Lobbyist Beam provided an overview of the 4 potential additions to the Legislative Agenda 
recommended by Councilmember Hiller which included:  
1. Zeroing out of Value of Structures: Structures that have remained vacant and have 
been appraised at $0 and are not accruing property taxes 
2. Vegetation Ordinance Adjustment: Currently the State of Kansas requires 
municipalities to cite overgrown vegetation, abate the property and then charge.  
3. New Tenant Notification of Open Code Violations: Landlords would be required to 
provide notice to tenants if they have any open code violation prior to signing a lease. 
4. State override on signs in public right-of-way: Changing state law that prohibits 
municipalities from prohibiting political signage in the public right-of-way at least 45 
days prior to an election.

Data Centers: Vertical vs. Horizontal
How many are multi-story? Do they all need huge acreage? Or can you go vertical?
Prepared September 1, 2026 · United States focus, with international comparison
They do not all have to occupy huge acreage, and they can go vertical — but in the United States they usually do not.
There is a clear pattern why.
The dominant U.S. pattern is low and wide, not tall. Most purpose-built facilities — especially hyperscale campuses
for Amazon, Google, Microsoft, and Meta — are one story of servers, often with a mechanical “penthouse” on the roof
for cooling. Two- and three-story buildings exist and are becoming more common in a handful of tight markets. Four
stories and up remain unusual in the U.S. True high-rises are mostly overseas, or conversions of older urban buildings.
Typical U.S. hyperscale pattern: long, low halls; generator and transformer yards beside the building; cooling equipment covering the roof.
Why they stay short
Several engineering constraints stack against stacking floors. When land is cheap, a warehouse-style box is still faster
and cheaper per megawatt.
Weight. Server racks routinely need floor loading around 300–400+ pounds per square foot. A slab on grade
handles that cheaply. Every extra floor needs a much heavier structure.
Cooling. Heat wants to go up. A one-story hall dumps hot air through the roof. Add floors and you fight gravity,
steal roof area for more chillers, and run more pipe and duct.
Power and cabling. Vertical risers add cost, length, and failure points. Ground-level generator yards and
substations are simpler.
Moving equipment. A 2,000–5,000 lb rack is a forklift job on one floor and a crane or elevator problem on three.
Speed and cost. Single-story construction is faster to deliver when acreage is available. Industry models that
compared one- through four-story designs keep landing on the same conclusion: two and three stories are the
economic sweet spot when land is expensive. Gains flatten after that.

Where they do go vertical
Vertical construction appears where land, not structure, is the scarce resource:
 Northern Virginia (Ashburn / Loudoun) and Santa Clara have been adding two- and three-story buildings for
years. CoreSite and others have gone four or five stories in those markets.
 Meta started stacking two floors of server halls on existing campuses (for example, Prineville, Oregon) to squeeze
more capacity onto land already owned.
 Dense Asia and Europe go much higher: an 11-story Meta campus in Singapore, a 30-story facility in Hong Kong,
Equinix buildings of 8–12 stories in Amsterdam.
 New York has a well-known conversion: Sabey’s 375 Pearl Street, a 32-story former Verizon building with data
halls on selected floors — not a purpose-built server high-rise from the ground up.
In cities, two- and three-story is already the norm. In farm country with cheap land and a fat power line, operators still
sprawl.
Campus layout in a metro-adjacent market: multiple halls, rooftop cooling arrays, and substantial yard space for power and circulation — still
mostly low-rise.
Acreage versus the building
The building is only a slice of the site. A 200,000–600,000 square-foot hall might sit on 5–15 acres of footprint, but the
campus is often 50–200+ acres — and 500–1,000+ acres for the newest 1-gigawatt-class AI campuses. Floor-area
ratios can be tiny; in extreme cases only a few percent of the controlled site is actual building.
The extra land is not decoration. It is for high-voltage substations (often 10–40 acres by themselves), generator farms
and fuel, cooling yards or water, security setbacks, and phased future buildings. Average new U.S. data-center 
building
size has roughly doubled since the mid-2010s to about 200,000 square feet. Average parcel acquired for these projects
has jumped as well — one 2024 industry figure put it around 224 acres.

Statistics (counts vary by definition)
“Data center” can mean a closet in an office or a 300 MW AI campus, so published totals disagree. Treat every
headline count as a range, not a census.
 U.S. operational facilities are commonly cited in the 3,000–4,400 range depending on the database. Some trackers
that include every small colo and enterprise room go higher. Virginia still leads by count and capacity; Northern
Virginia remains the world’s largest single cluster.
 Larger-building census (mid-2026): on the order of 1,259 existing buildings, 162 under construction, and 328
proposed, with the power pipeline roughly twice standing megawatts.
 Who is building: hyperscalers now dominate new capacity. Enterprise on-premise share of global capacity has
fallen sharply since 2018 as cloud and AI training load moved into purpose-built halls.
 Scale of a typical new hall: operator surveys put average facility size in the mid-30s of megawatts and climbing.
Average new building floor area is near 200,000 sq ft.
 Density is jumping. Rack power that used to average mid-single-digit kilowatts is now commonly in the teens to
high 20s kW in surveys, with AI/GPU halls far higher and increasingly liquid-cooled. Higher watts per rack can, in
theory, shrink future land need — that shift has not yet shown up as smaller campuses.
 Construction money: U.S. data-center construction spending recently overtook office construction. The industry
is also shifting from “one giant building” to campuses of several 150,000–400,000 sq ft halls that can be phased as
power comes online.

Bottom line
Going vertical works and is already used where land is scarce. Two to three stories is the practical U.S.
compromise. Most of the new AI capacity is still being built as low halls on large sites because power,
cooling, structure, and construction speed still favor spreading out when you can. You can take a
constrained urban or suburban parcel and stack two or three floors. You generally cannot turn a 1 GW
training campus into a downtown tower without paying a steep premium in cooling plant, structure, and
schedule.
Note on “where the models live”
Large language models and cloud platforms run in these buildings — training clusters in the huge rural and suburban
campuses, inference closer to users in a mix of large halls and edge sites. There is not one warehouse with a single
“brain” in it. It is a lot of identical halls, a lot of power, and a lot of cooling, spread across Virginia, Texas, the
Midwest, the desert West, and a handful of urban high-rises.
