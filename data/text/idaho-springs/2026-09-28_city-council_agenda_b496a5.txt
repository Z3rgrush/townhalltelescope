TO:   Idaho Springs City Council 
FROM:   Dylan Graves, Community Development Planner 
SUBJECT:  Idaho Springs Noise Ordinance Requirements 
MEETING DATE:  September 28, 2026 – Work Session 
Background: 
The City of Idaho Springs’ Municipal Code (ISMC) regulates noise in Chapter 17 – Miscellaneous Provisions and 
Offenses. 
 
The ISMC does not specify a maximum decibel level for noise. Currently, the Idaho Springs Police Department must 
make a judgement call on what types, volumes, and intensities of noise are unreasonable and address complaints based 
on that subjective standard. Additionally, a citation for Disorderly Conduct is a criminal citation, and the current code 
does not provide the city with any civil remedies related to excessive noise. 
 
Chief Buseck and the Police Department (PD) would like to have additional tools at the department’s disposal to better 
address noise complaints when they come up. They want the ability to give someone a ticket or citation when they go 
out to a property and someone refuses to comply with the noise requirements, rather than having to go through a 
nuisance abatement process. The PD would like to be able to apply specific decibel (dB) requirements so that there is 
an objective standard that the department can enforce. Chief believes that the department has the budget for a 
professional grade dB reader to be able to clearly identify the noise level coming from a property. Using this objective 
standard, an officer can quickly determine whether a complaint is valid.  
 
The goals of an update would be: 
1. Establish objective noise standards consistent with applicable provisions of Colorado law; 
2. Establish reasonable standards for activities that commonly generate noise, including construction, amplified 
sound, mechanical equipment, and temporary events; 
3. Provide clear and predictable standards for residents, businesses, property owners, event organizers, and City 
personnel; 
4. Prevent unreasonable noise disturbances while avoiding unnecessary restrictions on lawful activities; and 
5. Provide reasonable enforcement mechanisms for noise that cannot practicably be evaluated solely by 
measurement of sound pressure levels. 
 
How the Noise Ordinance can be Improved: 
Staff believe that we could add four specific items to the Idaho Springs noise ordinance to improve PD enforcement 
abilities for noise and clarify standards for everyone: 
1. Objective dBA standards — the Colorado baseline.  
2. Time-of-day standards — particularly for nighttime activities.  
3. Prohibited activities and operational standards — construction, amplified sound, vehicles, etc.  
4. General nuisance provision — for genuinely unreasonable noise that can't easily be captured by a meter. 
 
Staff believe that we can largely adopt the state’s noise regulations, but with some added details specific to Idaho 
Springs.

objectionable due to intermittence, beat frequency, or shrillness. The dB(A) described above would be measured from 
the property line and at least 25 feet from the source of the noise.

This all seems reasonable. Staff from the administrative department and PD would be comfortable also adopting these

OTHER THINGS TO CONSIDER: 
 
Construction Hours: 
The ISMC largely does not regulate construction noise, impacts, or hours, which has led to complaints in the past. The 
city’s Standards and Specifications for Design and Construction document states that right-of-way (ROW) permits 
authorize work between the hours of seven o’clock (7:00) am and seven o’clock (7:00) pm, Monday through Thursday, 
and between the hours of seven o’clock (7:00) am and five o’clock (5:00) pm on Fridays, unless the Applicant obtains 
written permission from the City Administrator to do the work earlier or later than the stated hours or on a weekend. 
However, for non-ROW permits, there are no standards.  
 
Staff believe it would be reasonable to establish construction hours as part of a noise ordinance update.  
 
Looking at other communities around the state, there are some common standards.  
• 7:00 a.m. and 7:00 p.m., Monday through Friday

• 8:00 a.m. and 5:00 p.m. on Saturdays.  
• Construction activities are generally prohibited on Sundays and legal holidays unless authorized by the City

The city would need to define construction activity, which could include heavy equipment use, demolition, concrete 
work, saws, compressors, generators, etc.  
 
Vehicle Noise: 
Vehicles in motion would be difficult to enforce under a noise ordinance because they are in motion and are not 
generating continuous noise levels above the required dBa at any given property for more than a brief period. Vehicles 
being run at a property for an extended period would be regulated by this ordinance, but vehicles in motion may need 
more or different consideration, which staff will need to consider in greater detail.  
 
Amplified Sound: 
Amplified sound. No person shall operate or permit the operation of an amplified sound system in such a manner that 
sound exceeds the applicable maximum permissible noise level at the receiving property, or otherwise creates a plainly 
audible and unreasonable disturbance to surrounding properties. 
 
Normal Residential Activities: 
Establishing a definition for “normal residential activities” seems beneficial to avoid situations where residents 
engaged in routine activities around their property are out of compliance with the noise ordinance. For example, lawn 
mowers, leaf blowers, and other power tools can exceed 55 dBa during normal operations, yet are routine and should 
not be prohibited simply because they are over a set noise limit. An exception for normal residential activities during 
daytime hours could be considered under the state’s noise ordinance.  
 
Ordinary activities aren't violations just because they're audible unless they violate the ordinance in some other way: 
• children playing  
• lawn mowing  
• snow removal  
• normal conversation  
• occasional parties  
• residential maintenance  
• normal vehicle operation  
 
Nuisances: 
The same nuisances would still apply as per Chapter 16 of the ISMC. Things like parties, loud music during “quiet 
hours”, etc., could be regulated in that way if they are creating a true public nuisance.  
 
Unreasonable noise prohibited. No person shall make, continue, or permit the making of noise that, because of its 
volume, duration, frequency, intermittence, character, or time of occurrence, unreasonably disturbs the peace, comfort, 
or repose of a reasonable person of ordinary sensitivity in the vicinity. 
 
Noise Permits: 
We may want to consider a 'noise permit' process for planned events that might exceed the permitted noise levels for 
an approved period. This could be a noise permit that allowed higher noise (perhaps only until 10 pm). This could 
allow for special events, small concerts, or other potential events to exceed the city’s noise ordinance for a certain 
period so that the Police Department would not need to respond to every complaint. A noise permit could be 
established as part of the City’s existing special event permitting process without any major additional changes needed 
to the ISMC. If the City wanted to allow events on private property to be allowed to exceed the noise ordinance, 
creating a special permitting process would be necessary.

SUBJECT:  Proposition 123 – Commitment Cycle 2027-2029 
MEETING DATE:  September 28, 2026, City Council Work Session 
 
BACKGROUND: 
 
The City of Idaho Springs is compliant with state Proposition 123 requirements and met its commitment for the first cycle of the 
program, that ran from 2024 to 2026. During this first cycle, the city’s commitment was 22 units.  
 
It is now time to make a new commitment for the next cycle, which is from 2027 to 2029. The city needs to submit its commitment 
by November 1, 2026, if we wish to remain a Proposition 123 committed community. 
 
Benefits of committing to the program include having access to technical assistance for housing development, access to Proposition 
123 specific funding (grants, loans, etc.), and the ability to collaborate with other committed communities. The city received several 
grants during the 2024 to 2026 cycle to help support projects, including funding to get the senior housing project at 839 CO 103 off 
the ground. Being a Proposition 123 community also allowed the Fieldhouse Apartments to secure state funding to help offset 
development costs and restrict the development to a 90% AMI limit.  
 
2027-2029 COMMITMENT REQUIREMENTS: 
 
The state has updated how they calculate a community’s commitment for the 2027-2029 period. 
 
The calculation is as follows: 
• Average number of annual residential new construction building permits (or equivalent) over the past three years 
• Multiply by 10%, 15%, or 20% (based on county job growth rate in relation to state job growth rate)  
• Multiply by 3 (or number of years of commitment) 
• Equals three-year unit growth commitment 
 
I have done the math on the city’s potential commitment based on that formula. The city issued permits for an average of 43 units 
per year from 2023 to 2025. Most of those units were the Fieldhouse Apartments, but the city also issued permits for eight townhomes 
(Habitat homes on Virginia Street), and two new manufactured homes on single-family lots during this period. This means that the 
number of total dwelling units in the city has increased by 129 since the start of 2023.  
 
The city would use the 10% multiplier for county job growth rate, per DOLA.  
 
As such, the calculation is as follows: 42 * 0.10 * 3 = 13 
 
The city’s new commitment for the 2027-2029 Proposition 123 commitment cycle would thus be 13 new affordable dwelling units. 
This is slightly lower than the commitment from the 2024-2026 cycle. City staff believe that this number can be reasonably met by 
2029. 
 
REQUEST FOR DIRECTION: 
 
Does City Council want City Staff to submit a commitment for the 2027-2029 Proposition 123 commitment cycle?

TO:   City Council 
FROM:   Dylan Graves, Community Development Planner 
SUBJECT:  Potential Contributions or Incentives for Affordable Housing 
MEETING DATE:  September 15, 2026 
 
BACKGROUND 
Because the city has been approached by affordable housing developers on several occasions to request incentives to help with 
funding gaps, I was asked to put together a memo that discusses a potential incentive program that the city could offer to developers. 
These requests have in the past requested both financial and non-financial incentives. Idaho Springs has previously considered 
incentives on a case-by-case basis. The goal of this memo is to lay out potential options to formalize the type and scale of potential 
incentives that the city would be willing to offer for affordable housing projects so that all projects are treated equally. This would 
create clarity for developers interested in doing business in the city, even if the ultimate direction is to NOT provide incentives for 
affordable housing.  
 
This report seeks to set out a few items for consideration: 
1. Does City Council want to offer incentives to help support affordable housing developments? 
2. What type of incentives City Council is willing to offer for affordable housing projects 
3. What is the maximum financial considerations that can be offered for a project? 
4. At what affordability levels will incentives be offered? 
 
AREA MEDIAN INCOME (AMI) CONSIDERATIONS 
For a long time, the City Council has discussed the idea that the Area Median Income (AMI) that is identified by the Department of 
Housing and Urban Development (HUD) and the Colorado Housing Finance Authority (CHFA) does not accurately capture Idaho 
Springs’ true housing needs.  
 
The Denver Metropolitan Statistical Area (MSA) Median Family Income is currently identified by HUD as $144,000 for a 4-person 
household making 100% AMI. This is the baseline that HUD and CHFA use when determining who can afford different units and 
at what rates rents can be set. For the Idaho Springs census tract, the “true” AMI for a 4-person household making 100% AMI is 
$105,417. This is ~27% lower than the rate assigned by HUD. This means that the census tract that includes the city is clearly 
misrepresented by the state’s standards. As such, Idaho Springs’ market rate housing and “true” median income is probably 60-70% 
AMI based on the HUD/CHFA charts.  
 
According to HUD charts, a one-bedroom unit designated as 100% AMI can be rented for up to $2,700. This is considered 
“affordable” for someone making 100% of the AMI. However, when looking at Idaho Springs’ true median income, the rate that 
would be affordable for someone making the city’s true AMI is only $1,845, a difference of $855 per month.  
 
The state defines affordable housing as rental housing offered at 60% of AMI and for sale housing offered at 100% of AMI. For 
Idaho Springs, true affordability for rental housing would be in the ~30% AMI range and for sale housing would be at the ~75% 
AMI range.  
 
 
 
Page 8 of 131
2 
 
With that in mind, the city does not “need” any housing at anything higher than ~ 50% of AMI. This is true when you look at the 
DRCOG Regional Housing Needs Assessment, which has the majority of the city’s needs in the 0-50% range. As such, staff 
recommend that if incentives are considered, they would be capped so that the city does not provide incentives for housing that the 
city does not actually need.  
 
For for-sale housing, a program that starts offering incentives at ~80% AMI might be reasonable, since the cheapest housing on the 
market in Idaho Springs city limits today are on the market at that level or higher. There are very few for-sale properties on the 
market now, so adding more units in the 80% AMI range or lower that can be purchased by local residents could be valuable.  
 
HOW TO SET UP AN INCENTIVE PROGRAM 
For any potential incentive program, the developer would need to show that there is a demonstrable financial gap. If there is not a 
gap in the financing, and the project could be built without city incentives, then there is no reason to provide incentive funding. 
Similarly, if there is a $200,000 funding gap, but they are eligible for $400,000 in incentives, the city would only provide a maximum 
incentive of $200,000. We would want to set this up so that the city is the funder of last resort – if our contributions are not required, 
they would not be provided.  
 
Staff would also recommend that the city establishes different “tiers” of standards depending on the affordability of the project to 
provide more incentives to projects that provide more affordability.  
 
For example, when looking at other communities that offer financial incentives, they might break down the incentives per unit as 
follows: 
 
For rental units at 50% AMI: offset of $5,000 per unit 
For rental units at 40% AMI: offset of $10,000 per unit 
For rental units at 30% AMI: offset of $20,000 per unit 
For rental units at 20% AMI or less: offset of $25,000 per unit 
 
For for-sale units at 80% AMI: offset of $5,000 per unit 
For for-sale units at 70% AMI: offset of $10,000 per unit 
For for-sale units at 60% AMI: offset of $20,000 per unit 
For for-sale units at 50% AMI or less: offset of $25,000 per unit 
 
Staff would also recommend that the city determine the maximum financial amount that would be offered per project regardless of 
the total number of units. This could be a percentage cap of the value of the project (e.g. up to 20% of total project budget) or a set 
amount (e.g. $1 million).  
 
POTENTIAL FINANCIAL INCENTIVES 
Financial incentives may include the following. If City Council wants to set up affordable housing incentives, it would be beneficial 
to determine which incentives are appropriate to offer developers.  
 
1. Direct financial assistance 
 
This is the most direct incentive for affordable housing and would involve the city providing direct financial assistance (cash 
payments) to help fund the project. There are cities that have set up affordable housing funds to help fund projects. Longmont, Fort 
Collins, Boulder, and Durango are four Colorado examples of communities that have provided direct funding to housing projects. 
Similarly, cities like Breckenridge or Aspen act as developers on projects and thus are the major funding source for housing projects. 
 
Potential value: depends on the project 
 
2. Land donation and land banking 
 
A land donation program would work by having the city donate land to an affordable housing developer, either fee simple or with a 
long-term lease, to offset the cost of land acquisition associated with the project. This donation could be a sale of the property to a 
developer or could be a long-term lease. Some cities, including Durango, Breckenridge, Frisco, Vail, Glenwood Springs, and 
Boulder, have land banking programs where they acquire land and hold it for housing development. Donating existing city land 
would be relatively inexpensive, though there is limited city-owned land currently available for that use. The state Department of 
Page 9 of 131
3 
 
Local Affairs (DOLA) and Division of Housing (DOH) have set up a land banking program that could help fund property 
acquisitions. 
 
Potential value: depends on the project – low for projects on existing city-owned land, high if the city needed to acquire property 
 
3. Waiver of building permit fees  
4. Waiver of plan review fees 
 
These two incentives could result in a direct cost to the city, since 80% of building permit fees are paid to SAFEbuilt. Staff would 
not recommend the waiver of any fees that would be directly owed to SAFEbuilt for plan review or building permit fees. 
 
That said, a portion of the building permit and plan review fees are paid to the city (approximately 20%). For these fees, the waiver 
or reduction would be less impactful and would cost only city staff time associated with review. 
 
Potential value: For a project valued at $20,000,000, permit fees would be $82,519.63, plus a plan review fee of 65% of the building 
permit fee, leading to total fees of approximately $136,157.39. Of that amount, the city’s portion from the permit and plan review 
fees would be approximately $27,231.48.  
 
5. Waiver or reduction of water tap fees 
6. Waiver or reduction of sewer tap fees 
 
These water and sewer tap fees are plant investment fees paid for the maintenance and construction of water and sewer facilities in 
Idaho Springs. Currently, the city’s water and sewer plants are in good working order, but plant investment fees are used when 
maintenance is required. 
 
Potential value: Depending on the number of taps needed and the size of taps needed, waived fees could be substantial. 
 
7. Waiver or reduction of impact fees 
 
The city charges $5,000 / unit of multifamily housing for impact fees, which are levied for the purpose of mitigating the impact that 
the development projects have upon the City's ability to provide specified public facilities. These fees go towards growth-related 
improvements, facilities and equipment in the general functional area of parks, fire, police, municipal facilities, recreation, 
transportation, and storm water management. In the past, the city has been willing to apply the impact fees to public improvements 
associated with the project in question. For example, the Fieldhouse Apartments project was able to apply their impact fees to the 
widening of Miner Street, addition of sidewalks, and a new water line that provided clear public infrastructure investment.  
 
Potential value: $5,000 / unit 
 
8. Waiver or reduction of third-party fees (Engineering, Attorney, etc.) 
 
Most land use projects require review by the city’s engineers and the city’s attorneys. Since the city contracts engineering and legal 
services, any reduction in these review fees would be a real cost to the city because these review fees will still need to be paid by 
someone for services rendered. 
 
Potential value: Costs depend on the number of review hours needed. Average costs per hour for these services are between 
approximately $188/hour and $240/hour.  
 
9. Waiver or reduction of planning / entitlement fees 
 
This would not cost the city any “real” funds, as this would simply be a waiver of application fees and staff fees associated with the 
development review process. This would be a cost in the sense of waiving fees associated with staff time but would not cost the city 
anything beyond staff salaries. These fees normally amount to a maximum of a few thousand dollars per project.  
 
Potential value: Planning review fees are $75/hour, Administrative staff fees are $50/hour, City Administrator fees are $100/hour 
 
10. Deferral of fees 
Page 10 of 131
4 
 
 
Instead of paying fees early on during the project before revenues exist to pay out of pocket costs/fees, the city could defer payment 
of fees until financing is available to pay for said expenses. Deferral could be an option to help support the project if the city is 
unwilling to waive or reduce fees. 
 
11. City Excise or Sales Tax Waivers 
 
During construction, materials and equipment may be subject to city excise or sales taxes. The city could waive these taxes for 
affordable housing projects. This incentive would require a rebate. The city would collect the relevant tax and then rebate it back to 
the developer. There would be no “real” cost to the city but the city would forego potential tax revenues.  
 
NON-FINANCIAL INCENTIVES 
There are non-financial incentives or bonuses that could be offered to developers looking to construct affordable housing in the city. 
These reduce costs of development and/or increase the total number of units that could be built on a given property.  
 
12. Parking Reduction / Parking Fee-in-Lieu 
 
Reducing parking requirements for affordable housing projects can help developers by reducing construction costs and allowing 
them to build more housing units, thus increasing long-term revenues. The downside to a parking reduction is that it will increase 
the parking burden on existing streets or city-owned parking lots. Parking fee-in-lieu is only $2,500 / space for residential uses. 
Something the city could offer developers is the ability to waive fee-in-lieu fees if they are eligible for a parking fee-in-lieu. 
 
13. Density Bonuses 
14. Height Increases 
15. Open Space Reductions 
16. Lot-Width Reductions 
17. Setback Reductions 
 
These programs allow additional housing to be built at a higher density on a given property. Adding an extra story to a building can 
significantly increase revenues for the developer without substantially increasing costs. Reducing open space requirements allows a 
developer to build more units on a lot. Lot-width reductions and setback reductions allow more space to be used for parking, building 
footprints, and other beneficial uses. Any or all of these potential land use incentives would increase a developer’s ability to maximize 
buildout of their property. 
 
These incentives come with a list of tradeoffs that would need to be considered. While they do not cost the city money, there could 
be impacts felt by adjacent property owners. Allowing increased height would create taller buildings that could impact views and 
change the community character by adding mass to existing neighborhoods. Open space reductions result in greater amounts of space 
filled with impervious surfaces. Lot-width and setback reductions would allow for buildings to be built more closely to adjacent 
properties.  
 
REQUEST FOR DIRECTION: 
 
1. Is City Council interested in pursuing affordable housing incentives further? 
 
2. How much is City Council willing to incentivize? 
 
3. Which incentives would City Council be willing to offer for affordable housing? 
 
4. What affordability levels would City Council want to see in exchange for an incentive?

SUBJECT:  Updates to Sections 21-205 and 21-220, Building Height 
MEETING DATE:  September 15, 2026 
 
BACKGROUND 
There has been significant talk about the need to consider architectural design updates throughout the city to better regulate how new 
developments or significant redevelopments look architecturally.  
 
The city regulates architectural design in several places, including: 
• Section 21-205: Design setting 
• Section 21-220: Multifamily and commercial architecture 
• Building height and other development standards from Sec. 21-63 
• Historic District: Design Guidelines for Historic Structures 
• Chapter 26: East End Overlay District (EEOD) 
• Other areas that impact overall building design (setbacks, parking requirements, landscaping requirements, etc.) 
 
I spoke with Planning Commission about these topics in August and there was interest in working on some or all of them. Key 
questions that came up were: 
 
1. Do we want to strengthen and/or update the design setting requirements in Section 21-205 for the area around the historic 
district and gateway areas? 
2. Do we want to update architectural standards citywide for new multifamily and commercial developments? 
3. Do we want to consider architectural standards for single-family and duplex construction?  
4. Do we want to consider changes to the city’s building height standards? 
5. Do we want to consider changes to the East End Overlay District? 
 
The goal of this work session item is to get high-level feedback on each of the above questions to determine how staff should 
proceed researching architectural design.  
 
Section 21-205: Design Setting 
For a bit of background, back in late 2025, I discussed with the Historic Preservation Review Commission (HPRC) ways to 
strengthen the two-block buffer zone discussed in Section 21-205(A)(2) of the Idaho Springs Municipal Code (ISMC), which 
requires the following: 
 
New developments within a two-block radius of the outside boundary of the Historic District should build upon and support the 
architectural features of the District itself. Use of Historic District building materials, architectural styles and designs based upon 
historic City sites are strongly encouraged. 
 
There are 102 total properties in the two-block buffer zone of the Historic District, and bordered by the following streets/roads: 
• 12th Avenue to the West 
• Virginia Street to the North 
• 19th Avenue/Soda Creek Road to the East 
• I-70 to the South 
 
Section 21-205(A)(4) states that New projects within the immediate vicinity (three hundred [300] feet of the edge of Colorado 
Department of Transportation right-of-way at the interchange) of Community Gateways shall develop elements of signage, 
landscaping and site design to create and support a sense of entry to the City. Design work at gateways to the City should reflect 
the historic mining heritage of the community.

Request for Direction: Does City Council want to see these sections strengthened and/or clarified to further protect the city’s 
historic district buffer and gateway areas? 
 
Section 21-220: Commercial and Multifamily Architecture 
Section 21-220 of the ISMC concerns multifamily and commercial development, setting architectural standards for new 
developments. This section applies to every multifamily or commercial development citywide, not just in the gateway areas of the 
city or in the “buffer zone”. Since there are strong examples of historic-style buildings city-wide and the city does have a number 
of existing, mature residential neighborhoods, ensuring that architecture is compatible across the city may be a positive goal, 
especially since we have heard that there is appetite for redevelopment on the east and west end of the city.  
 
Existing requirements are relatively broad, so perhaps we could focus on incorporating more specific language that speaks to the 
type of buildings we want to see around the city. Do we want all buildings to have elements of Victorian-era architecture, or are 
we okay with different designs outside of the downtown area (e.g. mountain modern design), as long as they look nice? There are a 
lot of directions the city could go in updating this section.  
 
Request for Direction: Is City Council interested in looking into revisions to Section 21-220, which applies to any multifamily or 
commercial development citywide? 
 
Single Family and Duplex Residential Design Requirements: 
If the city is worried about changes to the character of the community, the city could consider creating basic requirements for 
single-family architectural design. Currently, there are no such standards. Someone could demolish their existing 120+ year old 
home and build a modern home that does not have any historic architectural design features. The city has historically been hesitant 
to regulate new single-family developments, but if interest in the city continues to grow, we could see people interested in 
redeveloping existing single-family lots around the city. 
 
Request for Direction: Does City Council want to consider architectural design requirements for single-family and duplex 
developments in addition to multifamily and commercial developments?  
 
Building Height: 
Currently, the city allows a 35-foot maximum height limit in every commercial and residential zone district, with the exception of 
the East End Overlay District (EEOD), which allows even taller heights in some areas. The industrial zone districts also vary in 
height requirements.  
 
One of the concerns city staff have heard from the community is that a new 3-story building would be out of scale with existing 
properties around the city. Many of the existing single-family homes in Idaho Springs are shorter than 35’ and most are at 
maximum 2-stories tall. There is concern that if someone’s neighbor builds a 35’ tall building, its mass and scale would detract 
from the city’s current character.  
 
If City Council is interested in pursuing this further, there are a few ways that height could be addressed. The simplest way to 
address this concern would be to simply reduce the height maximum allowances in certain or all zoning districts, perhaps to 25-30 
feet. A second way would be to apply a standard that compares the height of a proposed building to adjacent buildings within a 
certain area. For example, the city could say that no new building can be more than 10% taller than the average height of principal 
buildings within 100’ of the subject property. This would mean that if the average height of those adjacent buildings is 25 feet, the 
new building being built could not exceed 27.5 feet. This ensures that new buildings are suitable from a massing standpoint to not 
stand out compared to its neighbors. The maximum height could remain at 35 feet where appropriate, but would limit height to a 
lower maximum in areas where existing buildings are shorter.  
Page 13 of 131
3 
 
Another important height-related consideration is how height is calculated. For a non-flat roof, the 35’ height limit is taken from 
the average height between the highest ridge and the eave height. This functionally allows for buildings that have a ridge height 
taller than 35’.  
 
Request for Direction: Does City Council want to consider changing the city’s height standards? 
 
East End Overlay District 
A final item for discussion is the city’s East End Overlay District (EEOD), which sets different standards for setbacks, building 
height, design standards, and other standards as compared to the city’s traditional zoning districts. The EEOD allows for buildings 
that are built closer to the street and, in some cases, taller heights, as seen below: 
 
No property owner or developer has yet taken advantage of the EEOD standards, but in the future, if someone submitted an 
application on the far east end of the city for a 57’ tall building, it would meet the EEOD standards. Similarly, a 46’ tall building 
could be permitted in the vicinity of City Hall. The goal of the EEOD is to encourage redevelopment of the eastern side of the city, 
and the way it does that is by creating flexible standards that would allow for more density and thus more profits. However, since 
there are concerns about building height and community character, staff thought it was worth bringing up whether any changes to 
the overlay district might be warranted.  
 
Request for Direction: Does City Council want to consider changes to the EEOD? 
 
Residential Historic Districts and Local Designation of Historic Properties 
Another important consideration is whether historic designation of historic properties could address local concerns about place-
making and design. There are enough buildings with high historical integrity to create new districts or expand the existing historic 
district. However, political will is needed, as there is a misconception that if your property is designated as a historic resource, you 
are severely limited in what you can do. However, if the city really wants to maintain historic character and preserve the defining 
characteristics of the city, preserving the existing buildings is critical. This will require public buy-in, since property cannot be 
designated without property owner consent.

Staff Analysis: 
 
1. Single-Family Detached, Single-Family Attached, Townhome, and Duplex Development 
 
No changes are suggested for single-family detached development. Idaho Springs’ current standards perfectly match the standards 
found across the 32 jurisdictions reviewed.  
 
For single-family attached, townhome, or duplex development, most other communities simply establish a standard for these units, 
rather than determining the parking requirement based on bedroom count. If the city wanted to make a change, setting these to a 
requirement of 2 spaces / unit (regardless of bedroom count) could be reasonable. 
 
2. Multifamily, studio 
 
Idaho Springs requires 1.25 spaces / studio apartment currently. The average across 32 surveyed jurisdictions for studios is 1.32 
spaces per unit, with a range of 1 / unit to 1.5 / unit being the most common range. This shows that Idaho Springs is regulating 
studio apartments similarly to other communities around Colorado.  
 
3. Multifamily, 1-bedroom 
 
Idaho Springs requires 1.5 spaces per 1-bedroom unit. The average required across the surveyed jurisdictions is 1.41 spaces per 1-
bedroom unit. Idaho Springs is already well-aligned with the average found across the state. The average is higher than for studio 
apartments, with the idea being that a 1-bedroom unit is more likely to be shared by two residents, while a studio is more 
appropriate for a single occupant. Only 3 of the 32 jurisdictions require 2 or more parking spaces per unit for 1-bedroom units.  
 
4. Multifamily, 2-bedroom 
 
Idaho Springs requires between 1.5 and 1.75 spaces per unit for a 2-bedroom unit. The surveyed average is 1.77 spaces per 2-
bedroom unit. Idaho Springs is thus very slightly below the standard found elsewhere in the state. The most commonly required 
spaces per unit across the surveyed jurisdictions is 2 spaces / 2-bedroom unit. 1.5 spaces / 2-bedroom unit is the second most 
commonly seen requirement. Almost no other jurisdictions have different parking space standards depending on square footage, so 
it may make sense to establish one standard for all 2-bedroom units, regardless of size. Something between 1.5 / unit and 2 / unit 
might be reasonable based on what we see elsewhere across the state.  
 
5. Multifamily, 3+ bedrooms

3 
 
Idaho Springs requires between 1.75 and 2.5 spaces per unit for a 3+ bedroom unit. The average across surveyed jurisdictions is 2 
spaces per unit. Staff believe that establishing a set standard regardless of unit size makes sense. Since Idaho Springs already 
requires 2.5 spaces / unit for larger 3+ bedroom units, establishing a set 2.5 space / unit standard may make sense. 
 
6. Senior Housing 
 
As we had previously discussed, some communities have different standards for senior housing developments. For jurisdictions 
who have separate standards for senior housing, the average number of spaces required per unit varied from 0.5-1.0 spaces / unit.  
 
The Planning Commission thought that it was wise to consider adding guest parking standards for multifamily units to 
account for guests and visitors. The Planning Commission also thought it would be wise to set a single standard for single-
family attached and/or duplex, multifamily 2-bedroom, and multifamily 3+ bedroom units, rather than tying the number of 
parking spaces to square footage.

COMMERCIAL DEVELOPMENT 
 
The city’s off-street parking requirements for commercial uses are more complicated than residential uses, as commercial uses 
tend to require different standards depending on the type of use. For example, a theatre use requires 1 off-street parking space / 4 
seats in the theatre, while other uses are based on internal square footage (offices are 1 space / 400 square feet of floor area).  
 
After reviewing the city’s standards against the 32 jurisdictions surveyed, the city’s commercial parking standards are similar to 
the averages found elsewhere. In general, most communities regulate retail, office, and lodging uses similarly. However, there are 
a few items that city staff would like to discuss.  
 
1. Rafting Headquarters and other guiding/outfitting headquarters 
 
Larimer County is the only other jurisdiction besides Idaho Springs that I could find in Colorado that specifically calls out parking 
for rafting headquarters. Larimer County requires 1 parking space / 200 square feet of floor area, while Idaho Springs requires 1 
space / 400 square feet of floor area.  
 
A rafting headquarters concentrates a significant amount of use into a small area, including employee and visitor parking, storage 
of rafts, vehicles, and trailers, and safety training / education prior to heading off onto the river, taking the square footage of the 
headquarters building is an incomplete picture for parking demand. There are very few ways that this is addressed in other 
communities in Colorado that I could find, but I have considered what might be reasonable.  
 
Rather than just say 1 space / 400 square feet, the city could update the standard to 1 / space per 400 square feet, PLUS 1 space / 
employee or guide. Or the city could regulate it based on guests by saying 1 space / 400 square feet, PLUS 1 space / 3 customers.  
Page 17 of 131
4 
 
 
This would provide a base parking requirement for this use and then require additional parking based on the intensity of the use. A 
small rafting outfitter with only a few boats a day is going to generate far less traffic than a large outfitter with 20+ boats per day, 
yet both might operate out of a similarly sized headquarters building. 
 
This is something that could apply to other guiding or outfitting services. Rafting is currently the primary guided activity in Idaho 
Springs, though we have one ziplining operation and, in the future, could see more outdoor recreation-related service uses, like 
ATVs, mountain biking, fly fishing, etc. As such, it seems wise to address this standard currently while making other changes. 
 
The Planning Commission liked the idea of changing “River rafting offices and operations” to “Guiding offices and 
operations” to cover additional industries that are guiding / outfitter focused. They also liked the idea of setting a new 
standard based on the number of employees and guests that are anticipated at the site. Something like 1 space / employee 
PLUS 1 space / 3-4 guests at peak times. More research would be needed to determine how to best set this up and then 
monitor it in practice.   
 
2. Theaters, Event Center / Music Venue, and Indoor Recreation venues 
 
The city currently requires 1 space / 4 theatre seats. This is relatively common across the state. However, this calculation does not 
work as well if the theatre does not have seats. Eagle's land-use code says that when a parking standard is based on seating, “if 
fixed seats are not provided, then parking shall be determined at a rate of one space per four occupants.” Changing the wording in 
the ISMC from 1 space / 4 seats to “1 parking space / 4 occupants based on the maximum occupant load established by the adopted 
building code” would solve this problem and reduce complexity.  
 
It has been previously noted that theaters have an unusual parking profile, which may need additional consideration. A 7:00 PM 
show might create very high demand for 2–3 hours, but the parking demand is much lower during the day. The question for 
theatres and event venues is how City Council would like to consider uses that solely take place outside of the city’s “busier” 
times? 
 
The Planning Commission thought that a change from 1 / 4 seats to 1 / 4 occupants would clarify the requirement. There 
was not a clear recommendation about how to consider parking demand outside the city’s managed parking hours.  
 
3. Daycare 
 
The city does not have parking standards for daycare use. Daycare is a strong priority for the city and county over the next few 
years and if someone wished to establish a daycare, we would not have a clear parking requirement for this use. Daycare uses tend 
to have high peak parking demands during drop off and pick up but otherwise have relatively low parking needs throughout the 
day.  
 
Different jurisdictions use different standards. For those who establish it on a square footage basis, the average is about 1 space / 
300 square feet. The other way is to create a per child or per employee standard. Rifle and Nederland require 1 space / 10 children, 
and Grand Lake and Silverthorne require 1 space / 8 children. Carbondale, Alamosa, and Durango require 1 space / employee.  
 
Establishing a standard for this use may be beneficial in anticipation of more daycare and early childhood education options in 
Idaho Springs.  
 
The Planning Commission agreed that a standard for daycares would be beneficial. It was suggested that 1 space / 8 
children would be a good standard.  
 
4. Fitness Centers 
Page 18 of 131
5 
 
 
This is one of the only commercial parking standards where the city is generally more aggressive than other jurisdictions. The 
average across the other jurisdictions is approximately 1 space / 200-300 square feet of floor area. Changing this standard would 
reduce the parking demand for a 10,000 square foot gym from 67 parking spaces to 35-50 spaces. Speaking with the Clear Creek 
Metropolitan Recreation District, their maximum daily number of users is about 300 from when the facility opens at 6:30am until 
it closes at 8pm. This is an average of approximately 22 users per hour, though the before work and after work peaks are much 
higher. Those peak periods before and after work are the primary times when 1 space / 150 square feet might be appropriate. 
However, during the majority of the day, that many parking spaces would be unnecessary.  
 
The Planning Commission thought that this should be researched in greater detail. While 1 space / 150 square feet does 
seem higher than necessary, the Commission asked me to do some more research on the actual demand of the current rec 
center and on parking needs for other types of fitness facilities. 
 
5. Other 
 
We didn’t see anything else that is substantially different from what is seen elsewhere around the state. That said, does the City 
Council have any additional thoughts on parking regulations that might benefit from a change or might need to be added to the 
ISMC?
