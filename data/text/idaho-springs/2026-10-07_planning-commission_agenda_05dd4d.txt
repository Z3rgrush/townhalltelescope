GENERAL UPDATES 
Associate Attorney Nick Klien advised the Commission that, at the last Council meeting, Councilmember Collier 
requested that he address the role of alternates on the City’s boards, as there appears to be some confusion regarding 
their participation and contribution. Mr. Klien explained that, for quasi-judicial matters, including public hearings, 
alternates should not participate in the hearing. For example, this would apply to today’s public hearing regarding the 
Minor Subdivision. However, for non-quasi-judicial matters, such as Ordinance 17, 2026, alternates may provide their 
opinions and participate in the discussion. Mr. Klien further explained that if an alternate is prohibited from participating 
in a quasi-judicial hearing, they may, in their capacity as a member of the public, provide public comment during the 
hearing. This provides an alternative opportunity for the alternate to express their opinions.

Community Development Planner Communication 
Meeting Date: October 7, 2026 
To:  Idaho Springs Planning Commission 
From:  Dylan Graves, Community Development Planner 
RE:  Community Development Report 
 
Here are some updates on what might be coming up over the next few months and progress on things 
that we have already talked about, with estimated next steps.  
 
Additionally, we have two open seats at the moment on the Planning Commission. Jefferson Lich has 
stepped down from his Alternate position, and Kent Slaymaker stepped down from his Regular position. 
As such, we have one alternate seat and one regular seat available. Please let anyone who might be 
interested know about these openings. They were posted in the paper last Thursday. 
 
Code Amendments and other Planning Activities: 
 
1. Noise Ordinance – The City is working on an update to the existing noise ordinance. The goal is 
to create a more objective standard that the Police Department can use during noise-related 
disputes. The Department is going to be purchasing a decibel reader and will start taking some 
baseline noise measurements around the city, which will help determine what sorts of 
regulations might be most beneficial. Expect to see more on this before the end of the year.  
 
2. Exterior Lighting – This is on the agenda for tonight’s work session. 
 
3. Nonconforming buildings / uses – This is on the agenda for tonight’s PC Meeting. This would 
come to City Council after this PC meeting for discussion and first reading of the ordinance. 
More information on this is included in the meeting packet. 
 
4. Architectural Design – I spoke with the City Council about this on September 28th. I asked them 
about five different architectural topics that we had discussed and whether they wanted to 
consider them in more detail. These include: 
a. Changes to Sec. 21-205 (design setting) to better address the area surrounding the 
downtown historic district and gateway areas 
b. Changes to Sec. 21-220 (multifamily and commercial architecture) to see if they wanted 
these strengthened / clarified to better address new buildings around the City outside 
the downtown area 
c. Addition of single-family standards for architectural design if someone wanted to build a 
new house 
d. Potential changes to the height limits in certain zoning districts around the City 
e. Potential changes to the East End Overlay District allowances 
Page 6 of 178
2 
 
The City Council advised that a. and b. above are the highest priorities, but we should consider 
all five of the topics in more detail to see if any small tweaks, additions, or other changes might 
be beneficial. I will work on this topic over the next month so that we can discuss this topic 
again before the end of the year. There are no current developments under consideration, so 
we have a bit of time to work on this before any other applications are under review. I plan to 
have a work session on this topic in November or December.  
 
5. Parking Study – This was on the September 28th City Council Work Session agenda. City Council 
advised that we should consider changes to the parking requirements based on the study, so I 
will have this as a topic for discussion again at one of the upcoming meetings. 
 
6. Outdoor Storage Regulations – This is something that has been discussed in the past to better 
regulate how outdoor storage of equipment and materials is handled around the City on both 
residential and commercial properties to ensure that properties do not become eyesores. Things 
that could be included in a code amendment include location requirements for storage, 
screening standards, etc.  
 
7. Landscaping Standards – There are a few state regulations that have been passed over the past 
few years that are newly in effect or will soon be in effect. The first states that local 
governments are no longer permitted to allow new commercial and industrial developments to 
landscape with nonfunctional turf. In 2028, this regulation will be further applied to residential 
developments. Nonfunctional turf is any turf that is not located in a recreational use area or 
other space that is regularly used for civic, community, or recreational purposes. The law 
requires that cities require native species and xeriscaping standards. A code amendment is likely 
not needed now, but my plan is to start working on a list of native plants that work well in the 
Idaho Springs landscape based on altitude, climate, drought-tolerance, etc. This can then be 
distributed to developers and property owners who are looking to develop in Idaho Springs to 
better clarify the types of plants that the city wants to see and knows can thrive in this 
environment.  
 
8. Update to the city’s Communal Outdoor Dining Area (CODA) regulations to address design 
standards – This topic was broached by the Business and Community Promotions Board (BCPB) 
at their most recent meeting. This would affect activations in the downtown commercial area on 
Miner Street when the marketplace is set up and the road is closed to vehicles. There has been 
some talk about tightening up the rules and regulations about what can be put in activation 
areas to help increase continuity along Miner Street and ensure that all activations are safe, 
durable, and high-quality, for both safety and aesthetic considerations. The goal is to still allow 
businesses to maintain individuality and quirkiness, as desired, but to set basic standards to 
avoid safety risks or eyesores. I have not started this research in earnest.  
 
9. Historic Designation Code Amendment – The city has a goal to create new historic districts and 
designate new historic sites within the city. This protects the properties from substantial 
modifications while also making those properties eligible for grant funding and tax credits. 
Currently, to create a historic district, 100% of the owners of properties proposed for the district 
must be agreeable. Many cities have a lower standard – 70-80% is a percentage seen commonly 
elsewhere in the state. This is something that the city’s HPRC brought up at a recent meeting. 
Another topic for discussion in the future is a code amendment to set integrity standards for 
historic properties.

Voluntary Parking Fee in Lieu Program 
Pursuant to Idaho Springs Municipal Code Sec. 21 -134, in lieu of the provision of creating physical 
parking spaces, an eligible  applicant may request a substituted cash fee  in lieu of provision of  all or a 
portion of the otherwise required parking spaces.  
The Parking Fee in Lieu Program is available to properties that are within the parking fee in lieu 
program boundary, generally defined by Water St. to the south, Colorado Blvd. to the north, 13th Ave. to 
the West, and  Riverside Dr. to the East. Please  refer to Idaho Springs Municipal Code Figure 21- 134.1 
for an exact program boundary.  
Submission Requirements 
1. Site Plan 
2. Justification Report / Letter for the Request for Payment in Lieu of Off-Street Parking 
3. Proof of Ownership and Owner Consent to Application 
Decision Criteria 
1. Whether the applicant demonstrates that  all required parking cannot be reasonably located  on-
site due to site-specific physical constraints or site design requirements.  
2.  Whether the historic significance of  existing buildings or other site features would be adversely 
affected to meet the parking requirement on site.  
Whether the property is located in proximity to permanent public  parking or public 
transportation routes.  
4. Whether the characteristics of the proposed land use reduce the need for on-site parking. 
5,  Whether the public health, safety or welfare would be adversely affected by locating 
the required parking on the property. 
Fee Amount (Per space): 
Restaurant or Retail Development:  $20,000 
Office or Personal Service Development:  
$10,000 Residential Development: $2,500 
An approved applicant must  enter into a payment agreement with  the City which shall be recorded 
prior to the issuance of a building permit. The City Administrator is  granted the authority to grant 
a request for a payment plan which may include annual installments, including interest, over a 
multiyear period not to exceed ten ( I 0) years and which may include provisions for 
termination of the Certificate of Occupancy for the affected land use if timely payments are not 
made and such other remedies deemed adequate bv the City to address nonpayment.

RE: Request for Parking In Lieu Approval for 16 Parking Spaces Pursuant to Idaho Springs

Dear Planning Commission Members, 
As the Executive Director of the Clear Creek Center for Arts & Education (CCCAE), I am writing 
to inform you of the extraordinary gift of the historic Roberts Brothers' Garage property that is 
currently in the process of being donated to CCCAE by the Bair family. The property is held 
under Four Bairs, Inc. and is located at 121 15th Avenue. 
This generous donation will allow CCCAE to preserve, rehabilitate, and activate one of Idaho 
Springs' historic landmarks as a community arts, education, and cultural center serving our 
community throughout Clear Creek County. It is planned to be a flexible workshop, 
performance, classroom, exhibition, and event space, enabled by more than $3 million in 
funding support available to our community for this purpose. 
Per Table 21-127-1, Parking Requirements by Use, the anticipated uses produce the following 
parking requirements.  
Proposed use Code Standard Required Spaces (max 3000 sq ft)  
Event or theater 1 per 4 seats  16 (64-seat maximum/4) 
Arts/Classroom 1 per 300 GSF  10 (3000/300) 
Office 1 per 400 GSF 8 (3000/400) 
Because the event or theater use produces the highest parking requirement, CCCAE 
respectfully requests approval of a Parking Fee In Lieu arrangement for sixteen (16) required 
parking spaces pursuant to Section 21.134 of the Idaho Springs Municipal Code. 
We believe this request satisfies the Criteria in Section 21.134 and is consistent with the intent of 
the Code, which recognizes that certain historic properties and community-serving uses 
present unique circumstances in which strict compliance with on-site parking requirements 
may be unreasonable.

(1) All Required Parking Cannot Be Reasonably Located On-Site Due to Physical Constraints or 
Site Design Requirements 
The property is constrained by the existing historic building, limited site area, and the design 
requirements necessary to accommodate classrooms, performance space, an exhibition 
area, and accessibility improvements. Sixteen parking spaces cannot be accommodated on 
the site without substantially compromising the historic structure, accessibility, usable program 
space, and the community purpose for which the property is being donated. 
Providing all required parking on-site would require a reduction in usable building and/or 
program space, undermining the project's educational, cultural, and economic development 
mission and its benefits to children, seniors, and the broader community. The adaptive reuse of 
the property prioritizes public access, community programming, and preservation of the 
historic structure. As a result, all required parking cannot reasonably be accommodated on-
site without compromising the functionality, usability, and public benefit of the project. 
(2) Historic Significance Would Be Adversely Affected 
The proposed project preserves and activates a historic property that contributes to the 
character and cultural heritage of downtown. Constructing the additional parking necessary 
to satisfy the full parking requirement would require alterations that may diminish the historic 
setting, integrity, and character of the property. The adaptive reuse of this historic building as a 
community arts and education center preserves an important local resource while creating a 
new public benefit. The Parking Fee In Lieu option allows the City to support historic 
preservation goals while accommodating parking demand through shared public resources. 
(3) Proximity to Public Parking or Public Transportation 
The property is located in downtown Idaho Springs, within walking distance of public parking 
facilities, on-street parking, and other shared parking resources regularly used by residents and 
visitors. Because the center will operate primarily after 6:00 p.m., it can draw upon an existing 
parking network that serves multiple businesses, civic uses, and community destinations. 
Programming will occur at staggered times, including scheduled classes, workshops, 
performances, and community events, rather than generating continuous parking demand 
throughout the day. Participants may also walk from nearby neighborhoods, schools, or 
downtown businesses, carpool, or combine a CCCAE visit with another downtown destination. 
(4) Characteristics of the Proposed Use Reduce the Need for On-Site Parking 
The proposed use differs substantially from traditional retail, restaurant, or office uses that 
generate continuous daily parking demand. As a nonprofit arts and education center, 
CCCAE's activities consist primarily of scheduled classes, workshops, exhibitions, performances, 
community meetings, rehearsals, and cultural events that occur at designated times and

TO:   Idaho Springs Planning Commission 
FROM:   Dylan Graves, Community Development Planner 
SUBJECT:  Parking Fee-In-Lieu Application for Roberts Brothers’ Garage, 121 15 th Avenue 
MEETING DATE:  October 7 2026 
ATTACHMENTS: Application and Property Owner Consent Documents 
REQUEST 
Fee-in-lieu of up to 16 parking spaces at 121 15 th Avenue to accommodate an events venue and educational arts space within the 
Roberts Brothers’ Garage.  
 
BACKGROUND 
The applicant (Carla Cole, Clear Creek Center for Arts and Education (CCCAE)) has applied to the city’s Voluntary Parking Fee-
in-Lieu Program, as stipulated in Sec. 21-134 of the Idaho Springs Municipal Code (ISMC). They are seeking the Planning 
Commission’s approval to utility the Voluntary Fee-in-Lieu program for the 16 parking spaces required for their proposed 
development, an arts and education space and event venue at the Roberts Brothers’ Garage, located at 121 15th Avenue.  
 
The ISMC requires in Sec. 21-127 that 1 off-street parking space be provided per 4 seats for theater uses. The applicant proposes an 
approximately 60-seat theater, which would require 15 total parking spaces. They are estimating one additional space will be needed 
for loading. This would lead to a total of 16 required spaces. The 16 spaces would only be required during events where the capacity 
of the building would be up to ~60 people. This is anticipated to only be in the evenings and almost always after 6pm, after the city’s 
paid parking period ends. During the day, public events are not anticipated but there may be workshops, classes, and other educational 
programming taking place during that time. Approximately 6 spaces would be needed for daily activities based on the parking table 
from Sec. 21-127. Office space requires 1 space/400 square feet of floor area. The building is just over 2,000 square feet. So, while 
the typical parking needs are ~6 spaces, the maximum need for the theater/event space use will be 16.  
 
The current owners of the building, Four Bairs Inc., have negotiated with the CCCAE to set aside several spaces on-site for Beau 
Jo’s parking. As such, while there is some on-site space for parking at 121 15th Avenue, the available spaces are mostly set aside for 
use by the existing owner.  
 
EVALUATION AGAINST APPROVAL CRITERIA (ISM SEC. 21-134(E)) 
 
1. On-Site Parking Feasibility (Sec. 21-134 (E)(l)) 
 
Criterion Language: Whether the applicant demonstrates that all required parking cannot be reasonably located on-site due to site-
specific physical constraints or site design requirements. 
 
Analysis: The previous use of this building is as storage. It is being converted into an arts and event venue that will be used for live 
events, workshops, educational programs, practice space, and other activities related to the arts and arts education. This is a 
substantial change in use from the previous storage use. This transition necessitates compliance with the city’s updated parking 
requirements, which will require 16 parking spaces based on the parking use table for a theater use.  
 
The property has existing informal parking to the south of the Roberts Brothers’ Garage building. Based on submitted site plans, the 
current open space area on the site measures approximately 20’ x 70’. This would accommodate a maximum of 7-8 parking spaces. 
Access would be from the south end of the property, adjacent to the city’s public parking lots.  
 
A few of these spaces are to be retained by the current owners for their personal use. The current property owners – Four Bairs Inc. 
– would like to maintain several parking spaces on the property for Beau Jo’s employee parking. 
 
Additionally, the city is anticipating construction of a parking structure around this building as part of the city’s mobility hub project. 
This would block off a portion of access to the southern end of the property, reducing the amount of space available for parking. It 
is unclear at this time how much this area could be impacted.  
 
Finally, the long-term goal for the CCCAE is to use existing outdoor space as usable garden space that can be activated for events, 
or for an addition to the existing building that could be used to expand indoor programming.

2 
 
With all that in mind, staff do not believe that they can reasonably fit all 16 required parking spaces on the property. Given all the 
above, staff believe that Criterion #1 is met. There is a need for Parking Fee-in-Lieu to accommodate at least a portion of the off-
street parking required for the proposed project. 
 
2. Impact on Historic Significance (Sec. 21-134(E)(2)) 
 
Criterion Language: Whether the historic significance of existing buildings or other site features would be adversely affected to meet 
the parking requirement on site. 
 
Analysis: It does not appear that the historic significance of the existing building or other site features would be adversely affected 
if parking spaces were provided on-site. Informal parking has occurred to the south of the building for many years with no adverse 
impacts on the garage building. The only way to fit all 16 required spaces on the property would be to reconfigure the existing 
building, which would have an adverse historical impact on the property.  
 
This application appears to align with Criterion #2, though some number of spaces could be provided on the property without 
having adverse impacts. 
 
3. Proximity to Public Parking / Transportation (Sec. 21-134(E)(3)) 
 
Criterion Language: Whether the property is located in close proximity to permanent public parking or public transportation routes. 
 
Analysis: There is substantial public parking south of the property in the main City public parking lots situated between downtown 
Idaho Springs and Interstate 70. It is located approximately two blocks east of the City’s primary bus stop, located at Exit 240 off 
Interstate 70. Given its close proximity to these transportation and parking resources, this application appears to align with 
Criterion #3.  
 
4. Land Use and Parking Needs (Sec. 21-134(E)(4)) 
 
Criterion Language: Whether the characteristics of the proposed land use reduce the need for on-site parking. 
 
Analysis: The use of the space is increasing from a very low-impact space (private storage) to a relatively high-impact space 
(theatre/event space use). The need for parking will clearly increase once this use is established.  
 
However, there are several reasons why the need for on-site parking might be lower than anticipated. First, the daily use of the space 
will not induce the full parking demand required by the ISMC. The day-to-day parking needs are for a use more akin to an office or 
daycare use with intermittent demand. The main demand for parking will be for evening events. During these events, significant 
parking will be required. However, these events are likely to occur outside of the city’s paid parking hours, when demand for parking 
spaces is lower. Finally, it is anticipated that many visitors to this use will also frequent other businesses in the downtown Miner 
Street area. This use has the potential to generate additional interest in adjacent businesses during less popular times. Since the use 
may generate foot traffic elsewhere in the city in addition to the subject property, this use would spread the parking demand out 
across multiple businesses. 
 
Staff believe that this criterion is reasonably met to allow fee-in-lieu to be used for at least a portion of the spaces required.  
 
5. Impact on Public Health, Safety, Welfare (Sec. 21-134(E)(5)) 
 
Criterion Language: Whether the public health, safety, or welfare would be adversely affected by locating the required parking on 
the property.    
 
Analysis: The applicant has plans to activate outdoor spaces surrounding the existing building and is anticipating generating 
additional foot traffic in this area. Any on-site parking would need to ensure that impacts on pedestrians are mitigated. Staff are 
unclear whether on-site parking would have an impact on pedestrians in the area. Another potential impact would be on vehicle 
circulation. Drive lanes in this area are narrow and added parking in this vicinity could have impacts both now and in the future if 
the parking structure is eventually built.

Additional off-street parking could result in impacts to pedestrians or vehicle circulation, though impacts are unknown. Staff 
believe that no public health, safety, or welfare impacts would occur if parking fee-in-lieu is approved.  
 
FINANCIAL CONSIDERATIONS 
• Fee Amount: $20,000 / space 
• Payment Agreement / Plan: Per Sec. 21-134 (F) and (G), an approved applicant for the Parking Fee-in-Lieu program is 
required to enter into a payment agreement with the City, which must be recorded. The ordinance also empowers the City 
Administrator to authorize a payment plan at their discretion. This plan may include annual installments over a period not 
exceeding ten years, and may encompass interest and other provisions, such as potential termination of the Certificate of 
Occupancy for nonpayment. This payment agreement will be determined collaboratively by the applicant and staff. 
 
If all 16 spaces were approved for fee-in-lieu, the fee value would be $320,000.  
 
RECOMMENDATION 
This application appears to conform to a majority of the criteria for approval of a Parking Fee-in-Lieu application. As such, staff 
recommend approval of the subject application for the CCCAE arts and education center at 121 15th Avenue. Staff want to note that 
the request to approve a fee-in-lieu of all 16 required spaces is substantial. Analysis shows that at most, likely less than half of the 
required spaces could be physically constructed on the property. 
 
Motion to (approve/deny) the application for the Voluntary Fee-in-Lieu Parking Program for the CCCAE project to be located at 
121 15th Avenue, in the amount of $320,000.

PURPOSE OF THE COMPREHENSIVE PLAN 
The Colorado Revised Statutes give the authority and responsibility to municipalities to “make and adopt a master plan for the 
physical development of the municipality” (C.R.S. 31 -23-206). The statute further states: “The Plan shall be made with the 
general purpose of guiding and accomplishing a coordinated, adjusted and harmonious development of the municipality and 
its environs which will, in accordance with present and future needs, best promote health, safety, order, convenience, 
prosperity and general welfare.” (C.R.S. 31-23-207). 
The Comprehensive Plan is a long-range plan that looks into the future, using the past as a guide. It articulates a vision for the 
future of the City of Idaho Springs and provides the goals and actions to achieve that vision. It is a policy document that provides 
guidance to City Council, Planning Commission, City Staff, residents, businesses, and dev elopers to facilitate informed 
decisions about the current and future needs of the community. It is a valuable tool developed to: 
  
• Promote the community’s vision for the future of Idaho Springs; 
• Be a guide for consistent decision making;  
• Maintain and improve the physical environment of the City;  
• Be used as the basis for more specific rules, regulations, and ordinances that implement the policies expressed 
through the Comprehensive Plan;  
• Identify and set budgeting priorities; 
• Be used as the basis for future capital improvements, City projects, and evaluation of annexations and 
development proposals;  
• Plan in a thoughtful, cohesive, and comprehensive manner;  
• Protect the health, safety, and general welfare of the citizens in the community; and, 
• Address both current and long-term needs.

GUIDING PRINCIPLES 
People guide this Plan – current and future residents, business owners, visitors. Regardless of what your relationship with the 
city is, this plan is meant to address your needs and wants for the community.  Not everything in this Plan will address 
everyone’s needs but this plan attempts to be respectful of different opinions to avoid leaving anyone out . Inevitably not 
everyone will agree on the best idea on a topic, but this plan intends on providing a framework by which different opinions and 
ideas can be thoughtfully considered.  
Ultimately this plan is a visionary document and is made with a positive look to the future. Where existing regulations no longer 
serve the needs of the community, the plan seeks to change those over time. Where priorities have shifted, this plan is written 
to shift with them. However, at its core, the goal is to serve people. We intend to serve people by accommodating their

Land Use and Community Character 
There have been significant changes in the city since the adoption of the 2017 Comprehensive Plan. One of the most notable 
changes has been the opening of Virginia Canyon Mountain Park and the impact it has had on East End businesses. This has 
shifted business from being primarily geared toward the automotive industry to a destination for hikers and mountain bikers 
alike. In the Exit 240 Gateway Area, the City  has annexed critical parcels of land into City Limits. Some of these include the 
Idaho Springs Cemetery and the site of future affordable senior housing. 
One of the objectives of the 2017 Plan was to beautify, preserve, and activate the Historic District. In 2024, City Staff and  the 
Historic Preservation Review Commission (HPRC) updated the Historic District design guidelines, aimed at preserving the 
Historic District while achieving beautification and activation goals. Additionally, the City  has worked diligently to improve 
signage throughout Idaho Springs, including better wayfinding for key attractions such as the Charlie Tayler Water Wheel and 
Clear Creek Greenway. Furthermore, through the adoption of the City’s Managed Parking Program, we  have maximized the 
amount of paid parking spaces surrounding the Historic District, allowing us to close the Historic District to vehicle traffi c

Another objective of the 2017 Plan was to upgrade the appearance of Idaho Springs as viewed from I -70. One of the most 
significant ways this has been accomplished is the commitment that new developments with considerable visibility from I-70

Housing

HISTORIC PERSPECTIVE 
Long before white settlers took their first steps along Soda Creek, the Ute and Arapaho nations considered the hot springs as  
sacred ground where they could worship and bring their sick and wounded for healing.  By the time white settlers arrived 
looking for gold in the mid-1800s, Soda Creek was considered the dividing line between the Ute and Arapaho nations and had 
long been treated as neutral ground, dating back as far as the 1300s to 1600s.  
During the winter of 1858-59, George Jackson set up camp at the point where Chicago Creek and Clear Creek met. It was here 
that Mr. Jackson discovered gold on January 7, 1859, and helped spark the Colorado Gold Rush. Within a matter of months 
following Mr. Jackson’s discovery, thousands flocked to the region, bringing the first major white populations into the area 
and settling into the cities of Denver, Boulder, Central City and Idaho Springs. By 1861, the region was no longer considered  
part of the Kansas  Territory but the new territory of Colorado. The population of the area now known as Clear Creek County 
quickly grew to more than 60,000 people. When the Gold Rush ended, the territory continued to prosper in part because of the 
railroads built for the transport of gold.  
It is easy to see how Idaho Springs grew around the mines and followed the contours of the land. Industry developed along 
Clear Creek, homes and narrow streets sprang up at the base of the steep inclines and the residents built a host of churches and 
shops. In the middle of this, the Miner Street commercial district grew into the cornerstone of Idaho Springs’ historic 
landscape. All these elements - the topography, the mines, the homes, the shops, the churches and the creek - have survived 
relatively intact and, when viewed as a whole, afford modern day Coloradoans a rare insight into the history not just of Colorado 
but of development in the western United States. 
Mining brought railroads, wagon roads and commerce to the new city. With the advent of the 20th century, the number of 
mines and active claims began to shrink as the easily mined ores played out . The decline in precious mineral mining and 
population continued through the first half of the 20th century, and most of the mines had closed down by the end of World 
War II. Many of the towns created by the miners of the 1800s have disappeared or become ghost towns. Idaho Springs, widely 
regarded as one of the first sites of a major Colorado gold strike, is among the handful of western gold rush towns that have  
survived to the  present day. Unlike many of the others, however, Idaho Springs has not just survived but continues to be a 
vibrant community with a thriving downtown, steady population and a strong economy.

associated with mining and tourism. First, the construction of U.S. Highways 6 and 40 started the expansion of automobile

It also shortened day travel time from the Denver metro area to mountain communities across western Colorado.

Economy 
The Idaho Springs economy is regional in nature and is shaped by the metro area economy and that of the central mountain 
area. The economy is mostly based on tourism and outdoor recreation. Rafting revenues continue to grow,  and the recently 
opened Virginia Canyon Mountain Park (VCMP) has brought more visitors to the city, along with their dollars. The Mighty Argo 
Cable Car (MACC), completed in 2026, is expected to grow the city’s tourism base further.  
Sales tax receipts account for over 50% of the Idaho Springs General Fund. Since  2015, Idaho Springs has seen a consistent

What is the Difference between Future Land Use and Zoning?  
Future Land Use: serves as a community’s guide and framework for future planning and development decisions. The Plan 
illustrates general land use concepts and categories (e.g. residential, commercial, industrial, open space, etc.) but is flex ible 
enough to allow for other land uses to be considered if they meet other community priorities and complement existing land 
uses in the surrounding area.   
Zoning: specific, parcel-based regulations on land uses, density and intensity, types of development and other urban design 
or architectural regulations. Local regulations establish zone districts which may include multiple uses as identified within the 
future land use plan. Zoning boundaries are precise and typically follow parcel boundaries. See the table below as an example,

Community Character and its Role in Development 
Community character is a commonly discussed concept. We are seeing an increased interest in construction and development 
as the city becomes a regional destination for business and economic growth due to its proximity to I -70 and its connections 
to the Fro nt Range to the east and rural resort communities  further west. At the same time, Idaho Springs has established 
historic neighborhoods that feature walkability, connections to neighbors, and a sense of community that we do not want to 
lose. There are increasing concerns about traffic congestion, increasing tourism and visitation numbers, and a loss of character 
associated with new development that is of a different architectural design, scale, or mass as compared to what exists in the  
city today. The city hears a growing concern that the Idaho Springs peo ple moved to is no longer the Idaho Springs that they 
are living in today. 
With that in mind, community character is a key focus of any growth or development- related conversation about the city’s 
future. While the city does not want to discourage growth or development, we need to be careful that development 
complements and builds  upon the existing built environment that people know and enjoy. The preservation of community 
character can be an effective way to ensure that future developments work with – rather than against – the things that make 
Idaho Springs special. Future land us e decisions and code amendments should consider an analysis of the city’s existing 
makeup to ensure that future development works within the city’s core tenets and priorities to build upon the things that are 
already here. Rather than discouraging growth o r development, the city wants to encourage appropriate development that 
allows the city to remain a quality place to live, work, recreate, and visit for years to come.

Plan will lay out the key land use principles that should guide future development in Idaho Springs that build off those four  
general principles that guide all sections of the Plan.

Architectural Design Principles 
To ensure that future development decisions consider community character, this Plan establishes the following land use 
guiding principles: 
• Architectural design and building scale and massing that complement the existing historic downtown and 
neighborhoods are important to ensure Idaho Springs remains a healthy community.  
• Sustainability and environmental considerations are key to a healthy community – now and into the future.  
• A human-centric scale to development is critical to ensure that the city continues to cater to the people who make up 
the community.  
• The city wants to avoid a cookie cutter development style that could be found anywhere in the U.S. We want 
development that evokes “Idaho Springs”. 
The city has several characteristic architectural types for both commercial and residential structures. Most residential 
structures in Idaho Springs were built in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. New developments in proximity to historic 
areas of the city should complement existing architectural design and existing neighborhood mass and scale. Compatibility is 
not about copying historic architecture or ornamentation. Rather, it is about maintaining consistent proportion and massing, 
focusing on providing similar roof pitches, window proportions, vertical versus horizontal emphasis, and other com mon 
architectural forms that allow developers to construct things that are of a modern time while still fitting in with the city’ s 
historic neighborhoods. 
Development in and adjacent to residential areas are encouraged to incorporate similar architectural design to fit into existing 
neighborhoods. Commercial and multifamily buildings should be designed to avoid large, unadorned, featureless façades. 
Design should include a variety of architectural elements and consider the existing architecture of the area and complement 
features from the relevant architectural types found in the area. 
The Idaho Springs downtown area and historic neighborhoods were built on a scale that promotes mixed -use, walkable 
development that allows people to get around the city without a car. Post- war developments in the East End of the city were 
more car-centric but the core of the city’s built environment today was established before cars were readily available to Idaho 
Springs residents and visitors. Future development should consider this human scale to complement the city’s existing built 
environment. The city’s existing streets function as public spaces in many neighborhoods.  
Idaho Springs values development that focuses on sustainability and environmentally friendly designs. Sustainable is not just 
about “green” development. It is also about high-quality development that stands the test of time. Yes, we need housing, but 
we need housing that will last for more than 20 years. Flexibility of design is an important consideration – buildings should be 
able to change uses without needing major reconstruction.

Land Use Categories 
This element establishes the following use categories within the City of Idaho Springs. These are broad categories meant to 
guide development towards the community’s goals and objectives for the future of the city, and will be considered when 
making land use decisions on topics such as rezonings, final development plans, and other new development projects: 
1. MIXED DENSITY RESIDENTIAL:  
These are areas most appropriate for single family residential development and low -density multifamily development. 
Future development in these areas should be respectful of surrounding housing types, densities, building scale and 
massing, and predominant architecture. Most of the city’s existing single-family residential neighborhoods are designated 
mixed density residential. These neighborhoods are long -established and are made up of small, single -family lots. 
Redevelopment potential is relatively low, as lots are too small to accommodate much additional density. These are also 
areas that do not have adequate infrastructure to support much denser development, characterized by narrow roads, steep 
slopes, or other physical constraints. A developer looking to add density to this area would likely be required to provide 
additional infrastructure to accommodate that increase in density. 
2. HIGHER DENSITY RESIDENTIAL:  
Appropriate for higher density residential use, including multifamily development. These areas are either already higher 
density than the mixed density residential areas or have the infrastructure necessary to support higher densities. These 
areas have wider streets, sidewalks, and access to other necessary infrastructure. Some areas designated as higher density 
residential may only have single-family residences there now but could be redeveloped in the future to include duplexes, 
townhomes, or higher densi ty multifamily housing. Neighborhood -scale commercial developments or mixed -use 
developments could be appropriate in this area if they are community-serving.  
3. TRANSITIONAL MIXED-USE:  
These are areas typically connecting residential areas to commercial areas. Development should maintain or be respectful 
of residential character. Appropriate uses in these areas include single family residential, attached single family and 
townhouses, multifamily development that respects the historic scale, massing, and architecture of the surrounding areas, 
lower intensity commercial uses, civic uses, daycares, hospitality and tourist services, and other similar uses.  Areas 
designated as transitional mi xed use can support a variety of activities and uses but at their core, they still maintain a 
residential feel. The West end of Idaho Springs is designated as transitional mixed use. Currently, most of this area is 
single-family residential, though small -scale commercial development and multifamily housing developments do exist 
today. Redevelopment of th ese areas designated transitional mixed -use may be appropriate but should pay attention to 
and respect existing residential neighborhoods and the architectural scale, massing, and design that characterizes these 
areas.
