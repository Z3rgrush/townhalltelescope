SB 24-174, enacted in 2024, requires Colorado local governments to conduct housing needs 
assessments conforming to statutory requirements and methodology prescribed by the 
Department of Local Affairs (DOLA). This addendum addresses legislative requirements that are 
not addressed elsewhere in the existing Longmont Housing Needs Assessment (2023 HNA), 
which was originally published in 2023. 
A 2025 DOLA courtesy review of the 2023 HNA identified the following areas requiring updates 
for compliance with SB24-174: accessible and visitable unit needs; supportive housing needs; 
displacement risk; updated housing stock and jobs data; homelessness data; growth 
projections forecast; measures of local resources dedicated to the development of affordable 
housing; and market limitations to housing development.
Those components along with a summary of related community engagement, data updates on 
key demographic and market metrics are included in this addendum, with the following 
structure:

Summary of Engagement
Engagement for the Longmont Housing Needs Assessment Addendum draws on efforts from the 
2025-2029 Boulder, Broomfield, Longmont HOME Consortium Consolidated Plan and 2025 
Boulder County Human Services Needs Assessment. Previous engagement provides a foundation 
for understanding local housing conditions, barriers to housing access, displacement pressures, 
housing and service needs, and development constraints. Participants connected housing 
pressures with childcare, transportation, mental healthcare, workforce stability, service-provider 
capacity, and the ability of older adults and people with disabilities to remain in the community. 
Survey responses from Longmont residents also documented displacement, housing 
discrimination, financial insecurity, and concern about unaffordable rent increases. Engagement 
included:
 Stakeholder focus groups and interviews with over 25 participants representing service 
providers, developers, and economic development experts in Longmont.

 85 Longmont residents responded to the 2024 Boulder, Broomfield, Longmont HOME 
Consortium Resident Survey. Questions addressed housing and human service needs and 
barriers. 
 A resident focus group with residents of transitional housing, permanent supportive housing, 
and senior housing in Longmont highlighted circumstances that contributed to housing 
instability, current housing conditions, barriers to long-term stability, and gaps in community 
resources and services.
  A community meeting in Longmont was held in partnership with the Housing and Human 
Services Advisory Board. Participants took part in an interactive presentation and discussion 
about housing needs and policy priorities.
Primary findings from the engagement efforts are summarized below by topic area. 
Housing stability: 
 Transitional housing residents in Longmont described housing instability caused by 
foreclosure, relationship or family changes, the sale of rental properties, insufficient income, 
and a shortage of affordable housing. Some focus group participants remained unable to 
qualify for housing even with case-management support because their incomes were too 
low.  
 Survey respondents in Longmont reported financial insecurity, leaving many residents more 
vulnerable to housing loss: 34% worried about affording an unexpected expense, 31% 
reported living paycheck to paycheck, and 28% said they were financially worse off than the 
prior year.  
 Housing-specific pressures identified by survey respondents included unaffordable rent 
increases, difficulty paying rent or a mortgage, and utility costs. Among Longmont 
respondents, 15% reported experiencing displacement in the last three years. 
Housing conditions:  
 22% of Longmont survey respondents rated their home’s condition as fair or poor. Residents 
also described major repairs and replacement of home systems as threats to household 
savings in open-ended responses.   
 Stakeholders identified funding gaps for home modifications that would help people with 
disabilities and older adults remain in rental or owner-occupied housing. Countywide survey 
findings also indicated unmet accessibility needs, particularly for grab bars and ramps. 
Access to housing: 
 Survey respondents identified barriers to accessing housing (among residents wanting to 
move). Leading barriers were all affordability related: 26% cited inability to afford rent 
elsewhere; 21% cited an inability to afford another home after selling; and 16% cited 
application-fee or security-deposit costs. Among renters wishing to purchase a home, the 
most common barriers were inability to afford a down payment, (29%); inability to qualify for a 
loan (14%); and excessive debt (12%).

 Survey respondents also reported housing discrimination, with 13% of Longmont respondents 
indicating that they had experienced discrimination while looking for housing in the region. 
 People with prior evictions, justice-system involvement, poor credit, and other lease-up 
barriers may qualify for assistance but still struggle to find a property that will accept them. 
Stakeholders identified housing navigation, case management, and advocacy as important 
supports for closing the gap between program eligibility and successful placement.
Work location and commutes:  
 Housing affordability also affects workforce stability. Stakeholders reported that service 
providers and public-sector employers may lose employees who cannot afford to live locally 
and that long commutes can make positions less sustainable. 
 Transportation creates additional access challenges: 26% of Longmont survey respondents 
reported that bus trips take too long to reach their destinations. Focus group participants also 
described unreliable bus schedules. 
 Community meeting participants emphasized recruitment, training, and retention in the 
trades and wanted workforce-development opportunities to remain accessible to people 
without high school diplomas. 
Access to health care: 
 Mental healthcare was identified as an important but difficult-to-access resource by 
respondents in the resident survey. Community meeting and focus group participants also 
identified gaps in substance-use treatment and long-term care. 
 Service providers also emphasized that supportive services and case management are 
necessary alongside housing for people with behavioral health needs and households 
transitioning from homelessness. 
 Survey respondents reported reducing or foregoing mental health appointments, dental care, 
and medical care because of financial pressure, illustrating how healthcare costs can 
compound housing instability. 
Community and belonging: 
 Focus group participants valued Longmont’s cleanliness, bicycle trails, and network of 
community resources. Participants also identified a need for more affordable social gathering 
spaces and recreational opportunities. 
 Stakeholders described Longmont and the broader region as collaborative and effective at 
coordinating resources and referrals. However, underfunding, staffing shortages, turnover, 
administrative burdens, and increasing demand limit the capacity of community-serving 
organizations.  
 Housing costs may weaken community continuity by forcing families and workers to leave 
Longmont for lower-cost communities. Stakeholders connected these moves with school 
enrollment, reduced local spending, workforce losses, and fewer opportunities for 
households to remain in the community as their needs change.

Business stability: 
 Stakeholders connected high housing costs with employee recruitment, retention, and 
turnover. Employers may lose workers who cannot afford to live locally, while longer 
commutes may make employment less sustainable. 
 Stakeholders also observed that residents moving to lower-cost communities can reduce 
Longmont’s labor supply and local spending power, affecting local businesses and the 
broader economy.  
 Service providers reported difficulty retaining employees when wages do not cover local 
housing costs. Chronic underfunding, staffing shortages, administrative burdens, and rising 
demand further constrain the stability and capacity of these community-serving 
organizations. 
Cost Burden & Homelessness
Cost Burden. Housing is generally considered to be affordable to a household when monthly 
housing costs consume less than 30% of a household’s gross income. Households who pay more 
than 30% of income for housing costs are considered cost burdened, with “moderately” cost 
burdened households paying 30-49% of income for housing and “severely” cost burdened 
households paying 50% or more of income for housing. Severely cost burdened households face 
an elevated risk of eviction, foreclosure, and homelessness.
As shown in Figure 16, nearly half (49%) of Longmont’s renter households and 21% of Longmont 
homeowner households pay more than they can afford for housing. Nearly a quarter of the town’s 
renters (24%) are severely cost burdened, compared to 6% of the town’s homeowners.

Homelessness. The 2026 Point-in-Time (PIT) Count, an annual count of individuals 
experiencing sheltered and unsheltered homelessness on a single night in January, counted 168 
individuals experiencing homelessness in Longmont. Of the 168 people counted, 66% (111 people) 
were sheltered and 44% (57 people) were unsheltered. Note that PIT counts only include 
individuals whose living situation meets the federal definition of homelessness,1 which excludes 
individuals who are doubled up or in a temporary and informal living arrangement.
The majority (113) of the people in the 2026 PIT count were adult individuals; 50 were in families 
(e.g., parent and child), and 5 were unaccompanied youth. Sixty nine people were experiencing 
chronic homelessness (defined as those who have been homeless and living in a place not meant 
for human habitation, a safe haven or an emergency shelter continuously for at least 1 year or on 
at least four separate occasions in the last 3 years where the combined length of time homeless 
across those occasions is at least 12 months).
1 As defined by 24 CFR 578.3, “homeless” means “an individual or family who lacks a fixed, regular, and adequate nighttime 
residence.” This definition includes those with a primary nighttime residence in a “public or private place not designed for or 
ordinarily used as a regular sleeping accommodation for human beings” or in a “supervised publicly or privately operated shelter 
designated to provide temporary living arrangements”. It also includes individuals exiting an institution where they resided for 90 
days or less provided that they resided in “an emergency shelter or place not meant for human habitation immediately before 
entering that institution.” More information available here: https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/24/578.3.

The McKinney-Vento Homeless Assistance Act governs the education of youth experiencing 
homelessness. It uses a broader definition of homelessness than the federal definition, considering 
children homeless when they “lack a fixed, regular, and adequate nighttime residence.” McKinney-
Vento data provides counts of children experiencing homelessness in the St. Vrain REIJ school 
district, enabling a more comprehensive discussion of homelessness in Longmont. It should be 
noted that the St. Vrain REIJ school district extends beyond Longmont into Lyons, Niwot, Erie, 
Firestone, and a small portion of Broomfield. Reports from McKinney-Vento data will reflect 
homelessness in those areas as well as Longmont. 
In the 2024-2025 academic year, 673 students were experiencing homelessness (2% of the district’s 
32,279 students). Most (337) of the students experiencing homelessness were in a doubled up living 
arrangement, 269 were housed in emergency shelters or transitional housing, 43 were housed in 
hotels or motels, and 24 were unsheltered.

Accessible and Supportive Housing 
Accessible units needed. As data on the number of accessible housing units in Longmont 
are not available, this discussion focuses on future needs for accessible units. According to HUD 
data, 8,920 households in Longmont or 22% of the town’s households have at least one member 
with a disability. Based on this rate, 22% of new housing units should be accessible for residents 
with disabilities. 
Accessibility needs vary by disability type. Figure 20 presents prevalence rates of disabilities by type 
among households in Longmont. Housing needs of households containing a member with a 
disability vary by disability type. For example:
 Households that include someone with hearing or vision difficulties may need smoke detectors 
with visual strobe lights or handrails and grab bars;
 Households that include someone with ambulatory difficulties may need units without stairs, 
handrails and grab bars, and wheelchair accessible doors; and
 Households that include someone with cognitive difficulties and self-care or independent living 
difficulty may need homes with built-in access to supportive services and proximity to 
community resources.

Supportive units needed. In addition to supportive housing environments needed by some 
households with cognitive, self-care, or independent living difficulties, supportive housing units are 
needed by households experiencing homelessness. According to PIT counts in Longmont, the 
population experiencing homelessness included 69 people experiencing chronic homelessness. 
Chronically homeless residents were more likely to be unsheltered (46 people) than sheltered in 
an emergency shelter (23 people). No people experiencing chronic homelessness were sheltered 
in transitional housing, a type of shelter that provides longer-term temporary housing with 
supportive services. Transitional housing is considered a better resource than emergency shelters 
for people experiencing chronic homelessness because it provides interim stability as residents 
seek to move to permanent housing. Based on the most recent available count of residents 
experiencing chronic homelessness, Longmont needs to add 69 transitional housing units.

demand (and gaps), their experiences producing housing in the area, and strategic opportunities

and solutions to address barriers to housing production—especially at the most needed price-

Generally, participants viewed Longmont’s staff accessibility, planning framework, and willingness 
to collaborate as strengths. However, they emphasized that cumulative costs, some development 
standards and requirements (e.g., Inclusionary Housing requirements), lengthy approvals, and 
market uncertainty can combine to make projects financially infeasible. These challenges are 
particularly acute for smaller ownership units, townhomes, duplexes, micro-units, and other 
missing-middle products intended to serve moderate-income households.  
Meeting participants identified several interrelated barriers limiting new housing development—
particularly affordable, attainable, and “missing middle” housing—in Longmont.
Market-driven barriers: 
 Construction costs have risen substantially for labor, materials, fuel, delivery, infrastructure, and 
financing. Affordable and attainable projects were described as having especially thin margins.
 Water dedication fees were cited as a particularly significant expense. Participants noted that 
Longmont’s raw water fee has increased and is based on the full acreage of annexed land, 
including land used for drainage or other nonresidential purposes.
 Apartment rents have flattened or declined after several years of rapid growth, while 
development and financing costs remain elevated.
 Higher interest rates, market volatility, slower lease-up, concessions, and uncertainty among 
lenders and investors make it harder to secure financing.
 Projects underwritten using earlier assumptions about rent growth may now require additional 
capital.
 Developers are delaying projects and targeting later delivery dates in hopes that market 
conditions improve.
 Construction defects liability (at the state level) continues to limit condo development. 
 Longmont is largely surrounded by open space, leaving relatively few large annexation 
opportunities. Future housing development will increasingly depend on infill and 
redevelopment, which can be more complicated and politically sensitive.
Policy and process constraints:
 Existing lot-size, setback, density, and utility requirements can prevent smaller and more 
affordable housing products. Participants said minimum lot sizes may need to fall closer to 2,000 
square feet to support smaller ownership units.
 Duplexes and paired homes are not permitted in some single-family districts, limiting 
opportunities to add density while retaining a single-family neighborhood form. At the same 
time, some mixed-use districts require minimum densities that are too high for townhomes but 
are better suited to multifamily—this leaves very little feasible zoned area for missing middle 
products.

 Requirements for wide utility corridors, rights-of-way, and infrastructure separation can reduce 
buildable land and make compact development more difficult.
 Affordable housing requirements add complexity and cost to projects. Developers building 
affordable units on site also noted ongoing compliance complexity. 
 Projects can receive new comments during later review rounds rather than comments being 
limited to unresolved issues.
 Approval timeliness add cost and risk to projects. Specific considerations included: 
 Annexations and entitlements can take several years, adding substantial cost and risk.
 Six-week review cycles may restart even after relatively minor revisions.
 Council scheduling, public hearings, and repeated reviews can add months to the 
process.
 Participants said prolonged approval timelines can cause projects to miss favorable 
financing or market windows.
 Some developers also noted specific process concerns with large developments and concept 
plans, including: 
 Developers may be required to specify the location, density, and product type for each 
part of a project before market conditions or project details are known. Even small 
changes—such as adding two units—may trigger a concept plan amendment and 
another approval process. This is particularly challenging for large projects with 10- to 15-
year buildout periods.
 Developers also described inconsistent expectations between city staff and the Planning 
Commission regarding how detailed concept plans should be.
 Developers said vested-rights timelines may not provide enough flexibility for large, 
phased, or delayed projects. Longer vesting periods could allow projects to proceed 
when financing and demand are better aligned.

Current and Future Housing Needs
This section provides up-to-date information on housing units needed in Longmont. It updates the 
affordability gap analysis in the original HNA (from 2023) and adds projected needs as required by 
SB 24-174. 
Affordability needs. Root Policy Research conducted a modeling effort called a gaps analysis 
to examine how well Longmont’s housing market meets the affordability needs of current 
residents. The gaps analysis compares the supply of housing at various price points to the number 
of households who can afford such housing. If there are more housing units than households, the 
market is “oversupplying” housing at that price point. Conversely, if there are too few units, the 
market is “undersupplying” housing at that price point. 
The affordability gaps analysis presented in Section III of the 2023 HNA (Figures III-5 and III-6) 
summarized conditions in 2022. This section uses data from 2024-2025 to approximate current 
conditions in the rental and home purchase markets.
Note that the gaps analysis is intended to evaluate affordability needs among current residents 
not the need for additional housing to accommodate future or potential residents. Gaps identified 
in these analyses could be addressed through the production of new housing units at affordable 
price points or with subsidies on existing units (e.g., Housing Choice Vouchers for rental units or 
downpayment assistance for ownership units).
Rental affordability gap. The rental gaps analysis compares the number of renter households 
by income level and the maximum monthly housing payment they can afford with the number of 
affordable housing units in the market, including income-restricted affordable units. The “Rental 
Mismatch” column in Figure 27 shows the difference between the number of renter households 
and the number of rental units affordable to them at that price point. Negative numbers indicate a 
shortage of units at specific income levels; positive units indicate an excess of housing at that price 
point. Affordability gaps are shown by household AMI ranges published by HUD for a 2-person 
household (in line with the average household size) in Boulder County.

The rental affordability gaps analysis shows that collectively, there is a shortage of 3,041 units for 
renters with incomes below 50% AMI in Longmont. The largest affordability gap is for renters with 
incomes below 30% AMI: 3,636 renters fall within this income range, yet only 1,223 rental units are 
priced below $876 and affordable to them (a gap of 2,413 rental units). Renter households with 
incomes at or below 50% AMI who cannot find units affordable to them due to supply shortages 
are “renting up” into higher priced units, facing cost burden and constraining supply for renters up 
to 80% AMI (as evidenced by the cumulative affordability gap column).
Note that the “shortage” that appears for higher income households (120% AMI or more) does 
theoretically show a mismatch in their ability to pay for higher priced rental units and the lack of 
units at that higher price point. However, it does not necessarily mean they prefer higher priced 
units. Many households in this income range will “rent down” to spend less than 30% of their 
income on housing to save money, potentially for a downpayment to purchase a home. This 
increases competition for naturally affordable rental units, limiting the inventory of units for 
people with lower incomes even more.
Comparing the results of this analysis with the gaps analysis of the 2023 HNA (Figure III-5), the 
cumulative affordability gap has increased by 850 units for households earning 50% AMI and 
under, from 2,173 units in 2021 to 3,041 in 2024. Since 2020, Longmont has increased the overall 
number of rental units, however the average prices of these units remain unaffordable to renters 
below 50% AMI.

Housing production needs. The rental and purchase affordability gaps explained in the 
previous section indicate affordability needs, which may be addressed by constructing new units at 
affordable price points or by providing subsidies—such as vouchers for renter households or 
downpayment assistance for home purchases—to households at affected income levels. This 
production needs analysis estimates new housing units needed based on overcrowding and low 
vacancy rates—challenges which cannot be addressed by providing subsidies for existing units—
and future growth. 
Production needs, referred to here as current “catch-up needs” and projected “keep-up needs,” are 
estimated with methodology designed by Colorado’s Department of Local Affairs (DOLA) to 
quantify units needed to ensure housing availability and stability through 2035. Specifically: 
 Current “catch-up” units needed are the additional housing units needed to reach a rental 
vacancy rate of 5% and a homeowner vacancy rate of 2% and to alleviate overcrowding 
(defined by HUD as more than one person per room). 
 Projected need identifies “keep-up” units needed to ensure housing availability and stability 
for future resident and employee households based on state demographer projections of 
growth over the next ten years, with an accommodation for vacancy allowances (2% on owner 
units and 5% on rental units). 
Keeping with DOLA guidance and data availability for household and job projections, catch-up and 
keep-up needs are first calculated at the county level and then distributed to Longmont. Figure 30 
shows the state demographer’s forecasts of population and households for both Boulder County 
and Weld County through 2034. Ninety eight percent of Longmont households are located in 
Boulder County; as such, Boulder County projections are used as the drive of growth in Longmont.

 The additional units needed to alleviate overcrowding are estimated at a rate of one new 
housing unit for every two overcrowded units. ACS data identify 893 overcrowded units in 
Longmont so 447 units are needed to meet demand from overcrowding. Overcrowding is

others to manage living expenses on very low incomes. Units needed to alleviate 
overcrowding are therefore assumed to be most needed where the rental market currently 
under-provides units—primarily 0-30% AMI, according to the rental gaps analysis—so all units

 In total, Longmont needs to add 917 housing units (including 293 owner occupied units and 
624 renter occupied units) to alleviate both low residential vacancy and overcrowding for its 
current resident households. 
Projected “keep up” needs in Longmont. DOLA’s Housing Needs Assessment Methodology 
requires that projected housing needs are allocated by jurisdiction with consideration of 
employment factors (not just current household representation). The total projected rental and 
ownership units needed in Boulder County are distributed to Longmont according to a weighted 
measure which equally reflects the share of the county’s current jobs that are located in 
Longmont, the share of the county’s current households that live in Longmont, and the proportion 
of the county’s household growth since 2018 that occurred in Longmont. Specifically,
 According to Longitudinal Employer-Household Dynamics data, there are about 156,745 jobs 
in Boulder County, 23% of which are in Longmont.
 Approximately 30% of Boulder County’s current households live in Longmont according to 
ACS estimates. This factor helps account for location preference among workers.
 As discussed previously, household growth in Longmont far outpaced the growth rate in the 
County, meaning household growth in Longmont is a key driver of the county’s growth 
overall. Approximately 59% of Boulder County’s’ household growth since 2018 occurred in 
Longmont. 
 Combining these factors, approximately 38% of the County’s projected housing units 
needed are allocated to Longmont. This allocation means Longmont would need to add 3,362 
new housing units to accommodate forecasted household and job growth in the City.

Recommendations. The following recommendations are designed to provide a balanced 
approach to help the City address identified housing needs. Specific actions and tools will be 
identified and refined through development of the Housing Action Plan (to be completed by 
January 2028).  
Strategies are most effective when actions are tailored to the public sector’s sphere of influence 
and role in addressing housing needs.  An integrated approach that leverages key partners and 
financial assets (both land and funds) to create housing that serves current and future residents, 
preserve existing affordability; and assist low-income households will have the most success.
1. Continue regional collaboration and strategic partnerships. Boulder County is 
considered a national model for its regional collaboration through the Boulder County 
Regional Housing Partnership and Home Wanted efforts. Continuing such partnerships and 
collaborative efforts toward achieving the shared goal of 12% of the region’s housing 
affordable to low, moderate, and middle-income households by 2035 is critical to the success 
of housing programs in both Longmont and surrounding communities. 
As has been discussed, Longmont has a strong network of affordable housing providers and 
non-profit service providers. These entities are essential to effective service delivery and 
affordable production and provide a wealth of expertise in strategically addressing needs. 
However, the demand for services outstrips funding and capacity. 
Public-private partnerships are also important to the success of delivering affordable housing. 
Common public-private partnerships typically center around public investment in development 
projects for affordability but additional opportunities to create/improve efficiencies in housing 
delivery may exist with the business community and economic development agencies.
2. Build on recent success with continued review of development barriers to improve 
housing diversity and attainability. The City has successfully unlocked recent multifamily 
development to help address historical undersupply in the rental market. Additionally, the City 
has improved development processes, updated design standards, and adjusted policies on 
accessory dwelling units and short term rentals. The City should continue to monitor the 
efficacy of recent changes and evaluate new opportunities to reduce development barriers, 
particularly in ways that improve construction efficiencies for missing middle and innovative 
housing types that can be delivered at attainable price points. 
3. Increase the supply of income qualified affordable housing. The City continues to make 
progress toward the housing goal of 5,400 affordable units by 2035, but affordability needs 
persist—particularly for renters earning less than 50% AMI and for potential buyers

earning less than 100% AMI. Continued affordable production is critical and the City plays a key 
role in facilitating such production through gap financing, supporting LIHTC and other 
affordable developments, as well as creating zoning incentives to produce affordable housing. 
Key opportunities for the City in the current market and policy context are considering 
additional incentives for affordable and mixed income housing production, leveraging publicly 
owned land for affordable development, and continued investment in affordable projects. The 
City is also working to evaluate its inclusionary housing program to ensure it delivers desired 
outcomes as part of the City’s overall approach to housing affordability.
4. Protect existing affordable housing through preservation policies/programs. This 
strategy focuses on preservation of housing that is already serving low and moderate income 
households. There are two different submarkets of properties serving low- and moderate- 
income households: income restricted housing and naturally occurring affordable housing (or 
“NOAH”). NOAH generally reflects older apartments, smaller and attached for-sale homes 
(sometimes in poor condition), and/or mobile/manufactured housing. Preservation efforts are 
needed when affordability contracts on income-restricted units are set to expire, when NOAH 
is under market pressures to raise rents/prices above what low income households can afford; 
and/or when NOAH is for sale (especially to corporate investors). 
Preservation policies and programs can include: 
 Acquisition and rehab of aging multifamily or expiring affordable contracts (public sector 
role is typically just in funding non-profit acquisition/rehab); 
 Capital investments in exchange for temporary rent escalation caps; 
 Mobile/manufactured home park supports (from infrastructure grants to financial 
support of resident acquisition; and
 Anti-displacement and/or tenant protection programs.  
5. Diversify funding sources to support affordable housing production, preservation, and 
service delivery. The City already has a local, dedicated funding source for housing; however 
the primary source for such funding is through the City’s inclusionary housing ordinance and is 
therefore reliant on new development to generate resources. In order to create reliable 
funding for housing investments, the City may want to consider alternative sources less 
dependent on development activity (e.g., GO Bonds, sales taxes, property taxes, lodging taxes, 
General Fund set asides, etc.).  
Local funds are particularly effective for affordable housing projects because they provide a 
sustainable and flexible funding source without federal or state regulations. Fee revenue can 
be used for gap financing, land banking, development incentives or subsidies (such as fee or 
tax rebates), and/or direct services such as eviction prevention, rental assistance, home repair, 
etc.. Dedicated funds—as opposed to discretionary allocations—have the added benefit of 
dependable future revenue, which protects funding from shifting politics AND allows a 
City/County to bond against the revenue, which can support up-front costs of major housing 
investments. 
6. Invest in housing stabilization through direct support of housing programs and 
services for vulnerable and special needs populations as well as homeless response and

Down Payment Assistance Program Request
The City’s Down Payment Assistance (DPA) Program combines three sources of funding to 
help income-qualified households attain homeownership. The federally funded HOME DPA 
program operates countywide outside the City of Boulder and serves households under 80% 
of Area Median Income (AMI). The most recent closings in this program were in 2024, and 
those four closings exhausted the available funds, so no additional closings are possible for 
the foreseeable future until program income is received from current homes selling. In 2025, 
Council dedicated $500,000 for a pilot program using the City’s 2024 and 2025 Affordable 
Housing Fund (AHF) to expand opportunities for households under 80% AMI. The City was 
also successful in obtaining a $750,000 grant from the State’s Prop 123 funding programs to 
serve households with DPA up to 120% AMI. Since 2025, the City has spent $404,569 within 
the AHF DPA Program, and $559,980 within the Prop 123 DPA Program. In total, the City has 
served 10 households below 80% AMI and 13 households below 120% AMI since the program 
expansion occurred in mid-2025.
The program has been not only popular but has been absolutely necessary for 80% of True 
North’s buyers thus far. If more funding is not secured, True North buyers could face 
challenges in closing on their homes as early as January 2027.  Additionally, because Habitat 
for Humanity’s model of providing 0% interest mortgages has become financially 
unsustainable, Habitat buyers, who have historically not needed to rely on DPA, are facing a 
need into the future.
Staff has recently submitted an application to the State for another round of $750,000 in 
funds to support the Prop 123 DPA program into the future. In order to support a local match 
for that request and expand services to Habitat buyers, staff requests to repurpose $275,000 
in AHF funds to support DPA programs into 2027. We anticipate approximately 30 additional 
households would be served between now and the end of 2027 with the new set-aside of 
AHF funds and a new Prop 123 grant. The average DPA grant is $37,000, with funds used to 
assist buyers with their down payment and closing costs. Staff will likely include a request for 
additional support for the program mid-year as part of the 2027 Action Plan to extend 
capacity into 2028. HCI Staff has also reached out to other DPA providers to build a network 
to bridge the gap in assistance needed for 2027 and worked with lenders that provide grants 
to supplement the costs of purchasing a home.

Mountain View Mobile Home Park Request
In September 2026, the approximately 80 senior residents of the Mountain View Mobile 
Home Park received a Notice of Intent to sell the park from its current owner. Residents have 
120 days to organize into a group or association and submit an offer and purchase and sale 
agreement if they choose to pursue purchase of the park. This deadline will come in mid-
January, but the timeline can be paused in some instances. If no offer is provided, or other 
applicable method of extending the statutory deadlines is implemented by that time, then 
the park owner will likely list the property for sale. The stated desired purchase price is $17 
million.
Staff has been meeting with representatives from the park’s resident group, Thistle Resident-
Owned Communities (ROC), the State’s Division of Housing (DOH), Boulder County, and 
Impact Development Fund to explore options and provide technical assistance. At this time, 
in order to help determine the feasibility of a resident-purchase option, the resident group 
needs funding support to work with Thistle ROC, perform an appraisal and a capital needs 
assessment, and obtain legal services. There are groups in the state that do provide pro-bono 
legal services. Thistle ROC is also willing to front-fund their technical assistance services to be 
reimbursed at closing if a purchase is deemed feasible. They often work with resident groups 
in advance of that determination if a local agency is willing to provide funding support until 
the point that the purchase is deemed feasible. Thistle ROC has connections with appraisers 
and firms that complete capital needs assessments suitable for this type of transaction. 
Therefore, it would be most beneficial at this time if the City were to support the residents to 
get far enough through the process to determine feasibility of a purchase. Boulder County 
recently provided $42,000 in assistance to the San Lazaro Mobile Home Park to engage 
Thistle ROC to get to a feasibility determination. Since Thistle ROC’s cost model is based on a 
per unit calculation, and appropriate amount for the Mountain View Mobile Home Park 
would be $30,000. Staff proposes to provide a grant to the resident association for the 
purpose of engaging Thistle ROC for technical assistance (or engaging directly with Thistle 
ROC to allocate the funds, if most efficient).  If approved, staff would engage with the 
resident association and Thistle-ROC for a predevelopment services grant.
