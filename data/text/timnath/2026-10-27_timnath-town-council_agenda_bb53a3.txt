3. PUBLIC COMMENT 
 Individuals may comment regarding any topics of concern, whether or not included on 
this agenda.  
 Those who wish to speak are required to sign up at the table outside of the Council 
Chamber doors before the start of the meeting.  
 Each speaker will be allowed to speak one time during public comment for general and 
non-public hearing agenda items.  General public comment should not be used to 
speak on an item that has a public hearing. 
 Each speaker may only speak on each topic one time during a meeting. If there are 
two items scheduled for a public hearing, they should sign up for both items. 
 All speakers will be called to speak by the presiding officer from the list of those signed 
up.  
 Each speaker will be asked to state their name and general address for the record.  
Any speaker providing written comments or materials intended for the Council should 
be provided to the Town Clerk.  
 Any written comments received 24 hours before the meeting will be provided to 
Council, included in the minutes but not read aloud. 
 A timer at the podium will light up green and turn yellow to indicate that 30 seconds of 
speaking time remain.  The light will turn red when a speaker’s time has ended.  
 Each speaker will receive 3 minutes for their comments, unless otherwise indicated. 
 
 
4. CONSENT AGENDA 
 Use of the consent agenda allows Council to spend its time for items requiring more 
discussion or for public hearings. 
 The consent agenda is used to approve several action items as a group with one vote.  
 Any council member may request that an item be removed from the consent agenda 
for further discussion.

Dear Mayor and Town Council Members,
lam writing to express my serious concerns regarding Verdad Resources’ proposed Century Pad,
a 22—welloil and gas development located approximately one mile east of Timnath homes.
Although the proposed surface site is in unincorporated Weld County, its potential impacts
extend beyond county boundaries and directly affect the Timnath community.
I am particularly disappointed by the Town of Timnath’s lack of representation during this
permitting process. I attended the October 1, 2026, Weld County hearing regarding Verdad’s
Century Pad 1041 WOGLA permit. During that hearing, county representatives acknowledged
that the Town of Timnath had been noti?ed of the proposed project, but had not submitted a
letter expressing concerns and had no representative present at the hearing.
This project raises legitimate concerns about public health, safety, and quality of life. Oil and gas
operations can generate air emissions, noise, dust, and light pollution. An accident or release
could create additional risks, depending on its nature and scale. These are not merely
hypothetical concerns about an industry without a history of incidents. According to publicly
available information compiled by community advocates from regulatory records, Verdad
Resources has had 35 reported oil spills at its Colorado locations over the past 10 years,
including three in the 12 months covered by their reportA.These incidents do not establish that
a similar event would occur at Century Pad, but they underscore why residents deserve a
careful, transparent assessment of potential risks and emergency preparedness.
Traf?c safety is another signi?cant concern. The development will bring substantial truck traf?c
associated with drilling, completion, and ongoing operations. Residents traveling east on
Harmony Road and using nearby intersections could face additional interactions with large
industrial vehicles, including trucks transporting oil?eld equipment and materials. The Town
should seek clear information about projected truck volumes, routes, hours of operation,
hazardous-material transportation, and measures to protect residents using these roads.
The project also proposes horizontal wellbores extending west from the surface location into a
development area that includes land in Larimer County. This means the project’s footprint is not
limited to the visible surface pad. I urge the Town to review the proposed wellbore and
development maps and determine which Timnath neighborhoods and properties may be
affected, as it is my understanding that most home south of Harmony Road will be affected by
this. Residents deserve clear information about the location of the proposed wellbores and any
relevant risks, rather than being left to interpret technical permitting documents themselves.
My greatest concern, however, is that many residents may not even know this project is being
proposed near their community. Verdad’s formal noti?cation requirements have not
necessarily reached every household that could be concerned about the project. While I
Page 10 of 234
recognize that the Town may not control Weld County’s permitting process, it can help ensure
residents have the information they need to understand a major industrial development near
their homes.
The Town routinely communicates about road closures, construction, and other conditions that
affect residents, including matters that may involve outside agencies. I believe a proposed 22—
well oil and gas development of this scale warrants at least the same level of public awareness.
I respectfully ask the Town Council to:
1. Notify Timnath residents about the proposed Century Pad and its ongoing state
permitting process through the Town’s website, social media, and other available
communication channels.
2. Provide reliable information and resources so residents can review the project,
understand the potential impacts, and learn how to submit public comments or
participate in future proceedings.
3. Engage with the appropriate county and state agencies regarding traf?c safety, air
quality, emergency response, and the potential effects on nearby neighborhoods and
schools.
4. Keep residents informed of future permitting milestones and opportunities for public
input, rather than leaving individuals to discover these proceedings through their own
research or word of mouth.
lam not asking the Town to make decisions outside its jurisdiction. I am asking it to use the
voice and communication channels it does have to protect the interests of its residents and
ensure our community is not overlooked in a decision with potentially long—lasting
consequences.
Timnath families deserve transparency, meaningful communication, and the opportunity to
understand and respond to major industrial development near their homes. I urge the Town
Council to take these concerns seriously and act now to ensure residents are informed and
represented.
Thank you for your time and consideration.

Introduction and our request
Submitted by: David L. Rumley
Good evening, members of the board. My name is David Rumley. I'm an attorney
representing several Timnath Elementary families. I‘m also a Timnath parent, and my wife is
a Timnath Elementary graduate. Thank you for your time tonight.
We‘re asking for two things on October 27. First, vote on Timnath Elementary as its own
motion, separate from the other recommendations. Second, vote to keep it open.
This process was started to address declining enrollment. Timnath is not declining. It is 95%
full, its enrollment is projected to stay stable, and it serves one of the fastest—growingtowns in
Northern Colorado. It is on the list because of a building condition score. The speakers after
me will show how that score contradicts the district‘s own data. Each school deserves to be
judged on its own facts, and a separate vote lets you do that.
With my remaining time, I want to talk about the process.
The 25 committee members who voted on our region did not include a single Timnath
Elementary administrator. And the committee's only parent from the Timnath area, its co-
chair, lives in the Bethke Elementary area; living in the Timnath area is not the same as being
a Timnath Elementary family. Why is that significant?
The committee's very first assignment, before its first meeting in April 2025, was to review its
charter, established by district leadership, including the superintendent. That charter says that
"staff and families from schools potentially impacted by proposed changes must be offered
the opportunity to weigh in on the committee‘s work before recommendations are finalized."
It also says that when the committee begins discussing changes to a school, that school's
leaders "will be invited to engage.“
These are not suggestions. The charter uses mandatory terms such as "must" and "will." When
its authors meant something to be optional, like listening sessions, they wrote "may."
But when it came to schools facing change, they chose mandatory words, and they used them
every single time.
And just as deliberately, the charter's authors chose the word "before," not "after." Being
heard after a recommendation is final contradicts the express mandatory language of the
charter. There is nothing ambiguous about that.

How the vote was counted
Submitted on behalf of Timnath Elementary families
Thank you, members of the board.
I want to talk about how the committee reached its recommendation for our region, using the
committee's own minutes.
The board packet tells you the committee "achieved consensus alignment (89%) on the final
recommendation.“ The minutes show no vote at 89%. They show three regional votes: 100%,
96%, and for the Southeast, the one that closes Timnath, 72%. The average of those three is
89%. The number in your packet blends our region‘s narrow result with two others, and it
hides it.
The committee also chose its own bar. In August, the minutes describe the consensus range as
"70 to 90%," and members had already seen votes come in at 73% and 52%. Then, on
September 14, at the same meeting where the minutes record a "temperature check" on the
Southeast, the bar was set at 70%, the very bottom of the range. The minutes don't say who
proposed it or why.
That choice decided our schools fate. At 90%, the Southeast would have failed at both
meetings. It‘s the only region that needed the lower bar. Every other region cleared 90%.
When the bar is set at the bottom, and the only recommendation that needed the bottom is the
one that closes our school, families have every right to ask whether the bar was set to fit the
result.
My question: Who proposed lowering the bar to 70%, and why? And will the board ask staff
to correct the 89% so you can see the Southeast's actual 72%?

The timeline moved up
Submitted on behalfof Timnath Elementary families
Thank you, board members.
The committee‘s minutes from August and October 2025 lay out a roadmap: share data with
the board in January 2026, recommend facility changes in May 2026, and present
recommendations to the board in May 2027.
Instead, the recommendation reached you on October 13, 2026. The minutes never explain
Whythe schedule Changed.
A lot happened as the schedule tightened. In June, members signed a new non—disclosure
agreement expanding confidentiality to closure scenarios. A June 18 email about the June 22
meeting referred to "a z—score explanation we can leverage," two months before z—scores
appear in the committee‘s minutes. Timnath staff tell us that on September 28, three days after
the district's first notice to our school, district HR used a teacher work day to meet with
Timnath‘s teachers about what happens to them when the school closes. That was 15 days
before the board's first reading. The district told Timnath‘s 5- to lO-year-olds, in a Video
shown to every student on September 29, that if the closure moves forward, they "will have
access to more resources, programs, and opportunities in their new school." And next year's
school budgets won't be built until February, so the board is voting before the net savings can
be checked.
A faster process isn‘t wrong in itself. But when it shortens the time for community input and
for checking numbers like an unexplained $2 million, the board should know why.
My question: Who decided to move up the timeline, and why?

The building's condition
Submitted on behalf of Timnath Elementary families
Thank you, board members.
Timnath is on the Closure list because the closure ranking scored its building condition the
worst in the district. Records the district produced through a public records request tell a
different story.
The district‘s own prioritization table rates every school by its facility condition index, which
divides five years of repairs by the cost to replace the building. Timnath‘s index is 0.27. The
median for elementary schools is 0.25. Nine elementary schools rate worse than Timnath.
Seventeen of the other 30 elementary schools need more repair money per square foot.
By the district‘s own measure, Timnath's building is typical, not the worst.
The Closureranking used a different "FCA score," and the board packet never explains how it
was calculated. That score doesn‘t track the district's own repair cost data.
When the one measure that put a school on the closure list can't be explained, it shouldn't
decide that school‘s future.
My question: How is the FCA score calculated, and why does it rank Timnath last when the
district‘s own condition index does not?

The repair numbers
Submitted on behalf of Timnath Elementary families
Thank you for listening.
I want to talk about the numbers behind Timnath's building.
The district hired McKinstry to assess Timnath in 2023. That assessment puts Timnath's five-
year repair needs at $4,456,149. The board packet uses that number in one place. But the
Executive Summary uses $6,456,149, exactly $2 million more, with no explanation. Exactly
$2 million is also the amount the board approved in 2022 for Timnath‘s accessibility project,
money that was later moved out of that project. The district‘s dashboard shows Timnath's
condition index at 39%. Using the district's own $4.46 million figure, it's 27%.
The assessment also shows that Timnath‘s most urgent needs are modest. Its five top—priority
items total about $331,000. Only one is rated an “immediate concern": a $55,610 backup
generator. None of the priority items is stluctural. Parts of the roof were replaced in 2016, and
the fire alarm system was replaced in 2016 and meets current fire code.
This is a building that needs maintenance, like every building in the district. It is not a
building that needs to close.
My question: Where does the $6,456,149 figure come from? Is the extra $2 million the
accessibility project the board already approved and then defunded? And will the district
correct the 39% condition index before the board votes?

Accessibility: approved, designed, then defunded
Submitted on behalf of Timnath Elementary families
Thank you for the opportunity to speak.
The board packet says Timnath‘s accessibility upgrades "pose cost and feasibility challenges,"
and that their cost wasn‘t included in the repair estimate.
The district‘s own records tell a different story. In 2021, the district set aside $2 million in
bond money to make Timnath Elementary accessible In April 2022, the Board of Education
approved a $2 million design-build contract for the work. Engineers designed a new elevator
to serve every floor of the building, and finished their design review in March 2023.
Then, in September 2024, the district's own ledger records a "sweep“ of $1.86 million out of
Timnath's accessibility project. The only money ever spent was about $141,000 for the design
work.
So the money was approved. The plans were drawn. Then the funding was moved away. And
now the building‘s accessibility is listed as a reason to close it.
Every child should be able to get to every classroom. The board already decided Timnath's
children deserved that, and the plans already exist.
My question: Why was the $1.86 million for Timnath's accessibility moved out in September
2024, who approved it, and will the board restore it instead of closing the school?

Longer rides for young children
Submitted on behalf of Timnath Elementary families
Thank you for your time.
I want to talk about what this closure would mean for our children‘s day.
Today, the average bus ride for a Timnath Elementary student is 19 minutes. Under the
recommendation, it would be 31 minutes. The longest ride would go from 40 minutes to 50
minutes, each way. According to the district‘s own transportation analysis, that is the largest
increase of any in—townclosure.
The receiving schools are about five miles away. For a kindergartner, a 50-minute ride is a
long time. It means earlier mornings, later afternoons, less sleep, and less time for homework
and family. For parents who drive, it means longer trips and less time to volunteer at school.
There's also a simple, practical concern every parent of a young child understands. Many
five- and six-year-olds can't wait 50 minutes for a restroom. A school bus has no restroom,
and a driver can't stop. An accident on the bus is embarrassing for a small child, and it can
follow them into the school day.
For many families, Timnath Elementary is close to home. That's part of what makes it a
community school.
These costs don't show up in a savings table, but families feel them every day.
My question: How did the board weigh longer ride times for our youngest students against
the savings from this closure?

Distance is not response time
Submitted on behalf of Timnath Elementary families
Thank you for your time.
I want to follow up on emergency response, because the district's own numbers raise
questions it hasn‘t answered.
The board packet says the school resource officer assigned to Bamford is 17.4 miles away. A
different page says 19.4 miles. Either way, that officer is based nearly 20 miles from the
school.
And the packet only gives distances. It doesn't give a single response time for any police or
fire agency at either receiving school. It also gives no emergency response information for
Timnath Elementary today, so there's no way to compare.
Response time is what matters in an emergency. Miles on a map don't tell us how long it

Before moving more than 400 children, the board should know whether emergency response
would be as fast, faster or slower than it is today.
My question: Will the district provide actual emergency response times for Timnath
Elementary and both receiving schools before the October 27 vote?

Culture doesn't move on a bus
Submitted on behalf of Timnath Elementary families
Thank you, board members.
As a parent, I know a school's culture the minute I walk in: whether teachers stay, whether
families feel welcome, whether kids want to walk in the door. At Timnath, you can feel it.
And it shows up in the district's own numbers. Two out of three families who live in
Timnath's boundary choose Timnath. At the main receiving school, 44% of families in its
boundary choose a different school.
The district's costing sheet shows where this closure‘s savings come from: Timnath's
principal, assistant principal, two custodians and kitchen manager. The money for teachers
follows the students. The leadership team that built Timnath's culture is the main thing this
plan cuts.
Culture doesn't move on a bus. A University of Chicago study of school closures found that
students' learning dipped in the year closures were announced, and the students who gained
afterward were mostly those who moved to much stronger schools.
The packet's enrollment and budget numbers assume Timnath students go where they're
assigned. Timnath families have already shown what they do when they have a choice: they
choose Timnath. If some choose elsewhere instead, enrollment, budgets and savings all
change.
My question: How many Timnath families does the district expect to leave PSD's assigned
schools, and how does that change the savings?

The Town of Timnath
Submitted on behalf of Timnath Elementary families
Thank you, members of the board.
Timnath Elementary isn't just a school in the Town of Timnath. For more than a century, it has
been part of the town's identity. The original building opened in 1919.
The recommendation would close the elementary school at the heart of our town. According
to the board packet, the district lists the building‘s future use as ”to be determined."
That raises real questions for our community. What happens to a large, empty building in the
center of town? Who maintains it? How does losing the town's elementary school affect the
families deciding whether to move here?
Questionsabout renovating the building or using the district's land across the street should
shape this decision, not be answered after it.
Our town is growing faster than almost any community in the region. Decisions about its
school deserve to be made with the town, not just about it.
We‘d welcome a conversation among the district, the board and the Town of Timnath about
the school‘s future, including the land the district already owns here.
My question: Was the Town of Timnath consulted before the recommendation to close a
school within its boundaries, and what is the district‘s plan for the building?

Why a separate vote matters
Submitted on behalf of Timnath Elementary families
Thank you for your time.
I want to explain why we‘re asking for a separate vote on Timnath.
The recommendation in front of you includes several schools. Each one has its own
enrollment, its own building and its own community. But they are being presented together.
Timnath is different from the others on the list. It is the only one that is nearly full, at 95%. Its
enrollment is stable, and its town is growing. It is on the list for one reason: a building
condition score the district hasn‘t explained.
When schools with such different facts are bundled into one vote, board members who have
concerns about one school are forced to vote yes or no on all of them. That isn't fair to the
board, and it isn't fair to any of the communities involved.
A separate vote doesn‘t decide the outcome. It simply lets each board member weigh Timnath
on its own facts.
My question: Will the board president list Timnath Elementary as a separate item on the
October 27 agenda?

Planning for the children who are coming
Submitted on behalf of Timnath Elementary families
Thank you for the opportunity to speak.
I want to talk about the children who aren‘t in school yet.
According to the Census, more than 6% 0f Timnath‘s residents are under five years old.
Nearly one in three is under 18. That‘s about twice the share in Fort Collins. Our town grew
78.5% in just five years, and new families keep arriving.
And more families are coming. According to the Town of Timnath‘s planning staff, about
1,060 new single—familyhomes are planned in new developments in our town. If even half of
those homes bring one or two school-age children, that's 530 to 1,060 more Children in our
town. Yet under this recommendation, one of the receiving schools would already be near or
over capacity with today's Timnath students.
In March, the committee itself voted 23 to 0 that enrollment projections should include
buildable lots and housing turnover. Were Timnath's planned developments included in its
projections?
Those children will need an elementary school. If Timnath Elementary closes now, the district
will need to find room for them later, whether that means more buses, more portables or a
new building.
It is almost always cheaper to keep a full, working school open than to close it and rebuild
capacity a few years later.
The district already has a head start. It owns 48 acres across the street and a school that‘s 95%
full. What's missing is a long-term building plan for Timnath designed for the growth that's
already here.
My question: With about 1,060 new homes coming to Timnath, how does the district plan to
serve those children if Timnath Elementary closes and a receiving school is already near
capacity?

What the district's equity review found
Submitted on behalf of Timnath Elementary families
Thank you, members of the board.
I want to talk about something the district‘s own reviewers raised in the board packet.
The packet includes an equity review of each closure. For Timnath, it warns that the plan
would likely result in a student population that is "more racially and demographically
segregated than is ideal." It says more af?uent students would likely go to one receiving
school "with little increase in diversity," while students going to the other school would
continue current patterns.
In other words, the district's own reviewers expect this closure to separate Timnath‘s students
along racial and economic lines.
The review doesn't stop there. It recommends a "neighborhood-level enrollment analysis" and
a review of boundaries "to strengthen racial and socioeconomic integration." As far as we can
tell, that analysis has not been done.
When the district‘s own equity review identifies a risk like this, the board should have the
analysis it recommended before deciding, not after.
My question: Has the neighborhood-level equity analysis recommended in the board packet
been completed, and what did it find?

Closing
Submitted on behalf of Timnath Elementary families
Good evening, members of the board.
As you consider the Comprehensive Planning Committee‘s recommendations, I'd ask one
question: why are we closing a school that is ALREADY achieving the very goals these
recommendations are meant to accomplish?
Consider the committee‘s own goals:
0 Three to four classroom tracks per grade. Timnath has three tracks in kindergarten,
first, second and fourth grades, and four in third and fifth.
- About 400 elementary students. Timnath serves 427, plus 28 early childhood
students: 455 children in all.
- A diverse student body. Timnath serves 169 minority students.
- Full specials and enrichment. Timnath offers Art, STEM, Physical Education and
Music, plus Run Club, LEGO Club, Art Club, Spanish Club, Acting Club and Chess
Club.
These aren't goals for some distant future. They are what Timnath Elementary is doing today.
Timnath Elementary is not a problem to be solved. It is a success story to be studied and
supported. It is a jewel in the crown, not a thorn in the side.
The question shouldn‘t be, "Why should Timnath Elementary remain open?" It should be,
"Why aren‘t we working harder to understand why Timnath Elementary is thriving?"
My request: Please vote on Timnath separately on October 27, and keep Timnath Elementary
open.

SUMMARY 
The Town of Timnath leases and maintains a unique property known as the Timnath 
Reservoir. The town has invested in significant improvements on-site to benefit the 
residents and is looking to continue with more improvements. This project aims to add 
additional trail connectivity throughout the site, along with addressing safety concerns 
and maintenance issues. The town is seeking a qualified engineering consultant to 
design multiple sections of trail and address drainage issues on existing infrastructure.

N/A 
 
RECOMMENDATION 
Staff recommends approval of the attached Resolution. 
 
KEY POINTS

Page 50 of 234
Based on the PROST master plan conducted in 2025, trail connectivity was ranked as 
the community’s second highest priority.  The town has already constructed portions of 
a trail around the reservoir, and this project will continue expanding that trail network. 
  
The project was broken into 3 areas of work:  
  
1. Park Entrance to Event Space Trail Connection: Area A 
It will connect the entrance of the park with an existing trail near the event space located 
in the park. The trail will be a 10 ft concrete trail with a 2 ft crusher fine shoulder on one 
side. Trail will follow all ADA standards. The trail will need a fence along the portion 
adjacent to nearby ditch with a solar powered gate that will be utilized by maintenance 
staff and property owners. 
  
  
2. Attendant Shed to Reservoir Loop Trail (over dam): Area B 
Connecting the attendant shed and two existing trail pieces together. This trail will again 
be a 10 ft concrete trail that goes over the dam portion of the site. Trail will follow all 
ADA standards. 
  
3. Maintenance Road Regrading and Drainage Improvements: Area C 
Regrading of the existing maintenance road. The road currently channels any moisture 
down and has caused significant erosion to a trail and boat ramp location. The town 
needs the road to be engineered and graded to improve storm water conveyance, 
minimize erosion long term and utilize existing drainage where applicable. 
  
Given the size and financial implications of the project, the town issued a request for 
proposal RFP.  The town held a pre-bid meeting with 9 contractors in attendance and 
subsequently received 4 bids from the following firms: 
 Civil Specialties 
 Duran Excavating 
 NORAA Concrete Construction Corp 
 RCD Construction Inc. 
  
Following a thorough evaluation of the submittals, the town selected NORAA Concrete 
Construction Inc.  The selection was based on their bid submittal pricing, experience 
and expertise, ability to complete work in acceptable time frame, and track record of 
repeatedly completing projects with other local municipalities.
