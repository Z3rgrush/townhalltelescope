8 PUBLIC HEARINGS  
 a) Land Use Code Amendments 
  
Neighborhood Meeting Notice (Article 2.9.4): 
The amendment clarifies that the contact information required in neighborhood 
meeting notices shall consist of the property owner's address and phone 
number, replacing the generic reference to "contact information." The change is 
intended to protect applicants from having email addresses harvested for 
fraudulent purposes while still providing a public contact point. Following public 
comment from Kirsten Starman of the Ladera team, who raised the question of 
whether the notice should reference the applicant rather than the property 
owner, the Commission and Community Development Director Scott Robinson 
agreed that the language should be amended to read "owner or their 
representative," providing flexibility while maintaining public access to a contact. 
Chairperson Goldstein also raised, for future consideration, the question of 
whether the 500-foot notice radius is adequate in circumstances where adjacent 
properties fall just outside that boundary. 
  
Subdivision Applicability (Article 2.9.10.1): 
The amendment removes public right-of-way dedication as a distinguishing 
factor between minor and major subdivisions, relying solely on lot count. Six or 
fewer lots constitutes a minor subdivision; seven or more requires the major 
subdivision process. This change aligns the code with the Town's recently 
adopted requirement for dedicated public rights-of-way in residential 
subdivisions, which had created a technical conflict. 
Page 5 of 109
  
Administrative Plat (Article 2.9.11.1): 
The amendment expands the administrative plat process to allow private 
easement adjustments and relocations without triggering a full subdivision 
review, provided the easement holder consents in writing. Public easements 
and rights-of-way remain subject to standard review. Administrative plat 
applications involving related plan set changes may now be processed 
concurrently with a Modification to Prior Approval application under Section 
2.9.18, which governs changes of less than 10% to previously approved plans. 
  
Fences, Walls, and Hedges — Permitted Materials (Article 5.3.5.1.C): 
The amendment adds vinyl as a permitted fencing material, consistent with the 
Colorado Wildfire Resiliency Code, which the Town has adopted. Commissioner 
Wood expressed concern that explicitly naming vinyl would be inconsistent with 
other Town standards that prohibit vinyl building materials such as siding, and 
that wood fences already installed in developing neighborhoods create a 
practical complication. Director Robinson explained that the amendment is 
intended to provide a compliant option for fences located within eight feet of a 
structure, where wood is effectively prohibited under the fire code, and that HOA 
covenants may further restrict materials regardless of Town allowances. 
Commissioner Wood indicated he could support forwarding the amendment to 
Council with a note that the Commission discussed this concern. Staff also 
noted that Commissioner Bianchi had inquired about wrought iron; staff 
confirmed that decorative metal, already listed as a permitted material, 
encompasses that option. 
  
Motion to recommend approval of the Land Use Code Amendments to Town 
Council, with the amendment that the neighborhood meeting notice requirement 
reference the owner or applicant's contact information, was made by 
Commissioner Wood and seconded by Alternate Commissioner Mainz. The 
motion carried 4-0.

SUMMARY 
A working draft of the Comprehensive Plan is available for public review and comments 
on YourTimnath.org.  
  
Process Overview 
The Town retained the services of BerryDunn to update the Comprehensive Plan and 
kicked off the process in March of 2025 with an anticipated 12-18 month process. The 
update of the Plan is needed because of how quickly the Town has grown since the last 
Plan was updated in 2020. The State also has new requirements that need to be 
considered as part of a comprehensive plan (Water Supply Element, Strategic Growth 
Element, Housing Needs Assessment, Recreation and Tourism Element), most of which 
have a state-imposed deadline for adoption of December 31, 2026.  
  
The process to adopt the plan is to hold a public hearing on December 1, 2026 for a 
Planning Commission recommendation and hold a public hearing on December 8, 2026 
for Town Council adoption. This working draft is intended to seek additional feedback by 
the public and the Planning Commission. Subsequently, the Planning Commission can 
provide additional direction on changes needed to prepare a final adoption draft. Staff is 
continuing to review and further refine material - mostly the appendices. The State has 
conducted some preliminary reviews for respective State requirements, which their 
comments are included as attachments. A final adoption draft will be prepared for the 
public hearings in December.

The Timnath Comprehensive Plan establishes a long-range vision for managing the 
Town’s continued growth while preserving the qualities that make Timnath a desirable 
place to live, work, and invest. Timnath has evolved from a small rural community into 
one of Northern Colorado’s fastest-growing municipalities. The plan emphasizes the 
need to balance growth with infrastructure capacity, water resources, fiscal 
sustainability, and community character. 
  
 The plan’s overarching theme, “Growing with Purpose,” reflects a commitment to 
directing future development in a strategic and sustainable manner. Rather than limiting 
growth, the plan focuses on ensuring that new development occurs where services, 
infrastructure, and transportation networks can be efficiently provided and maintained. 
This theme is consistent with a Place Types framework where it focuses on the desired 
character, intensity, and function of existing, emerging, and changing areas of town, 
providing greater land use flexibility while creating a clearer vision for future 
development within the growth management area. The framework supports a range of 
environments, including established neighborhoods, mixed-use centers, employment 
districts, downtown area, and preserved rural and open space lands. 
  
Two of the major priorities the plan identifies are housing diversity and economic 
diversification. Housing diversity as one of Timnath’s most pressing needs. While the 
community has historically been dominated by single-family homes, demographic and 
economic trends point to increasing demand for a wider range of housing options 
including townhomes, duplexes, apartments, accessory dwelling units, workforce 
housing, and senior-oriented housing. A diverse housing stock will ensure future 
residents can find housing options that match their needs and budgets throughout 
different stages of life. Colorado's planning statutes now require planning commissions, 
when adopting or amending comprehensive plans, to consider applicable Housing 
Needs Assessments (HNAs) by December 31, 2026, then to follow-up with a Housing 
Action Plan by January 1, 2028. 
  
Economic diversification seeks to transition Timnath from primarily a residential 
community to a more balanced community that offers additional employment 
opportunities, commercial services, and local destinations. Key strategies include 
attracting job-producing businesses, expanding commercial activity, fostering 
entrepreneurship, and leveraging regional transportation corridors to strengthen the 
local tax base and reduce dependence on residential development. 
  
Other priorities include water availability, infrastructure capacity, fiscal sustainability, 
protecting natural resources, Old Town revitalization and a more connected community. 
Together all seek to ensure that growth contributes positively to the community over 
time by building a more complete, connected, resilient, and economically balanced 
community over the coming decades.

Introduction and Vision
Since adoption of the 2020 Comprehensive Plan, Timnath has continued to experience 
substantial residential development, changing service demands, and increasing regional growth 
pressures. The Town’s population has grown from approximately 300 residents in the early 
2000s to an estimated 12,158 residents in 2025, transforming a historically agricultural 
community into a rapidly developing community within the Northern Colorado Front Range. 
These changes underscore the need to update the Comprehensive Plan to reflect current 
conditions and provide clear direction for managing continued growth, coordinating 
infrastructure and services, and responding to the community’s evolving priorities.
Growth is projected to continue in the coming years, with Timnath’s population projected to 
reach approximately 14,200 residents by 2029. Continued growth in Larimer and Weld Counties 
will place additional pressure on the broader transportation, housing, infrastructure, and service 
systems on which the Town depends. The actual pace and pattern of growth will be influenced 
by land use decisions, infrastructure capacity and investment, water availability, annexation, and 
development activity. This Plan looks beyond these near-term projections to establish a long-
term ten-year framework for growth and investment over the planning horizon.
This Comprehensive Plan establishes a long-range policy framework for responding to these 
changes intentionally. It coordinates decisions related to land use, housing, transportation, 
water and wastewater, economic development, parks and open space, public services, and 
infrastructure so that growth occurs in locations and forms that support Timnath’s long-term 
fiscal sustainability, community character, and quality of life.
The Plan builds from the direction established by the 2020 Comprehensive Plan while 
responding to the conditions and choices facing Timnath today. It introduces a future place type 
approach to improve upon previous land use categories and establishes a more strategic 
approach to evaluating future growth based on infrastructure, serviceability, community priorities, 
and regional coordination. The place types establish the intended long-term character and 
function of different areas but do not change existing zoning. Changes to zoning districts, 
permitted uses, or development standards would occur through subsequent amendments to the 
Town’s Land Use Code or individual rezoning processes.
A Community in Transition
Timnath’s rapid growth has created both new opportunities and important choices about the 
community it wants to become, while increasingly distinguishing it from other communities in the 
region. Recent development has been largely comprised of newer single-family neighborhoods, 
with 90.4% of housing units identified as single-family homes in 2023 and 89% of the Town’s 
housing stock constructed after 2010. Timnath also has a comparatively young, family-oriented 
population, and a medium household income of $170,041.
At the same time, Timnath remains closely connected to the broader Northern Colorado region. 
The Town supports approximately 1,400 jobs, while more than 2,800 residents commute to jobs 
outside the community. Recent and emerging commercial and mixed-use development, 
including development near I-25 and Harmony Road, Ladera, and Riverbend Urban Village, 
create opportunities to expand local shopping, dining, employment, and other destinations as 
the community grows.

Infrastructure and natural resources are equally important to Timnath’s future. The Town relies 
on external providers for water and wastewater service, making growth, annexation, water 
availability, utility capacity, and infrastructure investment coordination particularly important. 
Transportation needs will also increase as Timnath grows, creating a need to strengthen 
roadway, trail, walking, bicycling, and regional transportational connections. 
Together, these conditions present several fundamental planning choices. The theme of this 
Comprehensive Plan, Growing with Purpose, reflects Timnath’s commitment to shaping growth 
intentionally rather than simply responding to change. As the Town continues to evolve, this 
theme emphasizes growth that is coordinated with infrastructure, water resources, housing 
needs, economic opportunity, parks and open space, and the community’s desired character. It 
recognizes that Timnath can welcome new neighborhoods, businesses, amenities, and regional 
connections while still protecting the qualities that make the Town distinctive. Growing with 
purpose means making deliberate choices about where and how growth occurs so that each 
decision contributes to a more connected, resilient, livable, and fiscally sustainable future. 
Putting this commitment into practice requires the Plan to address several fundamental 
questions:
•Where and when should Timnath grow? 
•How should growth be coordinated with roads, water, wastewater, and other 
infrastructure capacity? 
•How can the Town provide a broader range of housing choices while maintaining high-
quality neighborhoods? 
•Where can additional employment, shopping, dining, and community destinations be 
supported? 
•How can parks, trails, open space, and natural resources remain defining features as 
development occurs? 
•How can Timnath retain its identity while becoming more integrated with the Northern 
Colorado region?
These choices from the foundation for the future place types, strategic growth framework, 
annexation strategy, housing policies, infrastructure and water planning, transportation 
recommendations, economic development direction, and implementation actions contained in 
this plan.
Relationship with Other Town Plans
The Comprehensive Plan works alongside the Town’s other adopted plans and ongoing 
planning efforts. Rather than replacing more detailed plans for transportation, parks and 
recreation, infrastructure, and other Town systems, it provides an overarching land use and 
growth framework within which those plans can be coordinated. Detailed documentation of 
existing conditions, technical analysis, community engagement activities, and the planning 
process are provided in Appendices B, C, and D.
Several recent Town plans also provide an important foundation for this Comprehensive Plan.

Future Place Types
What are Place Types?
Future place types describe the desired character, scale, function, and development patterns 
envisioned for different parts of Timnath. The Future Place Types Map shows where each place 
type is intended, providing geographic guidance for development, preservation, and coordinated 
infrastructure investment. Place types express the Town’s long-term vision rather than existing 
conditions and will inform future updates to the Land Use Code.
Why Future Place Types?
Timnath is adopting future place types to provide more complete and durable guidance than the 
eight land use categories used in the 2020 Comprehensive Plan which are described in Figure 2.
Rather than linking the Plan to zoning districts that may change over time, place types address 
the desired mix of uses, development pattern, scale, design character, connectivity, public realm, 
and function of each area. This approach allows the Plan to coordinate land use, transportation, 
infrastructure, housing, economic development, and growth management while remaining 
adaptable over time.
Place types are policy guidance, not zoning. They do not change existing zoning, permitted 
uses, development rights, or parcel-specific standards. Implementation will occur through 
subsequent Land Use Code amendments, rezonings, development review, and public 
investment decisions.
How to Interpret and Use Place Types
Primary uses are the predominant activities intended to define each place type, while supporting 
uses may complement those activities when appropriately located, designed, and scaled. The 
lists are illustrative rather than exhaustive; an unlisted use may be considered when it is 
consistent with the place type’s intended character, function, development pattern, and intensity. 
Intensity should be evaluated using the full place type description, including density, building 
scale, site design, connectivity, infrastructure capacity, and surrounding context, rather than any 
single measure. Where place types meet, development should provide appropriate transitions 
through compatible scale, building placement, landscaping, open space, street design, or other 
context-sensitive features.
Until the Land Use Code is updated, the Town should use the applicable place type as long-
range policy guidance alongside existing zoning and other Comprehensive Plan policies when 
reviewing annexations, rezonings, development proposals, and public investments. Existing 
zoning remains regulatory. Where zoning and the mapped place type differ, the place type 
identifies the Town’s long-term direction but does not itself rezone the property or alter existing 
development rights.

and function over time. The amount of land designated for each place type was intentionally 
considered in relation to the low-, medium-, and high-growth projections described in the Growth

Parks, Natural Areas, and Open Space 
“Parks, Natural Areas, and Open Spaces” include community and regional parks, greenways, 
natural areas, and other open lands that protect sensitive environmental features and provide 
recreation and respite for residents. These places encompass resources such as floodplains, 
wetlands, riparian corridors, native grasslands, and significant tree stands, along with developed 
park facilities that accommodate a range of passive and active recreation. Some sites function 
primarily as conservation or buffer areas with minimal improvements, while others include 
programmed facilities such as sports fields, courts, playgrounds, and community gathering 
spaces. Together, they form a connected open space network that supports ecological health, 
stormwater management, and community well-being. 
Primary Land Uses: Parks and Open Space; Sensitive Environmental and Ecological Areas; 
Sports Complexes and Rec Facilities 
Rural Areas
“Rural areas” are intended to preserve working agricultural landscapes, natural resource lands, 
and long-term growth options rather than to accommodate suburban or estate-lot residential 
development. The predominant pattern is active agriculture and ranching on large parcels, 
with exceptionally low residential densities (residential only as an accessory to 
farming/ranching) and minimal extension of urban services. Overall residential density in rural 
areas is very low, not exceeding about one dwelling unit per 35 acres, to avoid premature 
fragmentation of the landscape and fiscally challenging rural sprawl. Typical uses include crop 
production, grazing, agricultural support facilities, and natural resource management, with a 
limited number of single-family homes directly associated with agricultural operations. 
New non-agricultural residential development should be extremely limited and, where allowed, 
occur at very low overall densities and in configurations that do not preclude future, more 
efficient urbanization when annexation and services become appropriate.   
Primary Land Uses: Single-Family Development; Working Agricultural Landscapes; Natural 
Resource Lands

Established Neighborhoods
“Established neighborhoods” are predominantly single-family detached areas 
with uniform setbacks, building designs, and lot sizes. Small-lot homes and attached 
townhomes occur where they maintain the existing rhythm and feel of the street. That rhythm is 
shaped by lot width, setbacks, building massing, roof form, and overall height. Streets follow a 
curvilinear pattern with occasional cul-de-sacs rather than a traditional grid, producing irregular 
block shapes and varied lot configurations. Landscaped front yards, sidewalks, 
and tree-lined streets create an attractive streetscape and comfortable residential environment.

Neighborhood parks, schools, and faith-based institutions are distributed throughout the area, 
providing gathering spaces and convenient access to daily needs. 
These neighborhoods are designed primarily around automobile access, with local trips served 
by a hierarchy of local, collector, and arterial streets. Sidewalks, trails, and bicycle connections 
are present in some locations but are often discontinuous. Future planning focuses on 
enhancing connectivity within neighborhoods, improving pedestrian comfort along key routes, 
and strengthening links to the town’s broader trail network and nearby 
centers. Small-scale neighborhood commercial, low-intensity multifamily housing, 
and mixed-use nodes are appropriate at neighborhood edges or along key corridors where they 
can provide local services and housing diversity while preserving established neighborhood 
character.

Emerging Neighborhoods 
“Emerging neighborhoods” are walkable, mixed-housing areas organized around a connected 
street network and one or more neighborhood centers. Blocks are short and regular, with 
frequent intersections and alleys that provide rear access to garages and service areas. A wider 
range of housing types, such as large and small-lot single-family homes, cottages, townhomes, 
courtyard units, and low-rise multifamily, is integrated within each neighborhood, allowing 
residents to choose from diverse housing options while sharing the same streets, parks, and 
schools. While there is intended to be a mix of housing types and lot sizes within emerging 
areas, the overall average residential density of each development application in this place type 
should fall within a range of at least nine units per acre up to a maximum of 18 units per 
acre. Small-scale commercial, civic, and mixed-use buildings are concentrated at neighborhood 
centers and along key streets, putting daily needs within a short walk or bike ride. 
Streets, public spaces, and buildings work together to create a strong sense of place. Primary 
streets frame views to parks, schools, and other civic buildings. Sidewalks, street trees, 
and on-street parking define comfortable pedestrian environments, and buildings sit close to the 
sidewalk to create an active public realm. Future planning and development reinforce this 
connected pattern, prioritize walking and bicycling, and support a balanced mix of uses and 
housing types while maintaining a cohesive neighborhood character.

“Mixed residential” areas are predominantly residential neighborhoods with a higher overall 
intensity and a much greater share of multifamily and attached housing than emerging or 
established neighborhoods. Building types include stacked flats, walk-up apartments, 
townhomes, rowhouses, courtyard and mansion apartments, and small-lot single-family homes,

diversity and additional population to support nearby services and transit. The overall average

closer to the street than in established neighborhoods, with modest front setbacks, sidewalks, 
and street trees that support a more urban residential feel. Future development reinforces the 
residential character of these areas while expanding housing options, improving walkability, and 
supporting small-scale services that meet daily needs without transforming the area into a

Mixed-Use
“Mixed-use neighborhoods” are compact, walkable areas where residential 
and neighborhood-serving nonresidential uses are co-primary, at an intensity below downtown 
but higher than surrounding residential neighborhoods. These areas include both 
small neighborhood-scale nodes and short, main-street-style corridors that provide daily goods, 
services, dining, and civic uses within or adjacent to residential areas. Building types include 
townhomes, rowhouses, stacked flats, walk-up apartments, courtyard and mansion 
apartments, small-lot single-family homes, and mixed-use buildings with active ground floors 
and upper-story residential or office. Mixed-use neighborhoods commonly sit along key 
corridors, at important intersections, or embedded as nodes within larger residential areas, 
providing complete-neighborhood amenities and supporting transit where 
present. Neighborhood-scale commercial, office, and civic uses are integrated as core 
components rather than incidental elements.

Commercial Corridors and Centers 
“Commercial corridors and centers” are predominantly commercial areas located along major 
arterials, highways, and key nodes that serve community and regional retail, service, and 
employment needs. These places accommodate a mix of commercial formats, 
including multi-tenant centers, larger-format retail, restaurants, lodging, and auto-oriented 
services, in building and site types that support higher volumes of vehicular traffic. Limited 
residential or mixed-use may occur as part of larger center redevelopment or at strategic 
intersections, but commercial uses remain the primary defining element. Commercial corridors 
and centers often sit at highway interchanges, major intersections, and along key corridors into 
Timnath. Over time, these areas should evolve from fragmented strip development toward more 
organized, connected centers with improved streetscapes and better integration with 
surrounding neighborhoods.

Innovation and Employment  
“Innovation and employment areas” are predominantly employment-oriented districts that 
accommodate a mix of advanced manufacturing, research and development, 
laboratories, logistics and warehousing, flex-space, and office or business park uses. These 
places are intended to provide a range of job opportunities, from small startups and maker 
spaces to larger enterprises, in building and site formats that can adapt over time as industries 
change. Typical building types include flex industrial buildings, multi-tenant office/warehouse 
structures, research and lab buildings, stand-alone office buildings, and, in some cases, 
specialized production or logistics facilities. Limited supporting uses, such as small-scale retail 
or services that primarily serve employees and nearby businesses, may be included but should 
not dominate. Innovation and employment areas are generally located with good access to 
major corridors or highways, minimizing heavy traffic on local neighborhood streets while 
connecting jobs to the regional transportation network.

Purpose and Planning Context
Colorado’s strategic growth planning requirements emphasize coordinated evaluation of 
housing needs, infrastructure, water supply, natural and agricultural lands, and regional 
conditions. Senate Bill 24 174 directs municipalities to adopt a Strategic Growth Element by 
December 31, 2026, and update it regularly through the planning process. For Timnath, this 
element builds on the Comprehensive Plan, GMA, annexation practices, Three Mile Plan, and 
intergovernmental agreements to guide the location and timing of development in response to 
local priorities, housing availability, resource constraints, and long-term development costs.
The Inventory and Analysis Report identify low-, medium-, and high-growth scenarios with 
projected 2040 populations of 14,587, 26,786, and 50,637, respectively. The low scenario 
applies regional growth rates; the medium scenario maintains Timnath’s recent share of 
regional population growth; and the high scenario assumes growth continues above regional 
rates. Growth is anticipated to occur near the medium scenario, reflecting continued demand 
and moderation from the exceptional pace of previous years. This scenario provides a planning 
assumption rather than a fixed forecast; actual outcomes will depend on market conditions, 
water availability, infrastructure capacity, and Town land use and annexation decisions.

Existing Strategic Growth Policies
Timnath currently utilizes several policies and planning tools to guide growth and annexation. 
The following sections describe this existing foundation and how each tool contributes to future 
growth decisions consistent with Colorado’s Strategic Growth Element requirements.
Growth Areas and Urban Growth Boundaries
Timnath’s GMA identifies the geographic area beyond current municipal limits that the Town 
includes within its anticipated long-term growth footprint. Inclusion within the GMA does not 
mean that property is currently within the Town, approved for development, or expected to 
annex within a particular timeframe. Annexation and development remain subject to separate 
planning, infrastructure, serviceability, transportation, environmental, fiscal, and development 
review considerations. 
The northern GMA boundary has been adjusted in coordination with the cooperative planning 
area north of Timnath. The adjustment changes which northern unincorporated lands are 
identified within Timnath’s anticipated growth footprint; it does not, by itself, annex or de-annex 
land, change zoning or development entitlements, establish infrastructure or utility service, or 
determine whether a particular property should be annexed or developed through the 
Comprehensive Plan’s strategic growth and annexation frameworks.
The GMA is also distinct from Town limits and the Three Mile Plan area. Town limits identify 
property already incorporated into Timnath, while the GMA includes unincorporated land within 
the Town’s anticipated growth footprint. The Three Mile Plan area is a separate statutory 
annexation-planning geography; inclusion within that area does not indicate that annexation will 
occur. 
Annexation and Growth Management Approach
The Comprehensive Plan provides Timnath’s primary policy direction for long-term growth, while 
annexation and the Three Mile Plan serve separate roles in evaluating potential expansion. 
Annexation incorporates property into the Town and allows municipal land use and development 
requirements to apply.
The Three Mile Plan addresses the statutory planning requirements associated with areas 
surrounding Timnath’s municipal boundaries. Its planning area is distinct from both the GMA 
and Town limits, and inclusion within the three-mile area does not establish Town support for 
annexation or development. Areas outside Timnath’s anticipated growth footprint should

policies rather than assumed to be appropriate for future development.
Coordination with Regional Partners 
Timnath’s intergovernmental agreements (IGAs) and cooperative planning areas provide an 
established framework for coordinating growth, annexation, development review, infrastructure,

WC.3.3: Water Serviceability and Infrastructure Capacity
Use water serviceability, infrastructure capacity, and provider coordination as key 
considerations in annexation, rezoning, and development review decisions.
Because Timnath does not operate municipal water or wastewater utilities, annexation, rezoning, 
and development review should incorporate documented provider capacity and serviceability. 
Early coordination with ELCO, FCLWD, NWCWD, and regional sanitation districts can identify 
infrastructure constraints, clarify improvement responsibilities, and help ensure that new 
development can be served reliably without creating avoidable long-term system obligations.
WC.3.4: Future Growth Area Alignment
Align future growth areas with confirmed or planned water infrastructure capacity to support 
efficient service delivery and long-term conservation outcomes.
Directing growth toward areas with confirmed or planned water infrastructure capacity supports 
efficient service delivery and reduces the need for premature or costly system extensions. 
Coordinating future development patterns with water availability, provider capital plans, and 
conservation objectives will strengthen Timnath’s strategic growth framework and improve long-
term infrastructure and resource efficiency.
Goal 4: Monitor, Adapt, and Improve Conservation Strategies Over Time
Effective conservation planning requires regular evaluation of program participation, policy 
performance, provider conditions, and changes in growth, drought, and regulatory requirements. 
Establishing a consistent monitoring and review process will allow the Town to identify effective 
strategies, address implementation gaps, and adjust its approach as new information becomes 
available. Peer benchmarking can supplement local data by identifying practices suited to 
Timnath’s governance structure and regional context.
WC.4.1: Monitor Conservation Participation and Performance
Track participation and performance in Town-supported conservation initiatives to evaluate 
program reach, identify implementation gaps, and inform resource allocation.
Maintain consistent participation data for workshops, landscape programs, HOA outreach, and 
other initiatives, supplemented by available provider data on conservation outcomes. Use 
findings to identify communication and technical assistance needs and improve education and 
incentive programs.
WC.4.2: Review and Adapt Conservation Policies and Drought Procedures
Periodically review and update conservation policies, ordinances, drought response procedures, 
and implementation tools using monitoring results, provider input, and community feedback.

Purpose and Planning Context
Timnath’s principal natural resources include the Cache la Poudre River corridor, Timnath 
Reservoir, wetlands and drainageways, lakes and ponds, alluvial groundwater areas, 
grasslands, agricultural lands, and connected open spaces. These resources support habitat, 
water quality, groundwater recharge, flood management, recreation, and community character. 
Although no federally designated critical habitat has been identified within Timnath, available 
federal and state data indicate that several threatened, endangered, proposed, migratory bird, 
and rare species may occur within the Town or Growth Management Area. The resource maps 
and detailed inventories provided in Appendix B should inform land use planning, development 
review, open space planning, and infrastructure decisions.
Continued development may place pressure on these resources through the conversion and 
fragmentation of agricultural lands, grasslands, and habitat areas; increased impervious 
surfaces and stormwater runoff; greater outdoor water demand; and heightened exposure to 
drought, extreme heat, flooding, and grassland wildfire. The policies in this section therefore

surface water quality, and groundwater recharge; consideration of sensitive species and habitat 
during development review; and coordinated conservation, restoration, and hazard mitigation. 
These priorities support growth that responds to Timnath’s environmental conditions and 
strengthens long-term community and ecosystem resilience.

Goal 1: Balance Development with Open Space, Habitat, and Natural Landscape 
Preservation
Timnath’s undeveloped lands include agricultural areas, grasslands, shrublands, hay and 
pasture areas, riparian corridors, and other high-plains habitats that contribute to ecological 
function, wildlife movement, community character, and hazard mitigation. As development 
pressure increases, coordinated conservation and growth management will be necessary to 
maintain these resources as an interconnected system. This goal supports a balanced 
Page 59 of 109
47
development pattern that accommodates appropriate growth while protecting the natural 
landscapes that contribute to Timnath’s identity, environmental health, and long-term resilience.
NR. 1.1: Protect Mature Trees and Native Vegetation
Protect mature, healthy trees and native vegetation when planning improvements on Town-
owned land, within rights-of-way, and in designated open-space corridors. Establish clear 
standards for retention, removal, and replacement in development review, with appropriate 
consideration of tree condition, safety, and site constraints.
The standards should be maintained and communicated during private development and Town 
development projects, to ensure compliance. 
NR.1.2: Preserve and Connect Lands
Preserve and connect open spaces, agricultural lands, and undeveloped natural areas that 
provide habitat value, wildlife movement opportunities, and environmental benefits.
Maintaining connected open spaces and habitat areas supports ecological continuity while 
allowing development to occur in appropriate locations. Agricultural lands also remain an 
important component of Timnath’s landscape and contribute to community character, habitat 
value, and environmental resilience. Conservation priorities should therefore focus on retaining 
larger, connected resource areas and avoiding fragmentation that reduces their long-term 
function.
NR.1.3: Utilize Growth Management Tools for Conservation
Apply conservation criteria in future land use, annexation, rezoning, subdivision, infrastructure 
planning, and development review to identify and protect priority natural areas, habitat corridors, 
and open space connections.
Maintain current resource mapping to support transparent, coordinated decisions and direct 
growth away from lands with significant ecological or community value. Apply the habitat and 
wetland considerations in NR.2.4 and NR.4.2 within this shared review framework.
NR.1.4: Protect Sensitive Lands Through Design
Use conservation design, clustering, easements, land dedication, strategic acquisition, and 
partnerships where appropriate to protect sensitive lands while allowing responsible growth. 
Conservation design, clustered development, easements, land dedication, strategic acquisition, 
and partnerships provide complementary tools for protecting sensitive lands while 
accommodating responsible growth. Coordination with willing landowners, land trusts, and 
regional partners can expand preservation opportunities, while updated development standards 
can help retain larger, contiguous habitat areas and reduce fragmentation.
NR.1.5: Prioritize Open Space Protection
Prioritize open space protection in areas that also support hazard mitigation, including flood 
plain management, wildfire buffering, groundwater recharge, and stormwater management.
