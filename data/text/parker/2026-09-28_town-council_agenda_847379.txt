SUBJECT: LDO Requirements for Wildlife Fencing 
     
  
ITEM OVERVIEW:  
Planning staff received a request from a plant nursery to install wildlife exclusion fencing to 
protect its outdoor plant inventory from deer. The current Land Development Ordinance (LDO) 
does not provide specific standards for wildlife exclusion fencing. Because effective deer 
exclusion fencing generally requires additional height beyond what is currently permitted in 
front of a primary structure, staff is seeking Town Council direction on whether the LDO should 
be amended to allow wildlife exclusion fencing for plant nurseries. 
  
BACKGROUND AND ANALYSIS:  
Background 
The Town was approached by a plant nursery regarding the installation of wildlife exclusion 
fencing to prevent deer from accessing and damaging its outdoor plant inventory.  
 
Wildlife exclusion fencing would not be permitted under the LDO because of the following code 
limitations: 
• Fencing located in front of the primary structure cannot exceed 42 inches in height;  
• Fencing along the remaining sides of the property cannot exceed six feet in height; and 
• Chain-link fencing is prohibited. 
Research/Analysis 
Wildlife exclusion fencing is intended to prevent wildlife from accessing a defined area and can 
be used to protect landscaping, vegetation, and other plant materials from wildlife-related 
damage. Staff reviewed common fencing methods used to exclude deer and found that 
permanent woven wire or polypropylene mesh fencing is commonly used for this purpose. 
Effective deer exclusion fencing is generally eight feet in height. 
 
Staff also researched how other Colorado municipalities address deer and wildlife exclusion 
fencing. While specific municipal regulations are limited, staff identified the following examples: 
• Alamosa, CO allows deer fencing up to ten feet in height, with additional setback 
requirements for fencing exceeding six feet. 
• Buena Vista, CO allows deer protection fencing up to six feet in height, with opacity 
requirements for portions of fencing above four feet. 

Page 2 
• Note: Staff did not identify explicit wildlife exclusion fencing standards among the 
Denver metro communities reviewed. 
Wildlife exclusion fencing is also utilized in the Denver metro area for wildlife management 
purposes. Eight-foot wildlife exclusion fencing is commonly installed along highways to restrict 
wildlife access to the highway and direct wildlife toward designated crossings; examples 
include I-25 south of Castle Rock in Douglas County and wildlife fencing along E-470 north of 
Parker.  While these examples are not municipal land use regulations, they provide a local 
example of the height and design of fencing used to exclude large wildlife. 
 
Alternatives/Options 
Staff has identified two options for Council consideration: 
• Option 1 – Maintain the current LDO fencing requirements with no changes. 
• Option 2 – Amend the LDO to allow wildlife exclusion fencing for plant nurseries 
and develop appropriate standards for fence height, materials, location, and design. 
• Note: If Council wishes to proceed with Option 2, there are currently two (2) active plant 
nurseries within the town limits that would qualify, please see the attached map. 
Also, Council may wish to consider any requirements for fence post materials. The following 
requirements would apply for fence post materials under the current LDO: 
• A change in materials every 50’ along arterial and collector roads (typically a masonry 
column) is required; and 
• Materials are to tie to an existing building and/or existing fencing nearby – some 
examples might be posts of wood to tie to nearby wood fencing or posts that tie to black 
wrought iron posts if located nearby. 
 
  
FINANCIAL AND POLICY IMPACTS:  
None. 
 
COUNCIL DISCUSSION:  
Staff is seeking Council direction regarding proceeding on a proposed code change to allow for 
wildlife fencing.

September 18, 2026 
Dear Parker Mayor and Town Council: 
This letter is in support of the legislative code amendment that Parker is pursuing regarding fencing. We 
are grateful that the Town is now taking this issue seriously. However, we are disappointed that it has 
taken this long to address an issue that has had a significant financial impact on our business. We also 
appreciate the time Staff has taken to brainstorm potential fencing solutions for our use that could 
potentially meet the existing code. Unfortunately, after considering those options, we have not 
identified anything that is remotely feasible for protecting our nursery inventory. The fact of the matter 
is that the existing code does not adequately address the fencing needs of a nursery use in Parker. 
We provided extensive arguments supporting the allowance of wildlife fencing on our property as part 
of a Variance application that we ultimately did not formally submit due to the change in Parker’s Legal 
Counsel and because we were informed that a legislative code amendment was forthcoming. That 
narrative has been provided to Staff, and we anticipate it will be provided to Council. Because this issue 
is now being considered as a legislative matter, there are additional arguments in support of an 
amendment that we would like to bring to Council’s attention, which is the purpose of this letter. 
Pine Lane Nursery is a local, small business that contributes approximately $112,000 annually to the 
Town of Parker in sales tax, based on last year’s numbers. By virtue of the nature of our business and 
use of the property, we have approximately $2 million worth of inventory that must be stored outdoors. 
That inventory is susceptible to both theft and significant damage from the deer population. 
Since taking down our wildlife fence earlier this year and working with Parker to come into compliance 
with the existing code, we have experienced approximately $20,000 in damage to our inventory- and 
that number continues to increase. We estimate that dozens of deer come through our property each 
week. Wildlife fencing is therefore not simply a preference or convenience for our business; rather, it is 
a necessary means of protecting the inventory that is central to our business operation. 
The extent of the deer issue is readily apparent. Directly across the street from our property is chain-link 
fencing with signage that literally identifies the area as a “Habitat Area.” As development has increased 
around Parker in recent years, the deer have become a substantially greater problem for our business. 
This issue is further explained in the Variance narrative and supported by documentation from Colorado 
Parks and Wildlife. 
There is also objective support for the type of fencing we are requesting. A peer-reviewed publication 
from Colorado State University explicitly states that “adequate fencing to exclude deer is the only sure 
way to control deer damage” and identifies eight-foot wildlife fencing as an appropriate method of 
addressing deer damage. Eight-foot wildlife fencing is the same fencing that Pine Lane Nursery is 
advocating for on our property. This is not an arbitrary request; it is a fencing solution specifically 
designed to address the exact problem we are experiencing. 
We would also like to note that we believe this fencing was always planned and contemplated for our 
property pursuant to the PUD as well as the applicable CC&Rs, which were provided and attached with 
the Variance narrative. Our team has not been in agreement with Staff or Parker’s prior Legal Counsel 
regarding the applicability or interpretation of these documents. Rather than continue to expend 
resources on that disagreement, however, we are now directing our efforts toward a legislative solution 
to this problem. 
The surrounding properties further demonstrate that the existing 42-inch limitation does not reflect the 
actual character of fencing in this area. There are numerous chain-link fences immediately adjacent to 
and in close proximity to our property, many of which exceed 42 inches by a significant amount along 
their frontages. In fact, we included photographs of seven chain-link fences either directly adjacent to or 
in close proximity to our property with the Variance narrative. 
From an aesthetic standpoint, we also do not understand why wildlife fencing would be prohibited on 
our property when substantially more visually prominent fencing already exists throughout the 
surrounding area. The proposed deer fencing is much less visible than many of the existing fences, some 
of which are colored or include vinyl tarp. The requested fencing would serve a legitimate business and 
property-protection purpose while creating little, if any, additional visual impact. 
It is also commonplace for traditional wildlife fencing to be used throughout Colorado to protect nursery 
stock and other vegetation from deer. Examples include Castlewood Gardens in Franktown, as well as 
other nurseries and garden-related businesses throughout the state that utilize deer fencing to protect 
their inventory. The use of wildlife fencing for this purpose is not unusual or inconsistent with nursery 
operations in Colorado. It is a practical response to a well-established problem.  
Finally, this issue has placed a significant burden on our small business. We have had to hire both legal 
counsel and a planning consultant to assist us in dealing with this matter. These are substantial expenses 
for a small, locally owned business, particularly when we are simultaneously absorbing tens of 
thousands of dollars in inventory losses because we have been working to comply with the existing 
code. Parker’s small businesses should not have to go through this simply to protect inventory that is 
necessarily stored outdoors as part of an otherwise lawful and established business operation. 
We respectfully ask the Mayor and Town Council to recognize the legitimate operational needs of our 
business and move forward with a legislative solution that allows appropriate wildlife fencing for 
nursery uses. We have worked with the Town, considered the alternatives presented by Staff, removed 
our existing fence, and made every effort to comply with the existing code. Those efforts have resulted 
in substantial financial losses without producing a feasible means of protecting our inventory. 
Please do not further deplete our resources by delaying a decision on an issue that has already caused 
significant financial harm to our business. We believe the requested wildlife fencing is reasonable, 
necessary, consistent with the character of the surrounding area, and supported by both the practical 
realities of our business and the documented need to protect nursery stock from deer. 
Thank you for your consideration and for the time that Staff and Council have devoted to this issue. 
Sincerely, 
Kurt and Angie Hughes 
Pine Lane Nursery

TO:  Honorable Mayor and Members of Town Council 
    
FROM: Cassidy Beaupre, Planner I 
Bryce Matthews, Assistant Director - Planning 
    
DATE: September 28, 2026 
    
SUBJECT: LDO Requirements for Gas Stations 
     
  
ITEM OVERVIEW:  
Planning staff has received concerns from the development community regarding the Land 
Development Ordinance (LDO) setback requirements for Automotive Fuel Sales. Specifically, 
concerns have been raised regarding the 250-foot setback from residentially zoned property 
when multifamily residential is located across an arterial road or highway, as well as the 500-
foot setback from sensitive uses. Staff is seeking Town Council direction on whether the LDO 
should be amended to modify these setback requirements. 
  
BACKGROUND AND ANALYSIS:  
Background/Prior Actions 
Prior to 2024, the Town did not have any use specific setback requirements for Automotive 
Fuel Sales. With the adoption of the new LDO on May 20, 2024, use specific setback 
requirements were established for Automotive Fuel Sales. These requirements did not apply to 
Planned Development (PD) zoned properties. On November 17, 2025, the LDO was amended 
to apply the Automotive Fuel Sales setback requirements to all zoning, including PD zoned 
properties. Following this amendment, Planning staff received concerns from developers 
regarding how the setback requirements affect development opportunities on commercial 
properties. 
 
The current LDO requires Automotive Fuel Sales to meet the following location and setback 
requirements: 
• Automotive Fuel Sales may not be located within 250 feet of a hard corner, as 
measured from the right-of-way line of the arterial roads;  
• Automotive Fuel Sales must be located at least 250 feet from residentially zoned 
property or from the nearest building of multifamily; and 
• Automotive Fuel Sales must be located at least 500 feet from sensitive uses, including 
schools, daycares, and assisted living facilities.  
The development community has raised concerns regarding two of these requirements 
specifically. First, that the 250-foot residential setback applies even when an arterial road or 
highway separates an Automotive Fuel Sales use from multifamily residential. Second, 
developers have noted that daycares and Automotive Fuel Sales are both commonly located 
within commercial centers, and the 500-foot setback between these uses can make commercial 

Page 2 
center design and development difficult. 
 
Alternatives/Options 
Staff has identified two options for Council consideration: 
• Option 1 – Maintain the current LDO setback requirements with no changes. 
• Option 2 – Amend the LDO to: 1) Exclude the 250-foot residential setback when 
multifamily residential is located across an arterial road or highway; and 2) Reduce the 
setback between Automotive Fuel Sales and sensitive uses such as schools (including 
charter schools), daycares and assisted living facilities from 500 feet to 250 feet.  
Staff reviewed commercial areas throughout the town to evaluate how the current setback 
requirements may affect existing and future commercial development. Staff has identified three 
(3) commercial areas affected if the code were to be amended to not require a 250' setback 
where the proposed automotive use would be across an arterial road from a multifamily use, 
which will be covered in the study session presentation. 
 
If Council wishes to proceed with Option 2, staff recommends making the same changes to the 
Car Wash Use Specific Standards to provide consistency and predictability between the two 
uses. 
  
FINANCIAL AND POLICY IMPACTS:  
None. 
 
COUNCIL DISCUSSION:  
Staff is seeking Council direction regarding proceeding with a proposed code change to: 
• Exclude the Automotive Fuel Sales 250-foot setback from residentially zoned property 
when multifamily residential is located across an arterial road or highway; 
• Reduce the setback between Automotive Fuel Sales and sensitive uses from 500 feet to 
250 feet; and 
• Apply any changes to the Automotive Fuel Sales standards to the Car Wash Use 
Specific Standards, as well.
