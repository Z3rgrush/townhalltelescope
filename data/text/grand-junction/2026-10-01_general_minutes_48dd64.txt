Housing Affordability Code Task Force – Meeting Summary 
10.01.26 
Primary Focus: Consultant kickoff, project scope and schedule, Land Development Code/TEDS 
assessment, housing-cost priorities, and expectations for recommendations. 
Consultant Introduction and Project Approach 
KLJ introduced its project team and outlined its role in reviewing the Land Development Code 
and Transportation Engineering Design Standards (TEDS). The consultant acknowledged that 
several team members previously worked for the City and addressed concerns regarding 
independence and objectivity. KLJ emphasized that prior experience with Grand Junction's 
regulations will be used as institutional knowledge, but the review will be conducted from a 
third-party perspective. 
The Task Force emphasized that the consultant's work must remain focused on the group's 
primary charge: reducing regulatory barriers and costs associated with producing housing. 
Members specifically asked KLJ to evaluate not only whether a regulation achieves a legitimate 
objective, but also the cost associated with achieving that objective. 
KLJ confirmed that construction, engineering, planning, and transportation expertise will be used 
to evaluate practical development impacts and costs. 
Working Agreements 
The group reaffirmed its existing working agreements, including listening to understand, 
allowing everyone an opportunity to participate, focusing on systems rather than individuals, 
avoiding assumptions about intent, and looking forward rather than revisiting past disputes. 
Additional expectations were discussed, including meeting review deadlines, maintaining one 
conversation at a time, and using individual development projects as examples without designing 
citywide regulations around a single project. 
Consultant Work Plan and Four-Bucket Approach 
KLJ is currently completing an Assessment and Opportunity Analysis of the Land Development 
Code and TEDS. Consultant recommendations and Task Force issues will be consolidated and 
organized into four general categories: 
1. Quick/easy amendments – relatively straightforward changes that can be drafted and 
advanced quickly. 
2. Items requiring additional Task Force discussion – issues where KLJ understands the 
concern but needs additional direction or selection among alternatives. 
3. Items dependent on another policy or process – changes that cannot be fully 
implemented until an underlying policy, plan, or related requirement is addressed. 
 
2 
 
4. Non-housing-related items – worthwhile issues identified during the review that are not 
directly connected to housing affordability and will therefore receive lower priority. 
KLJ reported that the various lists submitted to date contained approximately 160 individual 
issues before duplicates were consolidated, with roughly 120 remaining after initial 
consolidation. 
The consultant intends to track the source and disposition of each issue so members can 
determine whether their concerns have been addressed. Draft amendments will be provided to 
the Task Force in manageable sections rather than as one large document at the end. 
The preliminary assessment and categorization are expected to be provided to City staff at the 
end of next week, followed by development of a recommendation matrix and annotated outline. 
Concern About Prioritizing the “Hard” Issues 
A significant portion of the discussion focused on ensuring that consultant resources are not 
consumed entirely by easy code-cleanup items. 
Task Force members noted that many of the true “low-hanging fruit” issues have already been 
addressed through the previous code update or earlier Task Force work. Members expressed 
concern that the most consequential housing barriers are often the most complicated and 
historically have been deferred because they involve difficult policy, engineering, operational, or 
community discussions. 
The group emphasized that failure to address the difficult, high-impact issues would be viewed 
as a significant weakness in the project, even if numerous minor amendments were completed. 
KLJ clarified that completing easier amendments first is intended to efficiently remove them 
from the workload—not to avoid the more difficult issues. The consultant indicated that the 
categorization matrix should allow the Task Force to review priorities and redirect resources 
toward the highest-impact items. 
Preliminary Land Development Code Findings 
KLJ identified several preliminary themes from its independent review: 
• Decision criteria are sometimes unclear or insufficiently measurable. 
• Relief mechanisms do not always provide relief for the situations they appear intended to 
address. 
• Related standards occasionally overlap or conflict. 
• Tools intended to provide flexibility can themselves create additional regulatory barriers. 
• Applicants cannot always determine requirements in advance. 
• Some standards depend on external documents, undefined triggers, or unclear 
terminology. 
 
3 
 
• Procedures and requirements should be simplified where possible to improve 
predictability and reduce repeated staff/applicant interactions. 
Examples discussed included buffer exceptions, major versus minor subdivision classifications, 
shared driveways, loop lanes, environmental review triggers, and other standards where the 
applicable process or requirement may not be apparent at the beginning of a project. 
TEDS Preliminary Review 
KLJ's transportation engineer presented preliminary observations from the TEDS review. Initial 
concerns included inconsistent terminology, missing or unclear definitions, organizational issues, 
references to outside standards, ADA/PROWAG requirements, curb ramps, raised intersections 
and crossings, pedestrian facilities, and limited guidance regarding treatments such as RRFBs 
and HAWK signals. 
Task Force members generally agreed that TEDS contains opportunities for improvement but 
cautioned against allowing the project to become a comprehensive rewrite of every technical 
issue in the manual. 
The Task Force reiterated that the priority should be TEDS requirements that materially affect 
housing cost, housing supply, development time, predictability, or unnecessary regulatory 
burden. 
Evaluation Criteria for Code and TEDS Changes 
Members suggested that KLJ apply a consistent set of questions when evaluating regulations. 
Key considerations discussed included: 
• Is the requirement critical for safety, rather than simply desirable or more comfortable? 
• Is the cost proportional to the benefit? 
• Is the requirement unnecessarily complex? 
• Is it enforceable? 
• Is it necessary to achieve the intended outcome? 
• Who receives the benefit and who bears the cost? 
• Does it unnecessarily reduce housing yield or development feasibility? 
• Does it increase review time or uncertainty? 
• Could the objective be achieved through an incentive or alternative rather than a 
mandatory standard? 
The distinction between something being critical for safety versus simply representing a 
preferred or higher design standard became an important theme, particularly in the discussion of 
TEDS.

Measuring Housing-Cost Impacts 
The Task Force strongly encouraged KLJ to quantify the effect of proposed changes wherever 
reasonably possible. 
Members discussed a goal of demonstrating meaningful savings—not merely minor reductions—
particularly in the cost of producing a finished residential lot. Potential measurements could 
include: 
• Direct construction-cost savings; 
• Engineering and consultant costs; 
• Reduction in review rounds; 
• Reduction in entitlement/development time; 
• Additional lots or units produced per acre; 
• Land consumed by infrastructure requirements; 
• Reduced infrastructure requirements; 
• Improved project feasibility; and 
• Increased housing supply. 
Members acknowledged that not every benefit can be converted into a precise dollar amount. For 
example, the value of reducing a project from eight or nine review rounds to three or four may be 
substantial but difficult to calculate precisely. 
KLJ suggested developing several representative development scenarios and testing proposed 
amendments against those scenarios. Rather than establishing one theoretical “average” 
development, the analysis could use several project types—for example, infill, conventional 
subdivision, and a more infrastructure-intensive development—to illustrate potential impacts. 
The Task Force also discussed providing real-world comparisons between similar projects in 
Grand Junction, Mesa County, Montrose, or other jurisdictions to demonstrate differences in 
cost, processing time, infrastructure requirements, and development feasibility. 
Housing Supply as a Measure of Success 
Members stressed that affordability cannot be evaluated only through direct construction savings. 
Regulations may also affect whether projects are undertaken at all. 
The group discussed situations in which developers may choose not to proceed in Grand Junction 
because of infrastructure costs, regulatory uncertainty, processing time, or project feasibility. 
Members noted that reducing these barriers can increase housing production and competition 
even when the savings cannot be directly traced to the sale price of a particular house. 
Accordingly, the consultant was encouraged to consider both cost reduction and increased 
housing supply when evaluating recommendations.

Sidewalks and Infrastructure Standards 
Sidewalk requirements were repeatedly used as an example of the broader policy discussion. 
Members questioned whether wider sidewalks are always necessary for safety and encouraged 
examination of alternatives such as narrower sidewalks, sidewalks on one side of a street, or 
different standards based on pedestrian volumes and development context. 
KLJ noted that recommendations must also consider ADA requirements, applicable engineering 
standards, long-term maintenance, and other external requirements. Where the Task Force's 
preferred approach differs from common practice or an industry standard, KLJ intends to 
explain: 
• What is legally or technically required; 
• What represents best practice rather than a mandate; 
• What alternatives are available; 
• What other communities are doing; and 
• What costs or tradeoffs accompany each alternative. 
The Task Force emphasized that national guidance or another community's practice should 
inform the discussion but should not automatically determine Grand Junction's standard. 
Process Improvements 
Members emphasized that development process reform is as important as individual code 
amendments. 
Examples discussed included the consolidation of relatively simple actions—such as replats or 
property-line adjustments—into broader subdivision procedures that may require significantly 
more information and review. 
The Task Force asked that process issues be explicitly incorporated into the consultant's review, 
including: 
• Process sequencing; 
• Required submittals; 
• Major/minor classifications; 
• Number of review rounds; 
• Agency coordination; 
• Predictability of staff decisions; and 
• Whether every existing requirement is necessary for the particular application. 
Members also acknowledged that staff has already begun implementing process improvements 
and reported examples of faster responses and greater willingness to discuss alternative 
approaches.

Additional Issues and Case Studies 
Several potential subjects for continued review were identified, including: 
• Shared driveways; 
• Cottage courts; 
• Landscaping requirements; Detention pond landscaping; 
• Utility requirements affecting private streets; 
• Survey requirements; 
• Minor subdivision procedures; 
• Infrastructure requirements affecting attainable housing; and 
• Alternative development models demonstrated in communities such as Silt and Montrose. 
Members encouraged KLJ to identify additional barriers that the Task Force may have missed, 
particularly where the consultant's previous experience with the City's regulations reveals 
opportunities for meaningful cost savings. 
Communication and Comment Tracking 
The group discussed how Task Force members should communicate comments between 
meetings. To maintain transparency and avoid concerns associated with group email discussions 
and open-meeting requirements, comments will generally be routed through Kimberly, rather 
than members engaging in extensive group email conversations. 
Comments and recommendations will be tracked in a spreadsheet/matrix so members can see 
how individual issues are being handled. 
The Task Force emphasized that the tracking system will be particularly important because 
substantial work must occur between formal meetings. 
Staff Input vs. Task Force Recommendations 
The group discussed how disagreements between staff and the Task Force will be handled. 
Members expressed concern about returning to a process where separate staff and Task Force 
positions effectively become competing documents late in the process. Instead, the expectation is 
that staff technical knowledge, consultant analysis, outside standards, and Task Force direction 
will be brought into the discussion before recommendations are finalized, minimizing surprises 
when recommendations reach Planning Commission or City Council. 
KLJ will provide independent professional analysis and may identify best practices, regulatory 
requirements, alternative approaches, and examples from other jurisdictions. The Task Force will 
then use that information to formulate its recommendation. 
 
7 
 
Where disagreement remains, the differing technical or policy considerations can be identified 
for the ultimate decision-makers rather than resolved through an informal filtering of the Task 
Force recommendation. 
Overall Measures of Success 
The discussion identified several measures by which members would consider the project 
successful: 
Reduce cost. Recommendations should materially reduce the cost of producing housing and 
finished lots where possible. 
Increase supply. Changes should make additional housing projects financially and operationally 
feasible. 
Reduce processing time. Fewer review rounds and clearer processes should reduce applicant 
and staff time. 
Increase predictability. Applicants should be able to understand requirements and likely 
outcomes before investing significant time and money. 
Address difficult issues. The project should not stop with minor cleanup amendments. 
Preserve necessary safety outcomes. Requirements that are genuinely critical to safety should 
remain, while requirements based primarily on preference or comfort should be evaluated against 
their cost. 
Provide evidence. Recommendations should be supported with costs, timelines, project 
examples, comparative standards, or other evidence wherever practical. 
Next Steps 
KLJ will complete the initial Assessment and Opportunity Analysis and organize Task Force 
issues into the four identified categories. The consultant will also begin preparing the 
recommendation matrix and annotated outline, with Task Force members receiving materials in 
smaller sections as work progresses. 
The Task Force will review the categorization to ensure that its priority issues—including the 
difficult and high-cost items—are appropriately represented. Members and local industry 
professionals will continue providing examples and cost information that can support the 
analysis, including potential landscape-architecture input and real-world project comparisons. 
KLJ will continue coordinating with staff while developing recommendations and will bring 
alternatives and technical considerations back to the Task Force before final drafting. Planning 
Commission and City Council workshops are anticipated as part of the next phase of the process.
