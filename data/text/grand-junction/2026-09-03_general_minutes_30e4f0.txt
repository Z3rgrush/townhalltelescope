Meeting Focus 
The Task Force focused primarily on organizing the body of issues identified through previous 
meetings and developing a framework for the final Task Force deliverable and 
recommendations. The discussion shifted from identifying individual code and process problems 
toward creating a systematic method for evaluating existing and future regulations, policies, and 
development processes through a housing affordability lens.  
Proposed Structure for the Final Task Force Report 
The group discussed an initial outline for the Task Force's final report. The proposed report 
would include suggested outline: 
• Purpose and Context  
o Connections to other docs  
o Scale of the problem  
o Importance of cost discussions to housing affordability 
o The compounding nature of the problem  
o The importance of scale and the need for profitability to drive that scale (The 
Comp Plan is a blunt instrument and doesn’t provide adequate guidance) 
o The disproportionate impact of housing costs 
o The focus on local action 
o Cost includes – Direct cost + time + Yield + Uncertainty + Risk 
• Problem Statement  
o Broad categories – 1 or 2 sentences on each 
o Subcategories – 1 or 2 sentences each 
o Matrix of individual problems 
• Solutions and Recommendations  
o Central “umbrella” recommendation – The Review – Test – Lens 
o Matrix of Recommendations 
• Acknowledgements, Thanks, Citations, etc.

The intent is to create a concise, visually understandable report rather than an extensive narrative 
document. 
Organization of Identified Problems 
Keith presented work consolidating the Task Force's sticky-note exercises and prior discussions 
into four broad categories. 
A. Process – Application, Review and Approval 
Issues include: 
• Process sequencing and whether reviews must occur sequentially or could occur 
concurrently.  
• Process requirements including unnecessary or overly burdensome steps.  
• Issues outside the immediate application that become the applicant's responsibility, such 
as boundary, survey, or historic property issues.  
• Internal City factors and staff capacity.  
• Legal and survey-related issues.  
• Other uncategorized process barriers.  
B. Regulation and Code-Making Process 
Issues include: 
• Use of local expertise.  
• Consideration of costs and trade-offs.  
• Whether regulations actually accomplish their intended purpose.  
• Whether regulatory mechanisms are practical, enforceable, and effective.  
C. Culture of the Code / Overreach 
Issues include: 
• Why experienced applicants continue to encounter inconsistent or difficult processes.  
• Situations where private development is required to bear costs associated with broader 
community benefits.  
• Whether regulations are written primarily to prevent undesirable outcomes rather than to 
affirmatively encourage desired outcomes.  
D. Extra-Jurisdictional Housing Challenges 
Issues include: 
• Third-party referral agencies.  
 
3 
 
• Coordination with Mesa County, neighboring jurisdictions, utilities, and other outside 
entities.  
• Barriers that affect housing production but may not be entirely within City control.  
The Task Force generally supported the organizational approach, with additional wordsmithing 
and refinement anticipated. 
Problem Matrix 
A matrix was presented that lists the individual problems identified by the Task Force and 
connects each issue to one or more categories. 
Rather than forcing an issue into a single category, the matrix allows an issue to be associated 
with multiple categories or subcategories. This was viewed as particularly useful because many 
development barriers involve overlapping process, regulatory, cultural, and outside-agency 
considerations. 
The matrix could ultimately be sorted or weighted based on the number of areas affected. This 
may help identify issues that warrant the greatest attention from the City, Task Force, staff, and 
consultant. 
The matrix is also intended to preserve the detailed examples collected by the Task Force while 
allowing the main report to remain concise. 
Proposed "Umbrella" Decision-Making Framework 
A substantial portion of the meeting focused on developing an overarching framework that could 
be used when: 
• creating new regulations;  
• amending existing regulations;  
• evaluating development processes;  
• reviewing existing Code provisions; and  
• making policy decisions.  
The framework is not intended to automatically produce a particular outcome. Instead, it is 
intended to require a more disciplined and transparent conversation before a decision is made. 
The emerging questions include: 
What problem are we trying to solve? 
Clearly identify the actual problem before creating a regulatory response. 
Is it critical for safety? 
The group discussed distinguishing regulations addressing genuine safety concerns from 
 
4 
 
requirements primarily intended to advance general community welfare, comfort, aesthetics, or 
aspirational objectives. 
Why is the regulation/process necessary? 
If the issue is not clearly safety-related, greater attention should be given to the rationale for 
intervention. 
Have we utilized appropriate local expertise? 
Relevant practitioners and affected stakeholders should help identify practical consequences and 
alternatives. 
What are the trade-offs? 
Consider: 
• direct cost;  
• time;  
• housing yield;  
• flexibility;  
• uncertainty and risk;  
• applicant/developer burden; and  
• City staffing, administration, and enforcement costs.  
Will it work? 
Determine whether the regulation is: 
• practical;  
• legally supportable;  
• enforceable; and  
• likely to achieve its intended outcome.  
Is it equitable? 
Explicitly consider who pays and who benefits. Particular attention should be given where 
private development bears costs for benefits accruing broadly to the community. 
Is it scalable/flexible? 
Requirements should account for differences in project size, context, use, and impact rather than 
necessarily applying the same standard universally. 
Are there alternatives? 
Consider whether the desired result could be achieved through another method, including an 
incentive rather than a mandate. 
Regulatory "Stick" vs. Incentive "Carrot" 
 
5 
 
The group discussed a general principle that requirements addressing critical safety concerns are 
more likely to justify mandatory regulation. 
Where a requirement primarily addresses general welfare, aesthetics, comfort, or aspirational 
community objectives, the City should more deliberately consider whether an incentive-based 
approach may be more appropriate. 
The Task Force emphasized that this should not become a rigid decision tree. There will be 
circumstances where the distinction is unclear. The value of the framework is that it forces the 
underlying rationale and trade-offs to be discussed rather than assumed. 
Examples discussed included sight-distance requirements, sidewalk widths, parking lot islands, 
detention pond landscaping, irrigation requirements, and pre-application processes. 
"Best Practice" Is Not Sufficient by Itself 
The Task Force discussed concerns with adopting requirements simply because they are 
described as a "best practice" or because another jurisdiction has adopted them. 
Practices from other communities may still be useful, but the group suggested they should be 
evaluated through the proposed framework to determine whether they: 
• address Grand Junction's specific problem;  
• make sense under local conditions;  
• produce the desired result;  
• create reasonable costs and trade-offs; and  
• support housing affordability.  
The framework therefore would not prohibit best practices but would require additional 
justification before translating them into local regulation. 
Comprehensive Plan and Code Relationship 
The Task Force had significant discussion regarding the role of the Comprehensive Plan. 
There was general recognition that alignment with the Comprehensive Plan should not, by itself, 
be sufficient justification for a particular regulatory approach. Multiple regulatory alternatives 
may all advance the same Comprehensive Plan objective while producing very different costs 
and housing outcomes. 
Some members characterized the issue as the Comprehensive Plan becoming too regulatory; 
others suggested the underlying problem is how the Plan has been interpreted and used to justify 
Code provisions. 
 
6 
 
An emerging concept was that the Comprehensive Plan should remain largely aspirational and 
provide policy direction, while specific regulations should still be evaluated for: 
• necessity;  
• effectiveness;  
• trade-offs;  
• equity;  
• alternatives; and  
• housing affordability impacts.  
The Task Force also noted that the upcoming Comprehensive Plan update creates an opportunity 
to identify policies that are unintentionally contributing to regulatory barriers. 
Housing Affordability Must Remain Central 
Members emphasized that the Task Force's specific charge is housing affordability. 
Accordingly, the proposed framework should explicitly ask: 
• How does this requirement affect housing affordability?  
• What does it add to the cost of housing?  
• What community benefit is received in exchange?  
• Who bears the cost?  
• Is the benefit sufficient to justify that cost?  
• Could the same objective be accomplished through a less costly or more flexible 
approach?  
The group noted that the framework could ultimately have broader value for City policymaking, 
but the Task Force's application of it should remain grounded in its housing affordability 
mandate. 
Staff Collaboration and Administrative Culture 
Members discussed the importance of improving collaboration between applicants and City staff, 
particularly given current staffing constraints and the introduction of newer employees. 
Discussion emphasized that many barriers are individually small but collectively contribute to 
delay, uncertainty, and expense. Examples included plat execution requirements, document 
revisions, survey issues, and differences in interpretation among staff. 
The Task Force discussed the need to: 
• improve consistency;  
• provide appropriate staff discretion;  
• establish criteria for exceptions or alternative compliance;  
 
7 
 
• help staff understand the real-world consequences of seemingly minor requirements;  
• avoid an "us versus them" dynamic; and  
• ensure that discretion does not simply become permission to disregard Code 
requirements.  
The goal is a system that empowers staff to exercise reasonable judgment within defined criteria 
while maintaining predictability and fairness. 
Value for Planning Commission and City Council 
The proposed framework was also viewed as potentially useful for decision makers. 
Members expressed concern that existing hearing criteria can focus heavily on whether a 
proposal "aligns" with adopted plans without providing enough structure for evaluating: 
• effectiveness;  
• affordability;  
• competing interests;  
• costs and benefits;  
• equity; and  
• alternatives.  
A more standardized framework could improve staff reports and policy discussions by giving 
Planning Commission and City Council a clearer basis for weighing competing considerations. 
The group acknowledged that the framework will not remove politics or disagreement from 
policymaking. Instead, its purpose is to make the reasoning and trade-offs more explicit and 
transparent. 
Consultant Coordination 
The consultant kickoff is scheduled for September 11. 
The Task Force identified an opportunity to provide the consultant with: 
• the categorized problem list;  
• the detailed issue matrix;  
• previous TEDS subcommittee work;  
• the Code issue spreadsheet maintained by staff;  
• specific examples of development barriers; and  
• the emerging decision-making framework.  
Because the consultant already has familiarity with the City's Code, members believe providing 
this organized information could reduce time spent identifying known problems and allow the 
consultant to focus more quickly on potential solutions. 
 
8 
 
The consultant's work may also identify situations where a Code provision cannot readily be 
changed without addressing an underlying policy issue. 
Direction / Consensus 
The Task Force generally supported: 
1. Continuing with the proposed structure for the final report.  
2. Using the four broad problem categories and associated subcategories.  
3. Maintaining the detailed issue matrix as supporting documentation.  
4. Developing the decision-making "test" or "lens" as a major umbrella recommendation.  
5. Making housing affordability an explicit component of that framework.  
6. Refining the framework so it is understandable without requiring someone to explain it 
verbally.  
7. Testing the framework against several real-world examples.  
8. Considering opposing viewpoints/"devil's advocate" testing to ensure the framework does 
not predetermine outcomes.  
9. Providing the emerging framework and issue inventory to the consultant.
