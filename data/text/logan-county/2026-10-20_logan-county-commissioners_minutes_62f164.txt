PRAIRIE DOGS – Commissioner Santomaso reported that all three Commissioners were on a Z oom call last 
week with the Colorado Parks and Wildlife Commission concerning prairie dogs. The CPW received  a petition 
from a pro prairie dog group that wants to stop all prairie dog control on public lands. CPW usually takes these 
matters up between January and March. The Parks and Wildlife Commission at their meeting overruled what CPW 
wanted to do and moved it up to November. The CPW’s paper comes out on October 26th or 29th, one day ahead 
of ahead when public comments are due.  
There is a CPW meeting in Fort Morgan on November 4 and 5. Commissioner Yahn will be attending along with 
 
 
2 
Commissioners Brownell and Santomaso on one or both days. This does not affect private lands, but it does affect 
public lands. CCI has a form letter template that can be edited. Representative Dusty Johnson is here if people 
would like to discuss this issue with her. Written comments can be submitted through Engagecpw.com before 
October 29, 2026, at 11:59 p.m.  
 
Commissioner Yahn said the proposal concerns recreational prairie dog hunting on federal and state lands. It does 
not yet cover State Land Board lands, but he said that would be the next step. Echoing Commissioner Santomaso, 
he described the expedited process as far outside normal procedures. CPW is attempting a stakeholder process. At 
the last CPW meeting, where public comment was halted, he asked whether local voices would be heard. He was 
assured that elected officials attending the Fort Morgan meeting could speak  first, followed by the public . He 
encouraged anyone experiencing prairie dog problems to attend. He said the proposal lacks a  plan to compensate 
adjoining landowners if prairie dogs move onto their property from state or federal lands where hunting has stopped. 
He also questioned CPW’s disease management practices, noting that some colonies have been inoculated against 
plague, which he described as an important natural check on prairie dog populations. CPW reported that the 
population is stable, but county commissioners across Colorado have reported rapid growth, with prairie dogs 
overrunning land in the northeast, southwest, southeast, and northwest regions.   
 
Commissioner Yahn said  a neighbor does not control prairie dogs , resulting in severe wind erosion that carries  
topsoil from the neighbor’s property onto his land. He said prairie dogs undermine the fragile prairie ecosystem 
and urged people to speak out, although he questioned whether doing so would influence the Wildlife Commission. 
He said CPW staff  on the Zoom call  shared the commissioners’ concerns but characterized  Governor Polis’s 
appointees to the Wildlife Commission as supporters of “rewilding” who want the eastern plains returned to prairie 
dogs and buffalo  rather than human use. He expressed concern that this outlook extends to all commissions 
appointed during Governor Polis’s tenure.  
 
Commissioner Brownell stated that he contacted the  local State Land Board office. They are no longer enforcing 
prairie dog control on state leased land because of the new director. The new director was shown what damage they 
have caused to the land and she’s not concerned. The Governor’s Board appointees have also had an influence. 
 
Commissioner Yahn reported that Colorado responded to Nebraska’s Supreme Court complaint alleging 
noncompliance with the interstate compact. Colorado denied all allegations, including that well pumping prevents 
it from delivering the required summer water flows to Nebraska. Colorado maintained that its method for  
calculating wells’ effects on the river is scientifically sound. It  also denied interfering with Nebraska’s ability to 
build the Perkins County Canal, and Commissioner Yahn affirmed that Colorado is not doing so. 
 
Nebraska claims that Colorado has hired actors  to stand in the way of building the Perkins County Canal. They 
named Lower South Platte Water Conservancy District  as a group that's been formed to make sure that Nebraska 
complies with the compact as agents of Colorado against Nebraska's building of the canal, which Colorado denies. 
Lastly, was their complaint that the river should be easily administered, and when we do augmentation plans, it 
makes it too complicated, and it shouldn't be that way. Colorado denied all of those.  
 
Colorado filed a counterclaim against Nebraska . Nebraska allows wells to pump without any replacement to 
Lodgepole Creek, which comes down from Nebraska into the South Platte. This is in violation of the compact and 
puts a burden on Colorado water users to meet that compact because Nebraska is taking it out of priority. There are 
about 30 wells on Lodgepole Creek and 19 wells on the South Platte that affect the river in Colorado. Colorado’s 
counter complaint says Nebraska must shut all those wells off. This could be a big deal for some of the people  in 
Nebraska if they get shut off on Lodge pole Creek and the South Platte River unless they do some augmentation. 
Nebraska will have a chance to counter what Colorado has said.
