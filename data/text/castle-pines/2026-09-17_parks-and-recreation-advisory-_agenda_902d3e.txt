TO: Parks and Recreation Advisory Board Members 
 
FROM: Marcus Graves, Parks, Recreation, and Open Space Director 
 
DATE: September 17, 2026 
 
RE: Trail Mile Marker Spacing Recommendation 
 
 
Policy Question: Does the Parks and Recreation Advisory Board (PRAB) support staff’s 
recommendation to adopt Option 3, trail signage at the start, midpoint, and end of each trail 
segment, as the mile marker approach for the Castle Pines trail network? 
 
Background: The City of Castle Pines (the “City”) has identified 35 trail segments throughout 
the community. Since October 2025, PRAB has led a multi-phase effort to improve trail 
wayfinding, beginning with a trail naming initiative. To date, City Council has approved official 
names for 21 of the 35 trail segments, six in October 2025 and fifteen in March 2026, with the 
remaining segments to be named in a future phase. 
 
With trail naming underway, PRAB turned to trail signage. At its March 19, 2026 meeting, PRAB 
was tasked with evaluating trail signage styles, identifying key signage locations, and 
developing recommendations for City Council over the following months. On August 20, 2026, 
PRAB reviewed conceptual sign designs prepared by DTJ Design for three categories of trail 
signage: Trailhead Kiosk/Shelter (area map and orientation signage at park and neighborhood 
entry points), Trail Directional Signage (fingerpost-style signage at trail junctions, splits, and 
street crossings), and Trail Mile Markers (periodic wayfinding markers placed along trail 
corridors). For each sign type, PRAB reviewed two material and style options, a Wood Post 
style and a Corten (weathering steel) style, both incorporating the City’s brushed stainless-steel 
logo and lettering. 
 
PRAB’s August discussion focused on the trailhead kiosk and directional sign designs. This 
report returns to PRAB to resolve the remaining open question from that discussion: how 
frequently mile markers should be placed along the trail network. Board members have raised 
concerns about visual clutter from signs along trails and about the cost of trail signage, 
particularly as the program is scaled across the City’s 35 trail segments. To evaluate these 
concerns directly, staff mapped all three spacing options across the full trail network; those 
maps are included as Exhibits 1–3. Trail crossing signage (signage at street and roadway 
crossings) is being evaluated separately and will be brought to PRAB at a future meeting. 
 
Staff Analysis: Mile markers serve three main purposes: (1) helping trail users gauge distance 
traveled and remaining, (2) supporting emergency response by giving dispatchers and public 
safety personnel a consistent reference system for locating trail users, and (3) reinforcing 
consistent branding and wayfinding across the trail network. Accepted trail-design guidance 
(e.g., American Trails and accessible trail design resources) commonly cites quarter-mile

spacing as effective for wayfinding and accessibility, while also cautioning against “sign 
pollution”, the visual clutter created by over-signing a corridor. Communities balance these two 
considerations differently depending on trail context, sign design, and budget; there is no single 
national standard that applies to every trail system. Staff evaluated three types of mile marker 
spacing options for the Castle Pines trail network: 
 
● Option 1: Mile Markers Every ¼ Mile (Exhibit 1): Provides the most frequent, precise 
distance reference and the most granular location reference for emergency response. 
Mapped across the network, this option places roughly 65–70 markers on the trail 
system, by far the densest of the three options, and the clearest driver of both the 
Board’s sign-clutter concern and the highest total program cost. 
● Option 2: Mile Markers Every ½ Mile (Exhibit 2): Reduces marker count to roughly 25–30 
across the network, less than half of Option 1, while still providing regular, evenly spaced 
distance and location reference. A widely used middle-ground spacing approach, though 
it still adds a substantial number of new fixtures to the trail corridor beyond what exists 
today. 
● Option 3: Signage at Trail Start, Midpoint, and End Only (Exhibit 3): Adds one marker at 
the midpoint of each trail segment, supplementing the start/end signage already planned 
as part of the trail naming and wayfinding program. This is the lowest sign count and 
lowest cost of the three options by a wide margin and keeps the visual character of the 
trail system closest to how it looks and feels today. 
 
Across all three options, mile marker materials, mounting, and lettering would remain consistent 
with the trailhead kiosk and directional signage styles already reviewed by PRAB (Wood Post or 
Corten), maintaining a unified appearance across the trail system. 
 
Fiscal Impact: Option 3 adds only one marker per trail segment rather than one every quarter- 
or half-mile, it requires the fewest fixtures to fabricate and install, roughly one-third the marker 
count of Option 1 and well under half the count of Option 2 and can be completed in a shorter 
installation timeframe. Staff has not yet finalized unit pricing, as that depends on the final sign 
material (Wood Post or Corten), but the reduced fixture count under Option 3 stretches the trail 
signage budget further and allows more of it to go toward higher-quality, more intentional 
signage at each location rather than being spread across a larger number of markers. 
 
Staff Recommendation: Staff recommends that PRAB support Option 3, trail signage at the 
start, midpoint, and end of each trail segment, as the City’s mile marker approach. This 
recommendation is based on the following: 
 
● Minimizes sign clutter: Option 3 adds the fewest new fixtures to the trail corridor of the 
three options, directly addressing the sign-pollution concerns. 
● Preserves the trail character residents know today: because it adds far fewer signs than 
Options 1 or 2, Option 3 keeps the trail system closer to its current look and feel rather 
than introducing a dense new layer of signage. 
● Still upgrades wayfinding: start, midpoint, and end markers represent a meaningful 
improvement in location identification over the trail network’s current signage, even 
without quarter- or half-mile spacing. 
● Complements the directional signage already planned: trail directional (fingerpost) signs 
at junctions, splits, and street crossings will add their own wayfinding footprint along the 
corridor; layering frequent mile markers on top of that additional signage would 
compound clutter rather than improve wayfinding.

RE: Soaring Hawk Park Phasing Sequence Recommendation 
 
Policy Question: Does the Parks and Recreation Advisory Board (PRAB) support staff’s 
recommended phasing approach for Soaring Hawk Park, beginning with combined horizontal 
construction and initial amenities, trails, pickleball and tennis, followed by the athletic field and 
playground in the sequence presented? 
 
Background: The City of Castle Pines (the “City”) is advancing design and construction planning 
for Soaring Hawk Park. The park’s amenities were established through the adopted Soaring Hawk 
Park master plan and multiple years of community engagement and are not in question as part of 
this discussion; the four amenity groups , athletic field and parking, trails and natural area, 
pickleball and tennis, and playground, fitness and bike park, remain unchanged from the plan the 
community and PRAB have already shaped. 
 
The City retained Hord | Coplan | Macht (HCM) as landscape architect for the project. HCM has 
prepared a schematic design, “Potential Option A” (November 2025), for Soaring Hawk Park 
West, along with two preliminary initial -phase construction strategies for staff and PRAB to 
consider. 
 
Soaring Hawk Park buildout involves two kinds of construction: site- wide horizontal construction 
(mass grading, stormwater detention and drainage, utility service extensions, and primary 
roadway/parking sub-base) that is built once, and amenity-specific vertical construction (buildings, 
court surfacing, playground equipment, and field turf) that is built in phases. Staff has been 
evaluating how best to sequence these two kinds of work. 
 
This report presents staff’s recommended phasing approach, which blends elements of HCM’s 
preliminary strategies with the goal of advancing site- wide infrastructure while also delivering a 
usable amenity to the community in the initial phase.

Staff Analysis: Staff evaluated two preliminary initial-phase strategies proposed by HCM and is 
recommending an approach that blends elements of both. The recommended phasing 
sequence is as follows: 
 
• Phase 1 — Horizontal Construction + Trails and Courts: Site-wide grading, utilities, and 
detention, paired with west-side trails and pickleball/tennis courts, the first amenities the 
community can use. 
• Phase 2 — Athletic Field and Parking: Multi-use turf field with lighting, primary 120-
space parking lot, and restroom/athletics storage building. 
• Phase 3 — Playground, Fitness and Bike Park: Inclusive playgrounds, fitness/challenge 
course, toddler pump track, and secondary parking. 
Pairing Phase 1’s infrastructure work with the trails and courts avoids a second mobilization for 
those amenities and ensures the community has a usable amenity as soon as the initial phase 
is complete, while the athletic field and playground, the two largest single-amenity builds, follow 
as their own phases. 
Fiscal Impact: Cost estimates for each phase are still being developed by the consultant team 
and have not yet been finalized. Staff will present a detailed fiscal analysis, including phase-by-
phase cost estimates and funding sources, at a future PRAB and City Council meeting once 
quantities and constructability (grading, drainage, and utilities) have been validated. 
 
Staff Recommendation:  Staff recommends that PRAB support the recommended phasing 
approach for Soaring Hawk Park , beginning with combined horizontal construction and initial 
amenities (trails, pickleball and tennis), followed by the athletic field and playground in the 
sequence presented. 
 
Proposed Motion: "I MOVE TO SUPPORT STAFF’S RECOMMENDED PHASING APPROACH 
FOR SOARING HAWK PARK, BEGINNING WITH COMBINED HORIZONTAL CONSTRUCTION 
AND INITIAL AMENITIES, FOLLOWED BY THE ATHLETIC FIELD AND PLAYGROUND IN THE 
SEQUENCE PRESENTED.”
