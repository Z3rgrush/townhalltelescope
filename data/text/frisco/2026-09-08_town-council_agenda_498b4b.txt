Summary & Background: 
At the September 8, 2026 meeting, the Finance Department will present its annual 
review of the Town's cash and investment portfolio. The account-level holdings 
schedule reports a market value of $21,240,418 as of July 31, 2026. The presentation 
summarizes where funds are held, the portfolio's liquidity and maturity profile, security 
composition, credit and call features, and performance relative to the applicable U.S. 
Treasury benchmark. 
Town Council adopted the current Investment Policy in January 2026. The policy 
establishes safety of principal as the foremost objective, followed by liquidity and then 
return. It also requires an annual Council review of the portfolio, including holdings, 
market value, investment performance, and progress toward the Town's investment 
objectives and goals. 
 
Analysis: 
1. Investment Objectives and Policy Framework 
The portfolio is managed to preserve public funds, maintain sufficient liquidity for 
operating and capital needs, and earn a market rate of return within the Town's legal and 
policy constraints. Consistent with the presentation's investment-objective pyramid, the 
policy places safety and liquidity ahead of yield. Eligible instruments include U.S. 
Treasury obligations, federal instrumentality securities, local government investment 
pools, certificates of deposit, repurchase agreements, money market mutual funds, 
commercial paper, bankers acceptances, and certain municipal securities, subject to the 
policy's maturity, rating, diversification, custody, and counterparty requirements. 
2. Portfolio Holdings and Liquidity 
The account-level schedule in the annual review shows the following holdings as of July

The ratings dashboard shows 64% AA+ and 36% not rated (NR) for the non-liquid portion

4. Investment Performance 
The presentation reports a weighted average yield of 3.96% for securities and certificates 
of deposit and 3.85% for the total portfolio including liquid balances. The 2026 year-to-
date average for the one-year U.S. Treasury is shown at 3.75%. On that basis, the total 
portfolio exceeded the stated benchmark by approximately 10 basis points while retaining 
a high level of liquidity. This result is consistent with the policy objective of earning a 
market rate of return without compromising safety or cash availability. 
5. Governance and Oversight 
The Investment Policy provides the governance framework for the portfolio. It applies the 
prudent-person standard, requires adherence to the Town's ethics and conflict-of-interest 
rules, establishes approved broker/dealer and bank criteria, requires safekeeping and 
custody controls, and assigns policy administration to the Finance Director with support 
from authorized finance staff. The annual presentation to Town Council is the policy's 
primary mechanism for regular portfolio reporting and public transparency. 
 
Financial Impact: 
No budget amendment, appropriation, or change in investment authority is requested. 
Investment income from pooled cash supports the General Fund and other participating 
Town accounts. Under the Investment Policy, pooled investment income is allocated 
based on each participating account's proportionate average balance. The annual review 
reports portfolio yields and market values; it does not propose a change to the Town's 
current investment strategy. 
 
Alignment with Strategic Plan:  
The annual investment review supports the Town's broader goals for fiscal 
sustainability, transparent governance, and reliable delivery of municipal services. 
Preserving principal and maintaining ready access to cash helps ensure that adopted 
operating and capital commitments can be met, while competitive investment earnings 
reduce pressure on other Town resources. 
 
Sustainability: 
Section VII of the January 2026 Investment Policy directs staff to consider environmental, 
social, and governance (ESG) goals when an eligible investment offers substantially 
equivalent safety, liquidity, and return. Within those fiduciary and legal constraints, the 
policy encourages investment in companies with positive environmental, human-rights, 
fair-workplace, and equal-rights practices. It discourages investment in entities that 
receive a significant portion of revenue from the manufacture of tobacco products, 
firearms, or weapons not used in national defense. 
The ESG provision functions as a preference within the Town's authorized investment 
universe; it does not override the policy's primary objectives or Colorado law. The current

RE:  2027 BUDGET DISCUSSION

Summary & Background: 
The 2027 Proposed Budget provides an important opportunity for thoughtful discussion 
about Frisco’s future. Each year, the Town’s budget serves not only as a financial plan 
but also as a roadmap for implementing Council’s vision. Staff is pleased to present the 
attached budget documents for Council’s review and input at the September 8 work 
session. Feedback from this meeting will guide the final appropriations scheduled for 
first reading on October 13 and second reading on October 27. In preparing this 
proposal, Staff has ensured that the budget is balanced as required and continues to 
strive toward meeting Council-established reserve levels. 
 
Analysis: 
As with every budget cycle, the foundation for the 2027 Proposed Budget is Council’s 
vision and direction. The Town’s Strategic Plan has guided decision-making, 
emphasizing four key priorities: 
Quality Core Services – Ensuring the Town continues to deliver dependable, efficient, 
and high-quality services for residents, businesses, and visitors. 
Thriving Economy – Supporting tourism while diversifying and strengthening Frisco’s 
year-round economy. 
Community Inclusivity – Promoting a welcoming and supportive environment where 
all residents and visitors feel connected and valued. 
Vibrant Culture, Arts, and Recreation – Sustaining Frisco’s character as a mountain 
community that values recreation, creativity, and quality of life.

Over the past year, Town Council has further solidified long-term priorities by adopting a 
10-Year Capital Improvement Program, a 10-Year Housing Plan, and is currently 
in the middle of a future water rate study that will solidify the capital program for 
the Town’s water system for several years. These adopted plans are fully integrated 
into the 2026 Proposed Budget, aligning near-term decisions with long-term goals. 
Together, these frameworks ensure the Town is not just reacting to short-term 
challenges or desires, but methodically preparing for future needs in housing, 
infrastructure, and community amenities. 
Staff extends its gratitude to Town Council for the significant time and energy already 
dedicated to these planning efforts, which provided the guidance necessary to prepare 
a budget that is both resilient and responsive. 
Financial Impact: 
The economic outlook presents both opportunities and challenges: 
• Local economy: Sales tax collections through July 2026 are up 1% compared to 
last year, while lodging tax revenues are down 0.1%, primarily due to shifts in the 
short-term rental market. 
• State economy: Colorado continues to see modest growth, with gains 
concentrated in health care, construction, and professional services, though 
mountain tourism has slowed compared to prior years. 
• National context: Consumer uncertainty remains around inflation, tariffs, and 
federal policies—all of which affect tourism-driven economies like Frisco’s. 
 
While revenues are projected to remain flat between 2024 and 2026, the Town’s careful 
stewardship allows for stability and continued investment in community priorities. The 
2027 Proposed Budget totals $67,464,393 in appropriations and is structurally 
balanced, as required by Town Code. This has been achieved through a responsible 
mix of current revenues, the strategic use of fund balances, and conservative 
expenditure estimates. 
Although the 2027 Proposed Budget includes modest reductions from 2025 levels, 
these adjustments have been designed to preserve core services and ensure that 
residents and visitors continue to experience the high-quality amenities and programs 
that define Frisco. The Town’s strong reserves, deliberate financial management, and 
relative economic strength position Frisco well to navigate uncertainty while continuing 
to make progress on long-term goals. 
 
Alignment with Strategic Plan:  
The 2027 Proposed Budget is not simply a financial ledger—it is the primary tool for 
implementing the Town Council’s Strategic Plan. Every program, project, and initiative 
has been reviewed against the Strategic Plan’s four core priorities to ensure alignment.

Sustainability: 
There is no direct environmental impact from this work session. However, the 2027 
budget process will include review of capital projects, operational needs, and long- term 
investments that may support sustainability, infrastructure resiliency, and efficient  Town 
operations. 
 
Staff Recommendation: 
The September 8 work session will allow Staff and Council to review the budget in detail, 
considering not only financial trends but also the community impacts of the proposed 
priorities. Council will also continue budget discussions at the September 22 meeting, 
including review of non-profit discretionary grants and the Capital Improvement Program. 
The 2027 Proposed Budget balances fiscal responsibility with strategic investment, 
positioning Frisco to maintain high-quality services, support community priorities, and 
continue thriving even in a period of flat revenues. Staff looks forward to Council’s input 
to help finalize a budget that truly reflects the values and vision of the community.

against future revenues of the Town.  This type of funding is restricted to capital 
improvements and is not to be used for current or annual operations.  The following 
are summaries of the Town’s debt as of the end of 2025. 
 
The Town of Frisco issued C ertificates of P articipation (COPs) on May 25, 2002 in 
the principal amount of $2,500,000 for the purpose of financing improvements to 
the Frisco Bay Lakefront Park and Marina, other parks and recreation projects and 
other capital improvements.  T he Town refunded the 2002 COPs  at the par amount 
of $1,950,000 (Series 2010A) and issue d new Series 2010B COPs at the par amount 
of $2,500,000, for a total of $4,450,000 , on June 11, 2010 for the purpose of 
financing the Frisco Adventure Park  on Town- owned property known as the 
Peninsula Recreation Area (PRA).   The tax -exempt refunded bonds have a true 
interest cost of 3.44% with final maturity of 12/1/2021.  The newly issued 2010B

35% subsidy) with final maturity of 12/1/2030.

Cash Basis Financial Summary Section
This section provides a high-level snapshot of the Town of Frisco’s financial activity using cash basis 
accounting. Under this method, revenues and expenditures are recorded only when cash is received 
or paid, rather than when they are earned or incurred. This provides a clear, real-time view of available 
funds and the Town’s current financial position.
This summary is different from the year-over-year business activity section earlier in the report. That 
section looks at sales tax based on when sales happen, while this one focuses on when the Town 
actually receives the money—giving a real-time look at how collections are tracking against the budget.
This summary is designed to:
•Track revenue based on actual cash received.
•Support monthly budget monitoring and cash flow analysis.
•Complement economic activity data presented in the earlier sales tax trend comparison section.
Understanding the timing of tax collection versus remittance is key to interpreting the data in this report. 
Key Distinction
•Collected = When the customer pays tax to the business. 
•Remitted = When the business sends that tax to the Town. 
•When: At the time of sale to the customer.
•What Happens: A business charges and collects tax at the point of sale (in-store, online, etc.).
•Example: A $100 purchase with an 8% sales tax results in $8 collected from the customer by the business at 
checkout and held until the tax is remitted to the government.
•When: Typically by the 20th of the following month (depending on the business’ filing frequency).
•What Happens: The business submits the collected tax to the State and to the Town.
•Example: Sales tax collected in January is generally remitted to the Town in February.
•Businesses temporarily hold collected tax until their designated remittance deadline.
•As a result, the Town’s revenue generally lags behind actual sales activity by at least one month.
•For example, January shows $0 because those taxes (from January sales) are remitted in February.
•“Period 13” refers to November and December tax received in January and February but recorded as prior-year 
revenue to align with the year when the sales occurred, per Generally Accepted Accounting Principles.
•The following pages reflect when actual tax payments are received by the Town, not when the original sale 
happened. This is important for comparing monthly cash receipts against budget projections.
