A. Mayor Huelskamp updated the Trustees a salary survey that is underway and notified them 
that the Town has vacancies on the Planning and Zoning Commission, he is seeking 
suggestions for candidates.

discussed several of the projects but did not offer any direction or changes at this time.

response rate to exceed the 20% target by the time it closes.  A draft presentation will be 
offered at the October meeting.

decision.

Request for Board of Trustee Action 
Date:     October 20, 2026 
Title:  2027 Town Budget Public Hearing 
Presented By:    J.D. McCrumb, Town Administrator  
Prepared By:  J.D. McCrumb, Town Administrator 
Background: In Columbine Valley, the process of adopting an annual municipal 
budget is not simply an internal administrative decision: it must 
include a public hearing at which interested electors may comment or 
object. The public hearing ensures transparency, accountability, and 
provides a formal mechanism for citizen input before adoption. 
Per C.R.S. § 29-1-103, every local government must adopt an annual 
budget. The budget must present a complete financial plan by fund 
and include proposed expenditures, anticipated revenues, beginning 
and ending fund balances, and comparative figures from prior years.  
 
The statute also prohibits adopting a budget that provides for 
expenditures in excess of available revenues plus beginning fund 
balance. The budget must include a “budget message” describing the 
major features and the basis of accounting.  
 
After the proposed budget is prepared, the governing body must 
publish notice informing the public of a hearing at which adoption 
will be considered per C.R.S. § 29-1-108. The governing body must 
hold a hearing to consider adoption of the proposed budget and must 
consider objections of electors.  
 
Recommended Motion:  “I move to set a public hearing on the 2027 Annual Town Budget for 
Tuesday, November 17, 2026, as part of the consent agenda. 

 
Request for Board of Trustee Action 
Date:     October 20, 2026 
Title:  2027 Housing Needs Assessment Public Hearing 
Presented By:    J.D. McCrumb, Town Administrator  
Prepared By:  J.D. McCrumb, Town Administrator 
Background: Colorado Senate Bill 24-174, codified in part at C.R.S. § 24-32-3703, 
requires local governments to conduct and publish a Housing Needs 
Assessment by December 31, 2026, and update the assessment at 
least every six years. The assessment must follow the methodology 
established by the Colorado Department of Local Affairs and address 
housing inventory, demographic and economic conditions, housing 
needs, displacement risk, and potential responses. 
Pursuant to C.R.S. § 24-32-3703(3), the governing body must 
consider the completed assessment at a public meeting. Within 60 
days of that consideration, the Town must submit the assessment, 
together with any comments from the Board of Trustees, to the 
Colorado Department of Local Affairs. 
Although the statute does not specifically require a public hearing, 
Town staff recommends holding one to provide residents and other 
interested parties a formal opportunity to review and comment on the 
proposed assessment before its completion. The public hearing will 
also allow the Board to consider community input and identify any 
final revisions necessary before formal consideration in December. 
The public hearing is proposed for November 17, 2026, with formal 
Board consideration of the completed Housing Needs Assessment 
scheduled for December 8, 2026. 
Recommended Motion: “I move to set a public hearing on the Town of Columbine Valley's 
2026 Housing Needs Assessment for Tuesday, November 17, 2026.”

Town Administrator’s Report • October 2026 
Public Works Updates 
New Hire On Board 
James Register was hired to fill the vacancy of the part-
time Public Works Technician. With his background in 
construction, he was able to quickly begin contributing 
to field operations and maintenance activities with min-
imal ramp-up time. James has already proven to be a 
valuable addition to the Public Works team, assisting 
with inspections, maintenance, project oversight, and 
other day-to-day Town needs. 
Project List Reviewed & Updated 
In conjunction with hiring the new part-time Public 
Works Technician, the annual Public Works project list 
was reviewed and updated. The updated list identified 
111 outstanding projects and maintenance items await-
ing completion throughout the Town. The additional 
staffing provided by James has already increased the 
department’s capacity to address these items. He has 
begun completing several projects from the backlog 
while also assisting with routine maintenance, inspec-
tions, and ongoing Public Works operations. 
Intrepid Fiber Right-of-Way Permit  
A comprehensive 
right-of-way permit 
was prepared  
for Intrepid Fiber. 
The permit author-
ized Phase 1 work 
within the public 
right of way, includ-
ing installation of fiber-optic cable, handholes, flower-
pots, potholing, and associated restoration work along 
approved routes.  
• Phases 2 and 3 have not yet been approved.  
• Phase 2 was planned to address the ditch and lateral 
crossings once the necessary agreements are in place 
(shown above).  
• Phase 3 was planned to cover work within Wild  
Plum after the development is formally accepted by 
the Town. 
Read to Plow   
The Expedition plow received seasonal maintenance 
and repairs to prepare it for snow operations.  
• To address leaks in the hydraulic system, the plow 
received a complete hydraulic system overhaul, in-
cluding replacement of the cylinders, hoses, fluid,  
and seals.  
• The headlight assemblies were replaced due to yel-
lowing and cracking of the existing units.  
• The wiring harnesses were upgraded with heavier-
duty wiring and connectors to reduce the potential  
for electrical shorts.  
• All bolted connections were cleaned and retorqued, 
replacing any bent or damaged bolts. 
Street Cuts at Club Lane & Wedge Way  
Two street cuts associated with new sewer service  
connections were observed during the month  
(shown below).  
• Both projects involved connecting new residential 
construction to the existing sanitary sewer mains.  
• At one of the excavations, the existing water lateral 
was struck near its connection to the water main, 
which temporarily flooded the excavation.  
• The issue was addressed so work could continue,  
and the affected street and gutter areas required addi-
tional cleanup due to mud and sediment generated by 
the incident.

Executive Summary 
The Town of Columbine Valley prepared this Housing Needs Assessment (HNA) to satisfy Colorado’s housing-
needs-assessment requirements and, more importantly, to establish a locally grounded understanding of the 
Town’s housing conditions, changing household needs, and realistic opportunities and constraints. The 
assessment combines local building and land-use records, the Town’s 2020 Master Plan, community 
engagement, a 179-response resident survey, and State and federal demographic, economic, housing, and 
market data. 
Columbine Valley presents a housing context unlike that of many growing Front Range jurisdictions. The Town 
contains approximately 692 housing units and is substantially built out. Its housing stock is overwhelmingly 
detached single-family housing: 648 units, or approximately 93.6 percent of the local inventory. The remaining 
44 units are townhomes in Columbine Villas. No accessory dwelling units were identified in Town building 
records from 2010 through 2026. 
Recent building activity reinforces the built-out character of the community. The major construction wave 
from 2020 through 2022 was driven largely by Wild Plum, a discrete redevelopment of an approximately 105-
acre former horse property. Since 2023, the Town has permitted 13 new homes, but 12 were associated with 
scrape-and-rebuild activity, producing only about one net new housing unit over that period. The Town’s 
development pattern has therefore shifted from major new subdivision activity toward reinvestment, 
remodeling, and replacement of existing homes. 
The resident survey indicates substantial satisfaction with existing housing alongside a narrower but 
meaningful concern about future housing choice. Of 179 respondents, 96.6 percent expect their current 
home to meet their household needs very or somewhat well over the next ten years. Only 13.5 percent 
reported seriously considering leaving Columbine Valley within five years. At the same time, 67.2 percent said 
it would be very or somewhat important to have the option to remain in Columbine Valley if their housing 
needs change, while only 31.1 percent believe there is definitely a current housing option in Town that would 
meet their needs if they had to leave their present home. 
The survey also points to aging in place, housing costs, and lower-maintenance options as issues worth 
understanding. Property taxes were the most frequently selected factor that could make it difficult to remain 
in a current home. Among possible characteristics of a future home, smaller homes, single-story living, less 
yard or property maintenance, and smaller lots were among the most frequently selected. At the same time, 
maintaining the existing character of the community was the most frequently selected overall housing issue, 
and more than one-third of respondents selected the statement that they do not believe Columbine Valley 
has a significant housing need. 
These results do not by themselves establish a development need or policy response. Rather, they identify a 
central question for the assessment: whether the Town’s existing housing stock and limited realistic 
development opportunities can accommodate changing household needs over time while recognizing the 
community’s built-out condition, market realities, infrastructure constraints, and established character. 
Preliminary Key Findings 
• Columbine Valley is substantially built out, with very limited vacant privately owned land available for 
residential development. 
• The existing housing stock is highly concentrated in detached single-family homes, although 
approximately 23 percent of units are in smaller-lot or attached neighborhoods that can provide lower-
maintenance living options. 
• Recent residential activity is increasingly reinvestment in existing properties rather than net-new housing 
production.

• Most survey respondents expect their current homes to continue meeting their needs, and most are not 
actively considering leaving Town. 
• A meaningful share of residents nevertheless values the ability to remain in Columbine Valley if housing

• Aging in place, property-tax and insurance costs, smaller/lower-maintenance housing, and preservation

1. Purpose, Requirements and Methodology 
1.1 Purpose 
The purpose of this HNA is to describe existing housing conditions, evaluate current and future housing 
needs, identify displacement risks, consider the relationship between housing and employment, and assess 
the Town’s realistic capacity to respond to identified needs. The document is intended to inform future Town 
discussions; it does not authorize development, amend zoning, establish a housing production target, or 
commit the Town to a particular land-use action. 
1.2 State Requirement 
Colorado Senate Bill 24-174 requires local governments to conduct and publish housing needs assessments 
in accordance with a methodology developed by the Colorado Department of Local Affairs (DOLA). The 
governing body must consider the HNA at a public meeting and submit the assessment, together with any 
governing-body comments, to DOLA. DOLA acceptance depends on inclusion of the statutory HNA elements 
and demonstration that the required baseline components were considered. 
Primary statutory source: Colorado Senate Bill 24-174, C.R.S. §§ 24-32-3702 and 24-32-3703. 
https://leg.colorado.gov/bills/sb24-174 
1.3 Analytical Approach 
The Town’s approach is proportional to Columbine Valley’s size and development context. The analysis relies 
on multiple sources rather than treating any single dataset as determinative. Local administrative records are 
used where they provide greater precision than regional estimates; State and federal datasets are used for 
demographic, income, employment, affordability, vacancy, and projection measures; community 
engagement is used to understand resident experience and qualitative housing needs. 
• Local administrative data: housing inventory, building permits, demolitions, scrape/rebuild activity, 
remodels and major additions, and accessory dwelling unit activity. 
• Planning and land-use information: 2020 Master Plan, land-use mapping, remaining vacant parcels, 
redevelopment opportunities, floodplain and infrastructure constraints, and known development 
approvals. 
• External quantitative data: State Demography Office, U.S. Census Bureau/American Community Survey, 
employment and wage sources, housing-market data, and applicable DOLA data bundles. 
• Community engagement: resident survey, Board of Trustees meetings, Town communications, and 
targeted stakeholder/focus-group conversations. 
The Town distinguishes among identified housing need, theoretical or planned development capacity, 
constrained development capacity, and realistic near-term development opportunity. This distinction is 
particularly important in a substantially built-out community where a numerical housing need does not 
necessarily correspond to land that is practically available for housing.

5. Housing Market, Costs and Affordability 
Columbine Valley is a high-cost ownership market with exceptionally limited rental housing. The 2020–2024 
ACS estimates a median owner-occupied home value of approximately $1.58 million (±$197,240) and a 
median household income reported at $250,001, the upper reporting limit in the ACS table. Approximately 
97.6 percent of occupied units are estimated to be owner-occupied. Zillow’s August 2026 market data place 
Columbine Valley’s typical home value at approximately $1.69 million, among the highest municipal values in 
Arapahoe County. These market measures describe purchase-market conditions; they should not be treated 
as evidence that all existing owners have comparable incomes or liquid resources.

5.1 Income and Purchasing Power 
ACS estimates indicate a highly affluent community overall, but not a uniformly high-income one. 
Approximately 61.5 percent of households were estimated to have incomes above $200,000, while about 3.9 
percent were below $50,000, 12.6 percent were between $50,000 and $100,000, 12.9 percent between 
$100,000 and $150,000, and 9.1 percent between $150,000 and $200,000. These estimates have meaningful 
sampling error and are used as directional context rather than precise household counts. 
The combination of high purchase prices and limited unit turnover means that entry into Columbine Valley is 
difficult for households with low, moderate, and many middle incomes, even though the Town’s current 
resident population is relatively affluent. This is best understood as an availability and market-entry issue 
rather than evidence of widespread housing distress among current residents. 
5.2 Housing Cost Burden and Ongoing Ownership Costs 
ACS-derived estimates indicate that approximately one-quarter of owner households with a mortgage are 
housing-cost burdened under the conventional 30-percent-of-income measure, while the estimated burden 
rate is substantially lower among owners without a mortgage. The renter estimate is based on an extremely 
small sample and is not reliable enough to characterize a Townwide rental market. These conventional 
measures also do not fully capture a concern that emerged strongly in the Town survey: increasing property 
taxes, insurance, maintenance, and service needs can create pressure for older or fixed-income owners even 
where no mortgage is present. 
In the Town survey, property taxes were the most frequently selected factor that could make it harder to 
remain in a current home (57 respondents, 31.8 percent). Insurance costs were selected by 29 respondents 
(16.2 percent), home or yard maintenance by 27 (15.1 percent), and need for services or assistance by 46 
(25.7 percent). These responses suggest that affordability in Columbine Valley includes both market-entry 
affordability and the continuing cost of remaining in an owned home. 
5.3 Vacancy, Availability and Rental Housing 
SDO estimated 48 vacant units among 681 total units in 2024, a 7.02 percent vacancy rate. In a 
predominantly ownership community, however, statistical vacancy is not equivalent to homes being available 
for purchase or long-term rental; vacancies can include homes between occupants, seasonal use, and other 
categories. The Town survey contained 178 homeowners and one renter, underscoring the very small rental 
segment. ACS estimates similarly place homeownership at 97.6 percent. 
Market availability is therefore a more meaningful issue than vacancy alone. The Town has a small housing 
stock, relatively few annual transactions, and a narrow range of housing forms. The Realtor focus group will 
be used to test how often buyers seeking smaller, lower-maintenance, single-story, or lower-price options 
can actually find them, and whether households leaving Town would have remained if a suitable alternative 
were available. 
5.4 Data Limitations 
The ACS is indispensable for income and affordability characteristics that the Town does not collect, but it is 
subject to large margins of error at Columbine Valley’s scale. For example, the ACS estimates 792 housing 
units while the Town’s direct 2026 inventory identifies 692. The HNA therefore avoids false precision: local 
administrative counts control where available, while ACS percentages are presented as estimates and 
interpreted together with the survey, market information, and stakeholder input.

6. Employment, Wages and Jobs-Housing Relationship 
State methodology requires consideration of jobs and wages and the relationship between local employment 
and housing need. Columbine Valley is primarily residential and has no commercial district. The 2020–2024 
ACS estimates approximately 862 employed residents (±171), but this is a resident-based measure: many of 
those workers are employed outside the Town. It must not be confused with the number of jobs physically 
located within Columbine Valley. 
The Town’s principal place-based employment is associated with the Columbine Country Club, municipal 
operations, and service activity. A complete place-of-work job count by annual wage band requires Census 
LEHD/LODES or equivalent workplace-based data. That measure is retained as a final technical data item 
because resident employment data cannot substitute for it. The planned Country Club interview will also 
document the scale and housing implications of the Town’s largest employment center. 
6.1 Resident Workforce Context 
Among residents, ACS data show a highly professional workforce and a substantial work-from-home 
component. Data USA’s presentation of 2020–2024 ACS data reports that approximately 39.8 percent of 
workers worked from home and an average commute time of about 24 minutes. The largest resident 
employment sectors include professional/scientific/technical services, finance and insurance, and health 
care. These data describe residents, not local jobs, but they help explain why the Town functions primarily as 
a residential community embedded in the larger Denver metropolitan labor market. 
6.2 Jobs-Housing Relationship 
The local jobs-housing relationship is therefore structurally different from that of a municipality with a large 
employment center or commercial tax base. Columbine Valley supplies housing for residents whose jobs are 
largely regional, while the Town’s own lower- and moderate-wage service jobs are unlikely to be matched by 
housing at corresponding price points inside the Town. This mismatch does not by itself establish that such 
housing can or should be produced locally; it is a regional dependency that the HNA must acknowledge and 
that will be further informed by workplace-based job/wage data and employer input.

8. Current and Future Housing Needs 
8.1 Housing Need Is Multi-Dimensional 
For Columbine Valley, housing need cannot be reduced to a single unit-production number. The Town must 
consider the State’s quantitative estimates by income and dwelling type, but local conditions require 
additional interpretation. The community is substantially built out, ownership is dominant, rental housing is 
minimal, and the existing stock is concentrated in detached single-family homes. A numerical estimate of 
housing need therefore must be read alongside household change, affordability, market availability, and 
realistic development capacity. 
8.2 Emerging Local Need Themes 
• Aging and life-stage transition: the survey indicates interest in smaller homes, single-story living, and 
reduced maintenance, while the Master Plan previously recognized the importance of options for 
residents who wish to remain in Town as their needs change. 
• Ongoing carrying costs: property taxes and insurance emerged more strongly than mortgage payments or 
utilities as potential pressures on existing residents. 
• Housing choice rather than widespread dissatisfaction: most respondents report that their current home 
works well, but many are less confident that another suitable option exists within Town if their needs 
change. 
Town of Columbine Valley | 2026 Housing Needs Assessment | Preliminary Findings Draft   13 
• Entry and family proximity: some respondents identify purchase price and limited availability as barriers 
for family members or others who would like to live nearby. 
• Preservation and reinvestment: building data show an established pattern of remodeling, major 
additions, and scrape/rebuild activity, indicating that adaptation of the existing stock is a major 
component of the Town’s housing market. 
8.3 Housing Need by Income and Dwelling Type 
Colorado’s methodology requires local governments to estimate housing need by income level and dwelling 
type, including for-sale, rental, accessible, visitable, and supportive housing. SDO’s municipal HNA data 
bundles are county-based, meaning that the State-provided data for a municipality draw from county and 
census-tract information rather than a precise municipal market model. This is important in Columbine 
Valley, where the housing stock and income profile differ substantially from Arapahoe County as a whole. 
The evidence available at this stage supports several directional conclusions. First, the existing Town 
inventory has almost no rental supply and no apartment or condominium inventory, so rental need identified 
through regional or State data cannot be met by the existing local stock. Second, purchase prices near or 
above $1.5 million place most of the local for-sale stock beyond the reach of low-, moderate-, and many 
middle-income households. Third, the Town does contain 162 smaller-lot or attached units that provide some 
life-stage diversity, but survey results indicate uncertainty about whether an appropriate alternative is 
available when current residents’ needs change. Fourth, neither Town building records nor available 
statistical sources provide a reliable count of accessible, visitable, or supportive units. 
A precise numerical allocation of State/county housing need to Columbine Valley should therefore be 
presented with caution. The Town’s local capacity analysis demonstrates that a numerical estimate of unmet 
need is not equivalent to a feasible local production target. The final technical appendix will insert the HNA 
data-bundle tables used for income-category and dwelling-type estimates and will identify the geographic 
basis of each number. Until that extraction is complete, this draft does not manufacture a unit target from 
unstable small-area estimates. 
8.4 Future Need and Build-Out 
SDO does not publish municipal population forecasts; forecasts are available at county and regional scales. 
Applying county growth rates directly to Columbine Valley would overstate locally achievable household 
growth because the Town is substantially built out. For this HNA, future local housing change is therefore 
bounded by parcel capacity and redevelopment rather than by a simple population-growth multiplier. The 
Town may still experience demographic change, turnover, and changing housing preferences without 
significant net unit growth. 
9. Displacement Risk 
Displacement risk considers whether households may be pressured to leave their community because of 
housing costs, redevelopment, demographic or market change, or other housing instability. Columbine Valley 
differs from communities experiencing large-scale renter displacement because the local housing stock is 
overwhelmingly owner-occupied and the survey itself included 178 homeowners and one renter. 
Nevertheless, displacement risk can also take the form of older or fixed-income owners becoming unable to 
sustain increasing taxes, insurance, maintenance, or service needs. 
DOLA’s displacement-risk guidance calls for quantitative indicators as well as community engagement. 
Conventional indicators suggest relatively low risk of renter displacement because rental tenure is extremely 
limited and household incomes are high overall. ACS estimates also show very high homeownership and very 
Town of Columbine Valley | 2026 Housing Needs Assessment | Preliminary Findings Draft   14 
low levels of residents without a high-school diploma. Those characteristics reduce several forms of 
displacement vulnerability commonly seen in renter-heavy neighborhoods. 
Columbine Valley nevertheless has a different potential displacement pathway: aging homeowners or 
households on fixed incomes may face increasing property taxes, insurance, maintenance, accessibility, or 
service costs. At the same time, scrape/rebuild activity can replace an older, potentially smaller home with a 
substantially more expensive one, gradually changing the price distribution of the stock. The resident survey 
gives direct support for monitoring these pressures even though it does not indicate broad current instability. 
9.1 Community Engagement Indicators 
• 31.8 percent of survey respondents selected property taxes as a factor that could make it harder to 
remain in their current home. 
• 16.2 percent selected insurance costs; 15.1 percent selected home or yard maintenance; and 25.7 
percent selected need for services or assistance. 
• Open-ended comments included several concerns from older residents about rising property-tax 
burdens. 
• The Town’s building history documents ongoing scrape/rebuild and major remodeling activity, which 
should be considered when evaluating change in the price and size distribution of the housing stock. 
10. Development Capacity and Constraints 
10.1 Substantially Built-Out Condition 
The Town’s 2020 Master Plan concluded that major land-development opportunities were essentially 
exhausted following Wild Plum. Current Planning review is consistent with that conclusion. Only one vacant 
privately owned parcel, 801 Watson Lane, is presently identified as reasonably developable. Other vacant 
land is associated with the golf course, Town ownership, HOAs/metro districts, infrastructure, recreation, or 
common-area purposes and is not presently considered part of realistic residential development capacity. 
10.2 Infill and Redevelopment Opportunities 
Planning has identified several parcels previously discussed as potential infill or redevelopment 
opportunities, including 4 South Middlefield Road, 401 South Watson Lane, 3220 West Lake Avenue, 500 
South Watson Lane, and 600 South Watson Lane. These properties are already developed with at least one 
residence and therefore represent redevelopment rather than vacant-land development. One property 
previously identified in the 2020 Master Plan has since been developed with a large single-family home, 
reducing the previously identified redevelopment potential. 
10.3 Watson Lane Constraints 
The Watson Lane area illustrates the difference between theoretical capacity and realistic development 
capacity. Potential development may require significant coordination for access, floodplain management, 
utility service, drainage and detention, and traffic. Added density could require secondary access involving 
property controlled by neighboring entities. Two properties are affected by the 100-year floodplain. Achieving 
the maximum density contemplated in prior planning may also require coordination among multiple property 
owners; separate development could materially reduce developable land because of independent floodplain 
mitigation and detention requirements.

10.4 Capacity Framework 
Capacity Concept Meaning for Columbine Valley 
Theoretical / planned capacity Maximum units that planning documents or conceptual scenarios may suggest. 
Constrained capacity Capacity after accounting for floodplain, access, utilities, drainage, parcel configuration and ownership. 
Realistic near-term capacity Housing that could reasonably be expected given current ownership, market conditions, infrastructure, approvals and coordination requirements. 
 
The HNA should not equate theoretical capacity with expected housing production. This distinction is 
necessary to accurately describe a small, substantially built-out municipality. 
10.5 Water Supply and Utility Considerations 
The Town does not directly provide utility services. Denver Water delivers potable water, while sanitary sewer 
service is provided through Platte Canyon Water and Sanitation District, Southwest Metropolitan Water and 
Sanitation District, and Columbine Water and Sanitation District. Existing subdivisions are served through 
these regional systems rather than a Town-operated water utility. 
For HNA purposes, the relevant future water-supply question is incremental rather than systemwide. The 
Town is not planning another development comparable to Wild Plum, and realistic near-term net unit growth 
is limited by parcel availability and redevelopment constraints. Any additional units in the Watson Lane area 
or other redevelopment parcels would require project-specific confirmation of utility service and capacity. 
Planning has already identified utility coordination as one of the constraints in the Watson Lane area. 
Because the Town does not control the potable-water system and no final development program exists for 
the remaining parcels, this HNA does not assign an unsupported gallon-per-day estimate; instead, it 
identifies the number and location of realistic housing opportunities and requires utility-provider confirmation 
as development is proposed. 
11. Local Housing Resources and Programs 
The Town does not currently maintain dedicated housing or affordable-housing programs, housing funds, 
financial incentives, property held for housing purposes, or related housing expenditures. This is an important 
baseline condition for the State methodology. It also reflects the Town’s historic role as a small, 
predominantly residential municipality rather than a direct housing provider. 
12. Potential Policy and Programmatic Responses 
State methodology calls for recommended policy or programmatic responses to identified housing needs and 
displacement risks. At the draft stage, the Town should avoid selecting responses before the external 
quantitative analysis and stakeholder input are complete. The following response categories are appropriate 
for evaluation against the final findings; inclusion here does not constitute a recommendation to adopt any 
particular strategy. 
• Monitor and periodically update housing, demographic, and redevelopment indicators so future 
decisions are based on local conditions. 
• Preserve and facilitate reinvestment in the existing housing stock, including adaptations that allow 
residents to age in place where feasible. 
• Evaluate whether existing regulations unnecessarily constrain smaller, lower-maintenance, single-story, 
accessible, or adaptable housing where a realistic redevelopment opportunity exists. 
Town of Columbine Valley | 2026 Housing Needs Assessment | Preliminary Findings Draft   16 
• Consider non-land-use strategies addressing aging in place, accessibility, service connections, and 
resident education, recognizing that some housing challenges cannot be solved through new 
construction. 
• Continue to distinguish housing affordability for prospective buyers from ongoing carrying-cost pressures 
experienced by existing homeowners. 
• Use parcel-specific analysis for any future infill or redevelopment proposal rather than assuming broad 
Townwide development capacity. 
• Coordinate with regional and State partners where housing needs extend beyond what can reasonably be 
accommodated within Columbine Valley’s boundaries. 
13. Preliminary Conclusions 
The evidence assembled to date supports a picture of a stable, highly owner-occupied, predominantly single-
family community with strong resident satisfaction and very limited remaining land for new development. 
Columbine Valley is not experiencing the same scale or form of housing challenge as larger or faster-growing 
jurisdictions. Its principal housing questions are more specific: how existing homes will serve an aging and 
changing population; whether residents who need a different housing product can find one locally; how 
ongoing ownership costs affect the ability to remain; how reinvestment and scrape/rebuild activity change the 
stock over time; and what, if any, realistic infill or redevelopment opportunities can respond to identified 
needs without assuming capacity that does not exist. 
The external data reinforce rather than overturn the local story. SDO places the 2024 population at 
approximately 1,547 with 633 occupied units; ACS estimates depict a high-income, highly owner-occupied 
community with a median age near 50 and owner values around $1.58 million, but with unusually large 
sampling uncertainty. Market data confirm a very high-cost ownership market. The principal unresolved 
technical items are the workplace-based jobs-by-wage table and the final extraction of the State HNA data-
bundle tables by income and dwelling type. Realtor and major-employer input will then be added as 
qualitative market evidence before the November draft.
