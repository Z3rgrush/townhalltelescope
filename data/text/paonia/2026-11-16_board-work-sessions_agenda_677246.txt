Executive Summary 
The Town's municipal code rewrite provides an opportunity to modernize Paonia's land-use 
regulations, implement the Comprehensive Plan, expand housing choices, support economic 
development, preserve community character, and establish more effective development standards. 
The proposed revisions substantially reorganize Chapters 16 (Zoning), 17 (Subdivisions), and 18 
(Building Regulations).  They also clarify administrative responsibilities, eliminate outdated or 
potentially unenforceable provisions, and establish more predictable review procedures. 
Major policy recommendations include: 
• Housing and residential infill: Reduce minimum lot sizes and widths in older, denser 
neighborhoods; allow development on existing legal lots; reduce setbacks where appropriate; 
eliminate restrictive average-lot-size requirements; permit ADUs by right throughout residential 
areas; accommodate smaller dwelling units, duplexes, townhouses, cottage clusters, and 
cohousing; and provide flexibility for alley-accessed housing. 
• Affordable housing: Encourage the integration of permanently affordable units into market-
rate developments, rather than relying primarily on concentrated affordable housing projects.  
• Economic development: Expand opportunities for creative industries, live/work units, studios, 
workshops, small-scale production, cultural facilities, artisan businesses, and locally owned 
enterprises.  Accommodate these uses in appropriate commercial, mixed-use, industrial, and 
residential districts. 
• Downtown and neighborhood character: Establish simple, measurable design standards that 
protect historically significant buildings, encourage rehabilitation and adaptive reuse, and 
ensure new construction reinforces the scale, proportions, and pedestrian character of 
downtown without requiring imitation historic architecture.  Consider a companion illustrated 
design manual and separate demolition provisions. 
• Development and environmental standards: Modernize parking requirements and 
management, landscaping, permeable surfacing, stormwater management, and accessory 
building standards.  Incorporate energy-efficient building design, solar-ready construction, solar 
access considerations, mature tree canopy preservation, and enforceable Complete Streets 
requirements.

• Subdivision and planned development: Clarify major and minor subdivision procedures, 
establish a separate streamlined process for lot-line adjustments, modernize PUD requirements 
to encourage quality design, and establish appropriate standards for HOA documents, 
infrastructure maintenance, and public improvements. 
• Infrastructure financing: Consider adopting legally compliant development impact fees for 
roads, water, sewer, public parking, and other capital facilities to ensure new development 
contributes its proportionate share of the infrastructure costs associated with growth.  Require 
infrastructure bond cost estimates to include a 15% contingency. 
• Development review and administrative efficiency: Clarify the respective responsibilities of the 
Community Development Director, Building Official, and other Town officials; simplify review 
procedures; establish consistent application requirements and fee schedules; recover actual 
development application costs through advance payment; and provide appropriate fee waivers 
for public and qualifying community-benefit projects. 
• Code modernization and regulatory consistency: Reorganize Chapters 16, 17, and 18; update 
definitions and zoning classifications; incorporate applicable state statutory requirements, 
including the Vested Property Rights Act; eliminate outdated or unenforceable provisions; and 
establish clear, consistent approval and appeal procedures. 
These recommendations provide a practical regulatory framework appropriate to a small town.  They 
do not require Paonia to adopt the extensive and complex standards used by larger municipalities.

Identify Areas for Affordable Housing 
Does the Town intend to conduct a land availability survey to accomplish this?  For a community 
of this size, I generally recommend doing so only if the Town intends to partner with an affordable 
housing developer proactively and has secured financing for a project specifically designed to 
provide affordable housing.  Rather than conducting a survey and selecting sites independently, I 
recommend working directly with the developer's expertise to identify suitable tracts of land.  As 
someone who has been directly involved, on a developer's behalf, in selecting suitable sites, I 
know it is important to keep development costs down, and that individual site conditions can 
drive construction costs up or down. 
Another approach is to integrate affordable units throughout market-rate housing rather than 
concentrate them on separate developments.  The City of Boulder pioneered this approach in 
1985, with considerable success.  It reduces the concentration of low-income households in any 
one area and better integrates residents across income levels into the broader community. 
Paonia's Comprehensive Plan already supports this approach through policies that encourage 
ADUs, smaller lots, and smaller dwelling units in residential zones.  The Town should consider 
pursuing funding to offset the construction costs of affordable units throughout the community, in 
exchange for deed restrictions that keep rents or sale prices affordable for low-income 
households.  This would create more economically integrated neighborhoods rather than relying 
primarily on concentrated affordable housing developments.

Preserving Downtown Character and Older Residential Neighborhoods 
The Comprehensive Plan emphasizes preserving the character of downtown and the Town's older 
residential neighborhoods. 
Does the Town have an inventory of historically significant buildings and homes?  History Colorado 
does not appear to have a historic building survey for Paonia in its database. 
Buildings should not automatically be classified as historically significant simply because they are more 
than 50 years old.  A preliminary inventory should distinguish among: 
Page 11 of 31
Page 10 of 29 
 
• Contributing historic buildings. 
• Noncontributing buildings. 
• Vacant or undeveloped parcels. 
• Buildings with significant architectural features worth protecting. 
Recommended Regulatory Approach 
The period from 1900 to 1940 could be the primary focus, although buildings outside that period may 
also contribute to downtown character. 
The objectives should be to: 
1. Protect the historic commercial and residential buildings that give downtown its identity, 
particularly buildings constructed between 1900 and 1940.  Avoid remodeling that makes 
historic buildings look artificially new; the natural wear and patina of older buildings contribute 
to downtown character. 
2. Encourage continued use, rehabilitation, and adaptive reuse of historic buildings. 
3. Allow interior renovations that do not affect a building's exterior appearance. 
4. Ensure that new construction, additions, and redevelopment reinforce the scale, rhythm, and 
pedestrian character of downtown rather than overpowering it.  Discourage imitation of 
historic architecture. 
5. Maintain a continuous, pedestrian-oriented streetscape. 
New buildings should not be required to imitate historic architecture.  Welcome contemporary 
architecture that respects the scale, proportions, placement, and character of historic buildings. 
I recommend simple, measurable design standards administered by the Community Development 
Director rather than creating a separate design review board.  Straightforward projects could be 
approved administratively. 
Demolition: Consider a separate demolition provision.  A code that carefully regulates new 
construction but allows important historic buildings to be demolished without meaningful review does 
not adequately protect historic character. 
I also recommend a companion two- or three-page illustrated design manual showing acceptable and 
unacceptable examples of storefront rehabilitation, new infill, building additions, rooflines, and facade 
treatments.  The ordinance would contain enforceable requirements; the illustrations would help 
applicants understand how to comply. 
This combination is likely to be more effective for Paonia than a lengthy historic preservation code. 
Board direction: Do these recommendations reflect the Board's approach to preserving Paonia's 
character?

Lot Sizes and Residential Infill 
The Comprehensive Plan calls for reducing minimum lot sizes in the Town's denser areas.  I 
recommend a neighborhood-context approach rather than reducing all residential minimums by the 
same percentage.  Older, denser neighborhoods should accommodate smaller lots reflecting the 
historic pattern, while newer, low-density areas may retain larger minimums. 
The code should distinguish between minimum lot sizes for newly created lots and the ability to 
develop existing, legally established lots.  Both are important to encouraging infill and more affordable 
housing. 
The original Paonia townsite appears to include many lots approximately 25 feet wide and 125–135 
feet deep, or roughly 3,125–3,375 square feet.  Individual blocks and later subdivisions vary.  These 
historic dimensions are substantially smaller than the current 6,000-square-foot residential minimum. 
Option: Established Neighborhood Residential Area 
The Town could establish an Established Neighborhood Residential area on the zoning map covering 
older, more compact portions of Paonia.  Potential standards include: 
• Minimum lot area of 3,000–3,500 square feet. 
• Minimum lot width of 25 feet. 
• Duplexes and ADUs permitted by right. 
• Small-lot detached homes and townhouses. 
• Reduced setbacks and flexible lot coverage. 
• Lot consolidation permitted when owners choose, but not required for development of existing 
small lots. 
This would allow more housing without requiring wholesale redevelopment or large subdivisions. 
Related Code Changes 
Reducing lot area without revising other dimensional standards will have limited effect.  I recommend 
considering the following: 
1. Reduce minimum lot widths.  The existing 50-foot requirement is particularly restrictive.  
Consider 25-foot lots in R-3 and 30-foot lots in R-2. 
2. Allow development on existing legal lots.  A legally established lot should be eligible for 
development even if it does not meet current minimum lot area, width, or frontage standards, 
as long as it meets applicable building, access, fire-safety, and utility requirements. 
Page 13 of 31
Page 12 of 29 
 
3. Reduce setbacks where appropriate.  Smaller lots need appropriately scaled setbacks.  
Consider 15-foot front, 5-foot side, and 10-foot rear setbacks in R-2 and R-3, with additional 
flexibility for existing lots and historic development patterns. 
4. Eliminate the restrictive average-lot-size provision.  The current requirement that average lot 
size be at least 6,000 square feet undermines the objective of allowing smaller lots.  Replace it 
with objective standards addressing access, infrastructure, drainage, and usable building area. 
5. Allow narrow-lot housing types.  Explicitly accommodate duplexes, townhouses, cottage 
clusters, and small-lot detached homes. 
6. Consider alley-accessed housing.  Allow subdivision through the center width of a lot and 
construction of a small dwelling with access solely from the alley.  Tucson, Arizona, permits this 
approach in older areas.  Parking is a consideration; requiring one or two on-site spaces, 
depending on unit size, could address the issue.

Managing Parking Impacts as Residential Density Increases 
Reducing downtown parking requirements can improve walkability and land-use efficiency.  However, 
increased density and tourism may place additional pressure on nearby residential streets, particularly 
during summer weekends. 
The following measures could help manage parking demand: 
1. Public parking impact fee.  Consider a fee for new development based on the current cost of 
400 square feet of land in the project area for each parking space the development would 
otherwise be required to provide.  The Town would hold revenue in a dedicated fund and invest 
it for future public parking improvements, potentially including a parking structure when 
demand warrants it. 
2.   Shared parking agreements.  Where shared parking supports development or increased 
density, require an enforceable agreement to be filed with the Town.  Compatible examples 
include: 
a. Schools and entertainment, bar, or restaurant facilities, which have different peak-use 
periods. 
b. Churches with unused weekday parking and nearby offices or banks that need weekday 
parking. 
Agreements should include a remedy if shared parking becomes unavailable, such as providing 
replacement parking or paying an established fee. 
3.   Parking shuttles.  Require high-traffic businesses to provide frequent shuttle service to remote 
parking lots during peak periods where appropriate. 
4.   Town-owned parking.  Consider purchasing land near downtown for a public parking lot that 
could later accommodate a parking structure. 
5.   Downtown and neighborhood street parking.  Consider striping angled parking at 60–90 
degrees on appropriate downtown and older residential streets.  Some streets could also 
accommodate a striped bicycle lane.  These improvements may help absorb parking demand as 
tourism and residential density increase. 
6.   Consider adapting the Dutch concept of a woonerf, or "living street," on selected side 
streets.  A woonerf is designed as shared public space rather than primarily as a vehicle route.  
Its characteristics include: 
• Cyclists, vehicles, and, where sidewalks are absent, pedestrians sharing the same space. 
• Very low vehicle speeds, typically around walking speed. 
• Reduced or eliminated conventional separation between sidewalks and travel lanes.

• Street trees, planters, benches, and other features that make the street an extension of 
the public realm. 
7. Loading areas.  Consider not requiring on-site loading areas unless a business receives more 
than daily truck deliveries.  In small downtowns, delivery trucks sometimes stop in the street.  
This can also help slow traffic.

Parking Lot Surfacing and Landscaping 
1.   Permeable Parking Surfaces 
Encourage permeable parking surfaces throughout Town while distinguishing between 
engineered systems and less-stable materials. 
All parking areas shall have a durable, dust-free surface that provides stable, mud-free vehicle 
access and manages stormwater runoff. 
Stable permeable surfaces: Engineered systems designed for anticipated vehicle loads and 
traffic shall be permitted for parking areas of any size.  Examples include permeable 
interlocking concrete pavers, porous asphalt, pervious concrete, and engineered gravel-
stabilization systems. 
Less-stable permeable surfaces:  Unstabilized or lightly stabilized gravel and similar materials 
may be used for parking areas containing 20 or fewer spaces, provided they are properly 
installed and maintained to prevent excessive rutting, erosion, dust, and mud. 
Design and maintenance: Design permeable surfaces for site soil conditions, drainage, 
anticipated traffic, snowplowing, and local freeze-thaw conditions.  Property owners shall 
maintain surfaces in a safe, stable, and functional condition. 
2.   Landscaping 
Regarding the suggestion to reduce landscaping requirements, landscaping serves more than an 
aesthetic purpose.  It reduces runoff and helps limit impacts on stormwater drainage systems.  
In areas with greater lot coverage, landscaping provides important opportunities for 
stormwater infiltration and reduces runoff entering streets and drainage systems.

Accessory Dwelling Units (ADUs) 
Although Paonia is exempt from the new state statutes requiring certain jurisdictions to allow ADUs, 
they remain one of the most effective ways to expand affordable housing opportunities.  The 
Comprehensive Plan calls for allowing ADUs broadly throughout the Town. 
I recommend permitting one ADU by right on any lot with a lawful principal dwelling, subject to 
administrative review for compliance with objective code requirements. 
Definition of Accessory Dwelling Unit (ADU): A secondary, independent dwelling unit located 
on the same lot as a principal dwelling, designed for residential occupancy and containing 
facilities for living, sleeping, cooking, and sanitation.  An ADU may be located within, attached 
to, or detached from the principal dwelling. 
Applicability and Permitted Locations 
1. One ADU is permitted on any lot containing a lawful principal dwelling, in all zoning 
districts where residential dwellings are permitted. 
2.   An ADU may be: 
• Located within or attached to the principal dwelling. 
• Constructed as a separate, detached building. 
• Created by converting an existing accessory building, including a garage. 
• Incorporated into a new or reconstructed accessory building. 
3.   An ADU may be constructed at the same time as the principal dwelling or added to an 
existing developed lot. 
Development Standards 
1.   Number: No more than one ADU per lot. 
2.   Maximum floor area: 
• Lots of 6,000 square feet or less: 750 square feet or the gross floor area of 
the principal dwelling, whichever is less. 
• Lots larger than 6,000 square feet: 1,000 square feet or the gross floor area 
of the principal dwelling, whichever is less. 
3.   Minimum size: No minimum floor area beyond applicable building and life-safety code 
requirements. 
4.   Height: A detached ADU shall not exceed 25 feet. 
5.   Setbacks: 
• Attached ADUs shall meet the setbacks applicable to the principal dwelling. 
Page 17 of 31
Page 16 of 29 
 
• Detached ADUs shall meet the setbacks for accessory structures in the zoning 
district. 
• ADUs created within existing lawful structures may retain existing setbacks, 
subject to applicable building and fire codes. 
6.   Lot coverage: ADUs shall comply with applicable lot coverage requirements.  Existing 
lawful structures and nonconformities shall not, by themselves, prevent conversion of 
an existing building to an ADU. 
7.   Design: ADUs shall be compatible in general scale and appearance with the principal 
dwelling.  Identical architectural styles, materials, and roof designs shall not be required. 
8.   Access: ADUs shall have safe pedestrian access from a street or shared driveway.  
Emergency access shall comply with applicable fire and building codes. 
9.   Parking: No additional off-street parking shall be required for an ADU.  Existing parking 
may serve both dwelling units. 
10.   Short-term rentals: ADUs may not be used as short-term rentals. 
11.   Existing accessory structures: A lawful garage, shed, or other accessory building may 
be converted to an ADU if it complies with applicable building, fire, and utility 
requirements.  The presence of another lawful accessory structure shall not, by itself, 
prohibit an ADU.

The PUD section needs updating to reflect modern standards and the Comprehensive Plan. 
I support using PUDs to allow greater flexibility and creative community design.  However, to ensure 
that flexibility produces meaningful benefits, I recommend requiring projects to demonstrate at least 
one substantial public or community benefit, such as: 
• Better pedestrian connections. 
• More usable common open space. 
• Housing diversity or affordability. 
• Preservation of historic structures or significant natural features. 
• More efficient infrastructure. 
• Improved landscaping and stormwater management.

Development Application Fees 
Paonia's current development application fees do not cover the full cost of review, effectively 
subsidizing private development.  The Town should consider recovering the full cost of development 
review, including staff time required to process applications through final approval, public notice, and 
other processing expenses. 
I recommend requiring payment up front to avoid collection problems later. 
Proposed Fee-Collection Process 
1.   Require a pre-application meeting. 
2.   Require the applicant to submit adequate information before scheduling the meeting. 
3.   Following the meeting, staff estimates the time and material costs of reviewing the 
application through final approval or denial. 
4.   Add a 10% contingency to the estimate. 
5.   Require the applicant to pay the estimated review cost in full in advance. 
6.   Track actual staff time and material costs devoted solely to application review and 
processing. 
7.   Refund any amount paid in excess of actual review and processing costs.

Requirements to Ensure Energy Efficiency and Solar Access 
The following highlights key ordinance language that could be incorporated into Paonia's municipal 
land use code.  It applies to new residential units, including infill development and additional dwelling 
units in older downtown neighborhoods. 
These proposed standards encourage energy-efficient development, preserve opportunities for passive 
and active solar energy use, and protect the mature tree canopy that contributes to the environmental 
quality, character, and livability of Paonia's established neighborhoods. 
You could structure the code around four principles: 
1.   Energy-efficient development: Require new residential construction to meet applicable 
energy conservation standards and incorporate practical energy-efficient design. 
2.   Solar-ready development: Orient and design buildings to accommodate future rooftop solar 
installations and maximize reasonable opportunities for passive solar heating. 
3.   Solar access protection: Avoid unnecessary obstruction of existing and potential solar energy 
systems through building placement, height, massing, and landscaping decisions. 
4.   Tree canopy preservation: Retain healthy mature deciduous trees wherever feasible and 
recognize their contribution to both summer cooling and seasonal solar access.

Applicability 
A. These standards shall apply to: 
1.   Construction of new principal residential buildings and dwelling units. 
2.   Construction of accessory dwelling units (ADUs) and other detached residential units. 
3.   Substantial additions or expansions that materially alter building height, massing, or roof 
configuration. 
4.   New multifamily residential development. 
5.   Subdivision or redevelopment proposals involving the removal of mature trees or changes 
in site configuration that could materially affect solar access.

B. These standards shall apply to residential development throughout the Town, with particular 
consideration given to infill development in established neighborhoods characterized by smaller 
lots, mature tree canopy, and traditional residential development patterns. 
Energy-Efficient Building Design 
New residential development shall incorporate energy-efficient design measures appropriate to the 
building type and site conditions, including: 
1.   Building orientation and window placement that take advantage of available natural light and 
beneficial solar exposure. 
2.   High-performance insulation and building envelope construction. 
3.   Energy-efficient windows and exterior doors. 
4.   Air sealing and measures to reduce unwanted heat loss and air infiltration. 
5.   Energy-efficient heating, cooling, ventilation, and water-heating systems. 
6.   Use of energy-efficient lighting and appliances. 
7.   Design measures that reduce overheating during summer months while retaining 
opportunities for beneficial winter solar gain. 
Design Considerations 
Where practical and consistent with neighborhood character, building design will incorporate passive 
solar principles, including south-facing glazing, appropriate thermal mass, roof orientation suitable for 
solar installations, and shading devices that reduce summer heat gain. 
Solar-Ready Development and Solar Access 
New residential buildings shall be designed to accommodate future installation of rooftop solar energy 
systems reasonably. 
The placement, height, orientation, and massing of new residential buildings shall consider the 
potential effects of development on solar access to existing buildings and adjacent properties. 
Development proposals will demonstrate reasonable efforts to: 
1.   Avoid unnecessary shading of existing rooftop solar energy systems. 
2.   Minimize significant shading of south-facing windows and other established passive solar 
features on adjoining properties. 
3.   Maintain reasonable opportunities for future solar installations on neighboring properties. 
4.   Use building placement, roof configuration, and architectural design to minimize avoidable 
solar obstruction.

Solar Access Assessment 
For new residential construction on sites where building height, massing, or proximity to existing 
development could materially affect solar access, the Town may require a basic solar access 
assessment. 
The assessment shall identify: 
1.   Existing rooftop solar energy systems on immediately adjoining properties. 
2.   Significant existing passive solar features that could be affected by the proposed development. 
3.   The location and approximate extent of potential shading resulting from the proposed 
building. 
4.   Reasonable design alternatives that could reduce adverse shading impacts. 
Solar Access Priority 
In evaluating development proposals, the Town shall consider existing solar energy systems and 
established passive solar features while recognizing that solar access is one of several factors to be 
balanced with property rights, neighborhood character, tree preservation, and reasonable 
development opportunities. 
These standards shall not be interpreted to establish an absolute right to uninterrupted sunlight or to 
require removal of healthy mature trees solely to improve solar access. 
Mature Tree Canopy Preservation 
The Town intends to preserve existing mature trees while encouraging development designs that 
accommodate both summer shade and beneficial winter solar exposure. 
Where removal of a healthy mature tree is approved, replacement planting shall be required where 
feasible. 
The Town shall recognize that mature deciduous trees provide important summer shade while allowing 
substantially greater solar exposure during winter months. 
Site Planning and Building Placement 
A. New residential development shall be designed to use available solar exposure efficiently while 
maintaining the established pattern and character of the surrounding neighborhood. 
B. Site plans shall consider: 
1.   Building orientation and placement relative to the sun. 
2.   Existing mature trees and opportunities for tree preservation. 
3.   Building height, massing, and potential shading of adjoining properties.

Clarifying HOA and Town Roles and Responsibilities 
Municipalities sometimes face pressure to enforce HOA covenants that are not municipal 
requirements.  Conversely, HOA residents sometimes believe private covenants supersede municipal 
regulations. 
I recommend adding the following provisions to Chapter 17. 
Homeowners' Association Documents and Municipal Regulations 
A. Submission and Review of HOA Documents 
The Town's review of proposed declarations, covenants, and other HOA documents shall be 
limited to provisions relevant to compliance with approved development plans, required public 
improvements, common facilities, access, drainage, utilities, open space, and other 
requirements of this Code or conditions of approval. 
B. Applicability of Municipal Regulations 
All property within the Town, including property subject to HOA declarations, covenants, rules, 
and regulations, remains subject to applicable Town ordinances, land-use regulations, building 
codes, and other municipal requirements. 
C. Enforcement Responsibilities 
Homeowners' associations are solely responsible for administering and enforcing their own 
declarations, covenants, rules, and regulations.  The Town shall not interpret, administer, or 
enforce private HOA requirements except where it has expressly accepted an enforcement 
responsibility through a recorded agreement or other lawful instrument.

Adopt Development Impact Fees 
As Paonia prepares to lift its five-year water tap moratorium, it is an opportune time to consider 
adopting development impact fees.  Impact fees ensure that new development and redevelopment 
contribute their fair share toward the public infrastructure needed to support growth, including roads, 
water, sewer, and public parking. 
Paonia has historically deferred infrastructure maintenance and improvements because they have 
been a financial burden on the Town.  Impact fees would provide an additional funding source to help 
offset the increased demand that development places on existing systems and the capital 
improvements needed to accommodate that demand. 
Impact fees increase the cost of new development.  However, without them, the costs of 
accommodating growth can fall disproportionately on existing residents and taxpayers.  A predictable, 
equitable impact-fee system would help protect existing resources while supporting responsible, 
financially sustainable growth. 
Colorado municipalities can generally impose impact fees on new development and redevelopment 
that increases demand for public facilities.  However, a rational nexus is necessary but not sufficient.  
Fees must also comply with Colorado's statutory requirements and constitutional limits. 
The key distinction is between charging for incremental demand created by development and charging 
a redeveloper for deficiencies or infrastructure costs that existed before the project. 
Paonia can establish fees that apply consistently to new development and redevelopment, with 
appropriate credits for existing development rights and capacity.

Closing Recommendation 
The Town should prioritize completing and adopting revised Chapters 16, 17, and 18 before accepting 
new development applications when the water tap moratorium is lifted.  These regulations are 
essential to ensuring new development is reviewed against current, coordinated standards that reflect 
the Town's land use objectives, infrastructure capacity, and long-term community vision.  Accepting 
applications before the revised code is adopted could expose the Town to proposals evaluated under 
outdated or inadequate standards, potentially creating unintended development patterns and 
regulatory complications that may be difficult to correct later. 
If the water tap moratorium is lifted before the code revisions are adopted, the Town could consider 
establishing a separate, temporary emergency moratorium on accepting development applications 
until the revised chapters are finalized and adopted.  Such a moratorium would need to be narrowly 
focused, supported by appropriate findings and legal authority, and accompanied by a clearly defined, 
expedited schedule for completing the code revisions.  The Town would need to actively prioritize this 
work and demonstrate sustained, measurable progress to complete and adopt the revised code within 
no less than six months, consistent with applicable requirements governing emergency moratoria. 
The essential point is that lifting the water tap moratorium should not inadvertently open the door to 
development under regulations the Town is already working to replace.  The Town should have its 
updated regulatory framework in place before resuming acceptance of development applications.
