Staff Report on Adding Accessory Dwelling Units (ADUs) to Paonia’s 
Municipal Code 
 
This report and discussion request is being presented to the Planning Commission 
because the 2025 Comprehensive plan lists ADUs as a key part of creating housing 
affordability and a code rewrite is currently in progress and about to address the chapters 
related to ADUs.  
The meeting’s goal is to provide information for the code rewrite of Sections 16-18. 
This report identiﬁes key points to consider when deciding on ADU legislation, provides 
questions for discussion, staff recommendations, and background information about each 
point from: 
• The current Paonia Municipal Code 
• The 2025 Paonia Comprehensive Plan 
• A draft Paonia ordinance created in 2021 that did not get adopted, with additional 
commentary from current staff 
• CRS §29-35-401 to 405 (2025) provides key points on ensuring ADUs are legal, 
geared towards housing locals, and affordable. While the legislation is designed to 
outline requirements for a grant process that is no longer open, its information is 
well considered and helpful in shaping the use of ADUs. 
• The 2018 International Residential Code and the 2018 International Building Code, 
both of which have been adopted by Paonia. 
• Other relevant codes that provide frameworks or information appropriate to the key 
points. 
The packet contains: 
• This report 
• Large sections pulled from the above sources that are directly related to ADUs 
(recommended if you want more information than provided by the bullet points in 
the report) 
• The complete text of many of these sources. (not necessary to reference unless you 
would like the broader context of each source) 
• The Staff Memo to the STR Committee (referenced later in this document)

Location – Where are ADUs permitted? 
• The Comprehensive Plan mentions all residential zones except Urban Farm as 
places for ADUs 
 
• PMC Table 16-3 currently allows residences in limited ways that resemble ADUs in 
C-1, C-2, and I-1 Districts. 
Use  C-1 District  C-2 District  I-1 District  
Dwelling units as a part of a business use  S  S  S  
Dwelling units secondary to the business use  P  P  S  
S = By Special Review    P = By Right 
• Existing top ﬂoor, basement, and rear residences exist throughout C-1, and would 
likely be considered ADUs with the new code. Do we want to “grandfather” these 
units and forbid new ones, or allow ADUs in those zones if they ﬁt other zoning 
requirements? 
Staff recommendation is to allow ADUs in all Residential Zones, with existing 
nonconforming status (grandfathering) for ADUs existing in Commercial and Industrial 
Zones. 
Utilities – How should water, sewer, trash be handled – including physical 
infrastructure, accounts, and billing 
• No new sewer or water taps are required by PMC §13-1-70, but metering currently 
is.** (a) “Properties sharing a common line feeding through one property to another, 
or utilizing a single meter for two (2) or more units or serviced by only one water line 
shall be required to install separate meters so that service to each user is 
independent from every other. ” 
(b) “Each household unit shall be serviced by a separate water meter. Multiple 
household units in a single building owned by a single entity built prior to the 
enactment of this Article may be served by a single water meter under the condition 
that all units are included in one bill which is the responsibility of the property 
owner.” 
• **The code does conﬂicts with water moratorium language PM §13-1-131: (f) “It is 
presumed that each tap sold by the Town prior to the effective date of Ordinance 
No. 2020-01 was purchased for the connection of one (1) household unit or one (1) 
commercial unit to the Town's municipal water supply system.” 
• Sewer taps are less ﬂexible, needing a separate tap for each building according to 
PMC §13-5-330: “Each house, dwelling, building, store, premises, and other 
structures connected to or with the wastewater collection/sanitary sewer collection

system shall be separate and independent from that of any other house, dwelling, 
building, store, premises or any other structure, and each shall have an independent 
connection with the wastewater collection/sanitary sewer collection system, 
except in the cases of apartments, congregate residential living facilities,

for such facility in single ownership and which is sized and assessed based on the

• Laterals on the lot may be large enough to connect the ADU without a separate tap. 
Cursory research indicates this is the most common practice, however laterals may 
need to be upgraded by the property owner. (I was unable to confer with Public 
Works about this before the packet was produced, but will try to come to the 
meeting with additional information.) 
• No ADUs should be allowed on parcels that still have septic tanks – All ADUs should 
be connected to Town water and sewer. 
• Do we want to consider removing the “ban” on gray water or reclaimed water 
systems in our current adoption of the 2018 IRC? It may set off some of the 
additional water use allowing these systems. 
• Current billing practice is to charge a number of base rates equal to the number of 
businesses + residences on one account. (e.g. a duplex with one owner and one 
Town bill would have 2 water base rates + usage and 2 sewer base rates). Should 
billing follow this practice? 
Staff recommends maintaining a single water and sewer tap for the lot if one ADU is being 
added – multiple water taps can weaken the pressure in the system, and the code clearly 
allows this practice for water. Staff recommends adding a single ADU per lot to the 
exceptions outlined in PMC §13-5-330 for sewer taps with a provision made to assess the 
lateral’s ability to handle an additional residence. Staff also recommends allowing gray 
water systems in buildings. 
Parking – How should the additional vehicles be managed 
• PMC Table 16-5 requires 1.5 spaces for “efficiency or one bedroom” residences, but 
only with “the construction, addition or change of use [of a building], which requires 
a special review” (§16-6-10). 
 
• CRS §29-35-403 disallows jurisdictions from requiring “the construction of a new 
off-street parking space in connection with the construction or conversion of an 
accessory dwelling unit” except if there is existing parking that could be designated 
for the ADU, the ADU sits in a zoning area that requires one or more parking spaces 
for the primary dwelling unit, or the ADU is located on a block where on-street

Staff recommends exempting a single ADU per lot from the parking requirements outlined

Occupation – Owners, local workforce, vacations 
• The Paonia ADU draft code requires that “One of the dwelling units on the property 
must be, and remain, owner occupied,” which is pretty unenforceable (and may not 
be legal). However, 
 
• CRS §29-35-403 offers that you cannot “Require an accessory dwelling unit, or any 
other dwelling on the same lot as an accessory dwelling unit, to be owner-occupied; 
except that a subject jurisdiction may require a property owner to demonstrate 
that the property owner resides on the parcel when an application is submitted: 
I. To construct or convert an accessory dwelling unit. This exception does not 
apply for an accessory dwelling unit that is being constructed simultaneously 
with a new primary dwelling unit. 
II. For a license or permit for a short-term rental on the parcel through a local 
law or program. ” 
 
• In tandem with this report, we are providing the STR committee a memo explaining 
the ADU consideration process underway so they can consider how to match the 
goals of allowing ADUs to increase affordable housing. The two pieces of legislation 
(or code rewrite and legislation) should remain separate but headed towards the 
same goal. So, no STR legislation here, but perhaps we can provide suggestions to 
the STR committee. 
 
• Should new ADUs - not built in tandem with a new primary residence - demonstrate 
owner occupation at the time of application? 
 
• Is there a way to encourage renting ADUs long term for the local workforce? 
Size – How large or small should the ADUs be 
• I can ﬁnd no piece of PMC that provides minimum requirements for dwelling size.  
 
• The draft ADU code lists 800 ft2 as a maximum with no minimum. 
 
• CRS §29-35-402 requires ADUs to be allowed if they are between 500 and 750 ft2. It 
allows for other sizes, such as smaller than the primary residence.

• The 2018 IRC (our adopted code) provides minimum room sizes either literally (all 
rooms for living must be at least 70 ft2 and 7’ by 7’, except the kitchen) or practically, 
by specifying the required space around toilets, sinks, etc. The room dimensions 
can be a barrier to tiny houses, which can range between 60 and 400 ft2. 
 
• If we want to address smaller dwellings, Colorado’s 8 CCR 1302-14 adapts rules for 
Tiny Houses (which have a solid foundation) and Tiny Homes (which are on a 
chassis) from the 2024 IBC and 2024 IRC designed to provide “building codes for 
factory-built residential structures and tiny homes [and] factory-built nonresidential 
structures; [. . .]in those areas of the state where no standards exist.” 
 
Among other things, it updates the Appendix BB of the 2024 IRC which covers Tiny 
Houses to speciﬁc standards for Colorado.  
 
• **WARNING** adopting any new building code (like Appendix BB of the 2024 IRC 
and 8 CCR 1302-14) means we are required to adopt the Colorado Model Low 
Energy and Carbon Code (MLECC). This can prove difficult for new buildings 
because it contains requirements such as having electric panels and runs installed 
for solar panels, electric heat, and all electric appliances whether or not the home 
will be using gas for fuel or solar. 
 
• What should the sizes for ADUs be? Should we tackle tiny houses and other building 
types, such as yurts, in the same code? Should we limit them to 1 story? 
Staff recommends providing a maximum dwelling size (750 ft2) and either a minimum 
dwelling size or beginning a discussion of updating to the 2024 ICC code cycle. This may 
need to be a separate discussion and meeting, or the Committee can speciﬁcally address 
Appendix BB to allow for tiny houses, as they are growing in popularity and the office 
receives questions about them semi-regularly. 
Design – limitations and opportunities 
• Should we exclude some structures from being allowed as ADUs, such as mobile 
homes (already not allowed outside of MH zoning, of which we have none), motor 
homes, multi-purpose trailers, RVs, and Tiny Homes (on a chassis, not a 
foundation)? Keeping ADUs as more “house-like” structures can also be 
accomplished through building code requirements if it is desirable. 
 
• CRS §29-35-404 suggests helping with affordability by “Providing pre-approved 
plans for the construction of accessory dwelling units.” Would an RFQ for providing  
“cookie-cutter” ADU engineering plans for residents be worthwhile? Would this be 
needed as an addition to the code itself, or through another process?

• CRS §29-35-404 speciﬁes that the “rules” are equally applied to attached and 
detached ADUs. Should the code treat attached and detached equally, or should 
there be separate rules? 
 
• As Paonia’s code does not directly address many building aesthetics or historic 
preservation, it is unlikely obstacles to ADUs that may impact other jurisdictions will 
be issues here. However, the new code may address building facades in the C-1 
district, trying to keep the “feel” of downtown Paonia. 
 
Fees – Additions and/or subtractions 
• CRS §29-35-404 suggests “waiving, reducing, or providing ﬁnancial assistance for 
accessory dwelling unit-related fees that are incurred by low- and moderate-income 
households.” Should an income-based building permit fee reduction be mentioned 
in the code with amounts speciﬁed in a separate resolution for ADUs?  
 
• Leslie Klusmire, who is rewriting our code, suggested adding an impact fee to 
building permits to account for the increased use of Town services, roadways, etc. 
This may be another venue for providing a ﬁnancial assistance if adopted. 
Number – how many ADUs per lot? 
• The draft ADU ordinance mentions that only “one ADU per single-family dwelling is 
permitted. ” As it also restricts ADUs to R-1 zoning, this limits new ADUs to one per 
lot, as R-1 is limited to 1 residence. 
 
• Paonia’s current zoning allows up to 2 residences by right in the R-2 district and up 
to 6 by special review; 6 residences by right in the R-3 district and potentially 
unlimited numbers (as long as the lot is big enough) by special review; and 4 
residences by right in the E-1 district and up to 8 by special review.  
 
• Should more than one ADU be allowed per lot (such as 1 per single-family dwelling), 
or should they be limited to one per lot (such as an apartment complex in an R-3 
zone with 1 ADU)? 
Staff recommends limiting ADUs to 1 per lot. This would allow for an additional residence 
beyond what each zone normally allows, creating inﬁll while maintaining distinction 
between the zones, limiting the number of ADUs, and not losing the existing character of 
Paonia. It also avoids the potential complication of not allowing an ADU if the lot does not 
contain single-family residences – for example a building manager’s house on the grounds 
with an apartment complex.

Sale of and ADU separate from the primary dwelling 
•  CRS §29-35-404 encourages “Enabling a pathway for the separate sale of an 
accessory dwelling unit.” 
 
• Currently, to sell an ADU owners must either replat their lots, including providing 
separate sewer and water taps to the second dwelling, or turn the property into 
condos with an overarching association controlling the land itself. Both processes 
involve meetings with the Town Administrator, lengthy applications, and fees. They 
also may involve a public hearing with the Planning Commission and Town Board. 
See PMC Chapter 17 Article 5 for speciﬁcs. 
 
• Is providing a way for ADUs to be sold more easily separately from the primary 
dwelling(s) desirable? If so, should the condominium application requirements be 
struck from the code and individuals be allowed to create joint property ownership 
on a single lot with a written notice to Delta County and meeting Colorado’s legal 
requirements in CRS 38-33 (as Delta County does for mobile homes)?  
 
• Are there other ways of simplifying the process? 
Lots – Setbacks and open space 
• Should the setbacks be adjusted for ADUs (or for all structures) to make inﬁll 
easier? Currently PMC requires a minimum setback of 6’ on sides and 10’ on rear 
lots. Setbacks allow for privacy, but also ensure ﬁres do not spread easily between 
buildings. Any structure within 10’ of another structure requires a 1 hour ﬁre wall on 
both structures to ensure safety. The current setbacks overshoot this by minimally 1’ 
on each side and at least 5’ on the rear. (5’ setbacks on all structures ensures 10’ 
between structures).  
 
• Many of Paonia’s residences already have accessory structures well within the 
setbacks. Converting one of these to an ADU could be done as long as the non-
compliance is not made worse (as in it doesn’t encroach further into the setbacks). 
Having 5’ setbacks on the sides and back would preserve the ﬁre protection and 
allow more room for ADUs. 
 
• Should the minimum landscaped open space be adjusted to allow for more lot 
coverage by buildings?

(III) Accessory dwelling units provide families with options for
intergenerational living arrangements that enable child or elder care and aging
in place, and a 2021 survey by the AARP found that approximately seventy-five
percent of people fifty years of age or older want to stay in their homes or
communities for as long as they can. According to a 2018 study by the Center

desert-a community where there are no child care providers or so few options
that there are more than three times as many children as there are licensed

and urban communities and are a major reason for working parents to leave the
workforce.
(IV) Accessory dwelling units are often occupied at low to no rent by family
members, and if they are rented privately, their rents are relatively affordable
because of their small size;
(V) As Colorado’s population ages and typical household size continues to
decrease, accessory dwelling units offer more compact housing options that
align with the state’s changing demographics, and Coloradans over sixty-five
years of age are the fastest-growing age cohort in Colorado according to the
state demography office;
(VI) Accessory dwelling units enable seniors to downsize, move into accessible
units, or live with family or a caregiver while remaining in their communities. A
2018 AARP survey found that sixty-seven percent of adults would consider
living in an accessory dwelling unit to be close to someone but still have a
separate space. Most seniors do not live in homes that are accessible, even
though disability is prevalent among the senior population and increases with
age. Less than four percent of existing housing units in the United States are
estimated to be livable for people with moderate mobility difficulties, according
to “Housing for an Aging Population” in the journal Housing Policy Debate.
(VII) Relative to dispersed, low-density development, compact infill
development, including accessory dwelling unit development, reduces water

maintenance costs for water, sewer, and transportation infrastructure and lower
property and sales tax revenue. These increased costs are often borne by both
state and local governments.

accessory dwelling unit, or apply regulations to accessory dwelling units that
significantly limit their construction;
(XII) A number of municipalities have removed barriers to accessory dwelling

accessory dwelling unit permits increasing to ten to twenty percent of total new

(XVII) Local government regulation of accessory dwelling units varies
significantly within regions and statewide in Colorado in terms of where they
are allowed, the dimensional and design restrictions applied, and other
requirements. This inconsistency inhibits the development of a robust market
of accessory dwelling unit developers, modular accessory dwelling unit designs,
and associated cost reductions. Colorado is similar to most states in this regard,
and, according to “Zoning By a Thousand Cuts” in the Pepperdine Law Review,
which analyzed accessory dwelling unit regulations across Connecticut, “The
high degree of regulatory variation thwarts the development of prototype
designs or prefabricated [accessory dwelling units] that could satisfy different
rules across jurisdictions”.
(XVIII) More permissive regulation by local governments of accessory dwelling
units provides a reasonable chance for homeowners to construct or convert an
accessory dwelling unit and thereby increase housing supply, stabilize housing
costs, and contribute to affordable and equitable home ownership to adequately
meet the housing needs of a growing Colorado population.

through the construction or conversion of accessory dwelling units is a matter of
mixed statewide and local concern.

(1) The accessory dwelling unit fee reduction and encouragement grant program is created
in the department to provide grants to accessory dwelling unit supportive jurisdictions for
activities that promote the construction of accessory dwelling units, including but not
limited to, offsetting costs incurred in connection with developing pre-approved accessory
dwelling unit plans, providing technical assistance to persons converting or constructing
accessory dwelling units, or waiving, reducing, or providing financial assistance for
accessory dwelling unit associated fees and other required costs.
(2) Grant recipients may use the money received through the grant program to offset both
eligible costs and the cost of waiving, reducing, or providing financial assistance for
reasonable and necessary accessory dwelling unit fees and other required costs for:
(a) Low- and moderate-income households;
(b) Affordable accessory dwelling units;
(c) Accessible or visitable accessory dwelling units;
(d) Accessory dwelling units used as long-term rentals for members of the local
workforce; or
(e) Accessory dwelling units used to support other demonstrated housing needs in the
community.
(3) The department shall administer the grant program and, subject to available
appropriations, provide technical assistance, develop a toolkit to support local
governments in encouraging accessory dwelling unit construction, receive grant
applications and award grants as provided in this section.
(4) To receive a grant, an accessory dwelling unit supportive jurisdiction must submit an
application to the department in accordance with the policies and procedures developed by
the department pursuant to subsection (9) of this section. At a minimum, the application
must include the following:
(a) A copy of the certificate issued by the department pursuant to section 29-35-404
certifying that the local government is an accessory dwelling unit supportive
jurisdiction;
(b) The number of accessory dwelling units that the local government has permitted
and when the local government permitted those accessory dwelling units;
