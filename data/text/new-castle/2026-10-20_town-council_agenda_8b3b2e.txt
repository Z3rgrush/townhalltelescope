Re: Agenda Item: Charter School Presentation 
 
Date: 10/20/2026 
 
Purpose: 
The purpose of this agenda item is to provide time for Judy and Barry Brannberg to present their 
thoughts about opening a Charter School in New Castle and the Colorado River Valley.  
Proposed to RE-2 School District as the Garfield County School of Innovation (GCSI), Judy and her 
husband Barry have submitted a formal application to the School Board and are awaiting a review and 
decision by the School Board.  
If successful with their application, GCSI proposes to open a Charter School in New Castle in 2027 
and add classes in Rifle in year three of their operations. The first year includes Friday and after-
school Youth Center program for grades 6 through 9 in robotics, rocketry, aviation, and other STEM-
focused competitions, clubs, and activities so students on a four-day calendar stay busy and engaged 
in a robust learning environment. Additional programs, location, and grades will be added during the 
following years. 
Having had success on the front range with a very similar charter school model, the Brannberg’s see a 
need in the Colorado River Valley to expand on the great work that RE-2 is doing by offering a 
specialized curriculum with additional educational options for students and parents.

 This memorandum is intended to provide an overview of the requirements and process for 
adopting a comprehensive plan under local ordinances and state law. The Town of New Castle (the 
“Town”) adopted its current comprehensive plan on May 27, 2009. It has not been updated since 
its adoption. The current comprehensive plan can be found here:  
 
https://www.newcastlecolorado.org/media/2706. 
 
I. What is a comprehensive plan? 
 
 A comprehensive plan 1 is an advisory document adopted by a local governing body. It 
provides more general, aspirational, development goals of a community and will guide zoning and 
land use decisions and represents a planning commission’s “recommendation of the most desirable 
use of land. ”2 A comprehensive plan should be made “with the general purpose of guiding  and 
accomplishing a coordinated, adjusted, and harmonious development of the municipality and its 
environs which will, in accordance with present and future needs, best promote health, safety, 
order, convenience, prosperity, and general welfare, as well as efficiency and economy in the 
process of development.”3  
 
A local governing body may make a comprehensive plan binding on land use decisions if 
it chooses to adopt it as part of the land use code “after satisfying notice, due process, and hearing 
requirements for legislative or quasi-judicial processes.”4 
 
Notably, a developer sued the Town in 2022 to challenge the denial of a land use 
application in Castle Valley Ranch. Among other things, the developer challenged the Town’s 
reliance on the comprehensive plan as part of the grounds for denial. The Town prevailed in that 
lawsuit, and District Court Judge Denise Lynch included the following in her order:

II. What do the Town Code and Town Charter have to say? 
 
As a home rule municipality, the Town is given broad powers over matters of local 
concern.5 Article XIV of the Town Charter states that the Town has the power to supersede state 
statutes in matters of local concern to the fullest extent possible. Colorado courts have long 
considered land use regulations a matter of local concern.6 
 
To exert local control over land use matters, the Town established a zoning code and the 
planning and zoning commission. The zoning code is meant to further the goals and policies laid 
out in New Castle’s comprehensive plan.7 All provisions of the zoning code are authorized by Title 
31, Article 23 of the Colorado Revised Statutes, as amended. 8 The planning and zoning 
commission has all powers and authority under the same provisions of the C.R.S. as well as any 
other laws conferred by the State.9 Although the Town is a home rule municipality, which grants 
it ultimate authority in matters of land use, it has chosen to make its zoning powers and planning 
and zoning commission powers concurrent with state law.10 These portions of the zoning code do 
not contain any provisions that seem to conflict with state law, thereby subjecting itself to the 
requirements of C.R.S. Title 31, Article 23, which establishes, among other things, guidelines for 
adopting comprehensive plans.11

III. What must a comprehensive plan include according to state and local law? 
 
 A planning and zoning commission is tasked with creating a comprehensive plan in 
accordance with the requirements of C.R.S. § 31-23-206. Generally, comprehensive plans are only 
advisory in nature unless they are given the force of law as described above, but Colorado law still 
requires that certain elements be included when a municipality adopts or updates a 
comprehensive plan.  
 
 A comprehensive plan must include: (1) a narrative description of the procedure used for 
the plan’s development and adoption, including a summary of any objections made by neighboring 
jurisdictions and how they were resolved; (2) a three-mile plan for expansion through annexation; 
(3) a water supply element, created in consultation with any entity providing the municipality with 
water, estimating a range of water supply and facility needs for potential development as well as 
water conservation policies; (4) a strategic growth element that discourages sprawl and promotes 
development or redevelopment of underutilized areas; (5) most recent housing action plan adopted 
by the municipality , if the municipality meets the thresholds for creating a housing action plan  
under C.R.S. § 24-32-3705;16 and (6) an indication of how the municipality intends to provide for 
the recreational and tourism needs of residents and visitors.17

Elections: Electronic Ballot Return  
Last session, SB26-119 would have allowed municipalities to opt-in to using electronic 
ballot return methods for their statutory elections for all eligible voters, in addition to mail 
in ballots and in person voting. The bill ultimately failed, and proponents are looking to 
bring it back again next session. CML, the County Clerks Association, and the Special 
District Association have all been part of the stake holding process throughout the interim. 
In these discussions, CML has offered an alternative of allowing statutory elections to send 
electronic ballots to voters who qualify under the Uniformed and Overseas Citizens 
Absentee Voting Act (UOCAVA), similar to county coordinated elections.  
 
Labor: Firefighter Cancer Benefits and Workers’ Compensation Presumption 
The Colorado Professional Fire Fighters (CPFF) are expected to bring back legislation 
strengthening the workers' compensation cancer presumption for firefighters, similar to 
SB26-184, which Governor Polis vetoed. That bill would have expanded the types of cancer 
presumed to be occupational diseases under workers' compensation and raised the 
rebuttal standard from a preponderance of the evidence to clear and convincing evidence. 
CML, the Special District Association, the Fire Chiefs, the Colorado Firefighter Cancer 
Trust, and other stakeholders opposed the 2026 bill and have since met with CPFF to 
discuss how to best address their concerns about providing needed care and protections 
for firefighters diagnosed with cancer. CML staff will continue working with CPFF and other 
stakeholders to find a solution that meets the needs of all parties. 
 
Land Use & Utilities: Data Centers 
Last session, two competing data center bills (HB26-1030 and SB26-102) failed in the final 
days of session. For next session, multiple bills addressing data centers are expected:

preempt a local government’s ability to approve a data center development.

requirements on data center developers. The bill would be similar to SB26-102 that 
was supported by environmental groups. In the latest draft of this bill, local 
governments would be prohibited from allowing data centers as a use-by-right and 
would be required to hold a public hearing when considering approving a project. 
Further, the bill draft requires a local government to consider a data center

participant interaction.

 Legislative and policy recommendations may be presented directly to the CML Executive Board 
when time does not permit waiting to present it to the Policy Committee first.

The Colorado Municipal League supports cooperation among local, state, and 
federal officials to provide a strong partnership with Colorado’s cities and towns. 
CML’s dedicated advocacy team is a reliable source of information about legislative 
issues and their impact on Colorado’s cities and towns and their residents.
The CML Policy Statement has evolved throughout the history of the League 
and guides the CML Executive Board, committees, and advocacy team during 
the legislative session and throughout the year. The CML Policy Committee, 
which is open to representation from each municipal member and CML 
professional section, is charged with developing policy recommendations and 
proposing amendments to the Policy Statement. During the business meeting 
(held each year at the CML annual conference), CML members consider any 
recommendations and adopt the Policy Statement for the next year.
The CML Policy Statement consists of several major policy items but is not 
exhaustive. When legislation or policy issues are considered, the CML staff, Policy 
Committee, and Executive Board look first to the Policy Statement to develop 
recommendations and formal positions. If a specific issue is not found within the 
Policy Statement, the Policy Committee and Executive Board will consider and 
establish a CML position, if any. We welcome input and suggestions from members 
on CML policy and positions. We remain proud to be your source for advocacy, 
information, and training.

LOCAL CONTROL &  
MUNICIPAL HOME RULE
As the government most accountable 
to the people, municipal governments 
serve the people best by identifying 
local needs and desires and designing 
the best solutions for local needs. 
State and federal interference can 
undermine the ability of municipalities 
to recognize problems and innovate 
solutions that best meet their needs. 
At worst, state interference can violate 
the constitutional mandate of home 
rule. Therefore, the League:
• Urges state and federal officials to 
respect Colorado’s tradition of local 
control and allow municipal officials 
to address local problems without 
interference from the state and 
federal government.
• Urges Congress and the executive 
branch to respect the roles and 
responsibilities of states and local 
governments and similarly urges 
state officials to avoid preempting 
local authority.
• Supports state enabling legislation 
that provides municipalities with 
authority and flexibility to address 
local needs.
• Recognizes the desire of the 
citizens statewide and in many local 
communities — with adoption of 
a constitutional amendment in 
1902 and expanded amendments 
approved in 1912 and 1970 — to 
establish municipal home rule and 
opposes state action that attempts 
to weaken home rule authority and 
flexibility.
INTERGOVERNMENTAL 
COOPERATION
Citizens are best served when officials 
of federal, state, and local government 
(including municipalities, counties, 
special districts, and school districts) 
respect the roles of each entity and 
work toward common solutions. 
Therefore, the League:
• Supports increased dialogue 
and cooperation among federal, 
state, and local officials and the 
development of cooperative 
intergovernmental solutions to 
common problems. 
• Opposes the imposition of conditions 
on federal or state funding  
that are coercive, violate the constitu -
tional separation of powers, interfere 
with local decision-making and con -
stitutional home rule authority, or are 
not directly related to the  
purposes of the funding.

STATE FISCAL FAIR PLAY
Municipal finances are closely 
interrelated with state finances and 
policies, especially those that impose 
fiscal burdens on municipalities. 
Much state revenue is collected from 
activities and individuals residing in 
municipalities who expect to receive 
a fair share of the benefit of their 
payments. State adherence to fiscal 
fair play policies will greatly help 
municipalities and their citizens. 
Therefore, the League:
• Supports appropriate action to 
resolve state and local financial 
limitations caused by the interaction 
of various constitutional and 
statutory provisions and economic 
fluctuations.
• Supports continued state sharing 
with municipalities of equitable 
portions of existing and future 
revenues derived from funds that 
have traditionally been intended to 
be shared with municipalities, despite 
being collected by the state.
• Urges the state to avoid or exercise

law, and in regulatory programs that

unless the state provides adequate 
replacement revenues.
• Opposes disproportionate cuts 
in state programs that benefit 
municipalities.
• Opposes the state forcing the 
expenditure of local funds or 
requiring local governments to 
collect state revenues to fund state 
programs.

The primary revenue sources for

services and improvements and keep 
municipal property taxes relatively 
low. Appropriate actions at federal, 
state, and local levels should preserve 
or enhance these local revenues.
