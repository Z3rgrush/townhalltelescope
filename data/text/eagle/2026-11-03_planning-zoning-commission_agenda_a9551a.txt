Training 
 
Town Attorney Richard Peterson-Cremer of the law firm Karp Neu Hanlon provided training to the Planning Commission 
regarding its authority, legal responsibilities, and procedural requirements under Colorado law and the Town of Eagle’s 
municipal code. The presentation reviewed the distinctions between quasi-judicial and legislative proceedings, conflict-
of-interest considerations, the Colorado Open Meetings Law, the Colorado Open Records Act, and confidentiality 
obligations. 
1. Planning Commission Authority and Legal Framework 
The Town Attorney reviewed the legal framework governing the Planning Commission, including the Colorado Land Use 
Control Enabling Act, the Town Charter, Title 4 of the Town Code, and the Comprehensive Plan. He explained that the 
Town’s home-rule status provided additional authority over local land-use matters and that the Planning Commission 
served as both a recommending body and a final decision-making body, depending on the type of application. 
The Comprehensive Plan was discussed as a guiding document for legislative decisions and future code amendments. 
2. Quasi-Judicial and Legislative Proceedings 
The Town Attorney explained the distinction between quasi-judicial and legislative proceedings. 
• Quasi-judicial proceedings include development permits, subdivisions, special-use permits, and variances. These 
matters involved applying established code criteria to the facts of a specific application or property. 
Commissioners are expected to maintain impartiality, follow due process, consider the evidence presented, and 
establish a clear record supporting their decisions. 
• Legislative proceedings include code amendments, rezonings, and annexations. These matters involve broader 
policy decisions and generally applicable regulations. The procedural requirements differ from those governing 
quasi-judicial hearings. 
The Town Attorney explained that courts reviewing quasi-judicial decisions generally evaluated whether the decision-
making process was reasonable, followed proper procedures, and applied the law appropriately. He also clarified that 
the Town Council could accept or reject Planning Commission recommendations when acting as the final decision-
making authority. 
3. Ex Parte Communications and Hearing Procedures 
The Town Attorney Richard Peterson-Cremer emphasized the importance of avoiding improper communications outside 
public hearings, particularly regarding quasi-judicial matters. Commissioners were advised to avoid discussing pending 
applications with applicants or members of the public outside the formal hearing process. 
If an outside communication occurred, commissioners are encouraged to disclose it on record and provide the relevant 
information to staff so it can be included in the public hearing record. Commissioners were also advised to consult the 
Town Attorney or planning staff when uncertain about whether communication could affect their ability to participate 
impartially. 
The presentation also covered hearing procedures, including staff and applicant presentations, public comment, 
commissioner questions, deliberations, and motions. The Town Attorney recommended that commissioners focus public 
comment on the Commission rather than engage in direct exchanges with speakers. He also emphasized the importance 
of documenting findings, explaining decisions, and ensuring that conditions of approval were reasonable, achievable, 
and properly worded. 
The Commission discussed its customary practice of asking questions before public comment. The Town Attorney 
indicated that the Chair could determine the appropriate sequence and reopen questions when necessary. 
4. Ethics and Conflicts of Interest 
The Town Attorney reviewed the Town’s Code of Ethics, adopted in 2021, and discussed how conflicts of interest could 
arise in both quasi-judicial and legislative proceedings. 
Commissioners were encouraged to disclose potential conflicts, including personal, financial, employment, property, or 
familial relationships that could reasonably raise questions about impartiality. He explained that disclosure did not 
automatically require recusal and that each situation should be evaluated based on its specific facts.

5. Colorado Open Meetings Law 
The Town Attorney reviewed the requirements of the Colorado Open Meetings Law. He explained that communications 
involving three or more members of a public body could constitute a meeting when they are concerned about public 
business. 
Potential violations could arise through in-person conversations, text messages, emails, shared documents, or serial 
communications in which members discussed an issue individually but collectively reached a decision outside a properly 
noticed public meeting. 
The Town Attorney emphasized that decisions made in violation of the law could be declared void and could expose the 
Town to attorneys’ fees and other legal consequences. 
6. Colorado Open Records Act and Confidentiality 
The presentation covered the Colorado Open Records Act (CORA), which generally made records relating to Town 
business available for public inspection, subject to applicable exemptions. Examples included emails, text messages, 
notes, and AI chat records related to Town business. 
Commissioners were encouraged to use their official Town email accounts for Town-related communications and to 
remain mindful that written communications could become subject to public records requests. The Town Attorney 
explained that communications seeking legal advice from him were generally protected by applicable legal privileges, 
while other communications might be subject to disclosure. 
He also emphasized commissioners’ responsibility to protect confidential Town information and cautioned against 
forwarding legal correspondence or entering privileged legal advice into third-party AI tools, which could compromise 
confidentiality or privilege. 
7. Questions and Discussion 
During the discussion, commissioners asked about the participation of Town Council members at Planning Commission 
hearings, the appropriate order of questions and public comment, the consequences of Town Council decisions that 
differed from Planning Commission recommendations, and the process for proposing amendments during legislative 
proceedings.  
The Town Attorney explained that Town Council members could observe Planning Commission hearings but that 
participating in the proceedings could raise concerns about influencing the outcome. He also clarified that legislative 
proposals could be amended through the appropriate motion procedures and that Town Council generally retained final 
decision-making authority when the Commission served in an advisory role. 
8. Conclusion 
The training concluded with an emphasis on transparency, fairness, impartiality, appropriate documentation, and 
compliance with applicable laws and Town procedures. Commissioners were encouraged to consult the Town Attorney 
and planning staff whenever questions arose regarding conflicts of interest, hearing procedures, communications, or 
legal obligations. 
The presentation was positively received, and the Town Attorney agreed to circulate the training materials for future 
reference. Commissioners were reminded that the Town Attorney remained available as a resource for questions and 
guidance.

Summary of Resolution No. 15, Series 2026 – LUDC 26-05 
The Planning Commission reviewed Resolution No. 15, Series 2026, which proposed amendments to the Land 
Use Code to establish clearer procedures for appeals, referrals, and administrative decisions. The amendments 
were developed in response to direction from Town Council to improve procedural consistency, ensure due 
process for applicants and interested members of the public, and clarify the roles of staff, the Planning 
Commission, and Town Council. 
The proposed amendments eliminated the existing call-up provisions and established appeal procedures at each 
level of decision-making. Each application could be appealed only once, and the appeal process was limited to 
reviewing whether the lower decision-making body had acted reasonably, remained within its jurisdiction, 
provided a fair hearing, and based its decision on the evidence in the record. The appellate body would not 
rehear the entire application or consider new evidence. 
The amendments also established procedures for appeal hearings, clarified standing for applicants, interested 
parties, and the Community Development Director, and limited referrals to those initiated by the Community 
Development Director to the Planning Commission. The Planning Commission would no longer be able to refer 
applications to Town Council without first making a decision. Decisions made by the Planning Commission could 
still be appealed to Town Council when permitted under the Code. 
Additional amendments removed the separate appeal provisions for variances so that they would follow the 
same general appeal procedures as other applications. Table 4-17-1 was revised to clarify decision-making 
authority, identify which decisions could be appealed, and align the table with existing practices. Staff noted 
that additional updates to Chapter 4.17 would be needed to ensure consistency between the code text and the 
revised table. 
During the discussion, commissioners asked questions about the one-appeal limitation, public notice 
requirements for administrative decisions, the handling of new evidence, referral procedures, appeal deadlines, 
and the standards that appellate bodies would apply. Staff clarified that administrative decisions were subject to 
an informational notice period, that appeals would be based on the existing record, and that the 14-day appeal 
deadline was intended to provide a reasonable opportunity to appeal while avoiding unnecessary delays to 
development projects. A commissioner also was curious if the Town wanted to clarify the appeals process within 
Title 4 and the Town Attorney clarified he did not want to provide direction on how to appeal applications in 
district court. 
Following the discussion, the Commission agreed to clarify the proposed language to expressly state that any 
particular application or decision could be appealed only once. No public comments were received in person or 
online.
