Regional Sustainability Project Update 
Background 
The City of Salida and Chaffee County have used Energy Efficiency and Conservation Block Grant 
(EECBG) funding to support a regional sustainability contractor and advance coordinated work in energy, 
transportation, waste diversion, greenhouse gas planning, and community engagement. EECBG funding 
continues through the end of 2026, making this an important point to review progress, upcoming Council 
items, and how the work can continue supporting implementation after the grant period. 
This regional approach is important to community resiliency because many of the issues being addressed 
cross jurisdictional boundaries. Energy use, transportation, waste systems, and climate impacts affect City 
operations, residents, businesses, and regional infrastructure. Coordinated planning helps the City use data 
to identify priorities, pursue outside funding, reduce long-term operating risks, and prepare policies that can 
strengthen the community’s ability to adapt to environmental and economic change. 
Work Supported Through EECBG 
EECBG funding has helped create capacity to move sustainability projects from planning into 
implementation. Current and recent work includes: 
• Energy projects, including contractor workshops and staff training related to energy codes, Energy 
Smart home energy audits, and coordination on Xcel Energy programs. 
• Transportation planning, including the regional EV Readiness Plan, fleet and transit assessments, 
and continued pursuit of electric vehicle charging opportunities. 
• Waste diversion work, including solid waste and construction and demolition waste audits, Extended 
Producer Responsibility implementation support, and public education. 
• Business and community engagement through the Colorado Green Business Network, Green Week, 
workshops, public events, and regional outreach. 
• Technical assistance and grant development that allows the City to evaluate future projects before 
committing local resources. 
Greenhouse Gas Inventory  
The community greenhouse gas inventory provides the City with a baseline for understanding where 
emissions are generated and where local action can have the greatest impact. The inventory found that 
building energy represents the largest combined share of community emissions and transportation is the 
second-largest source. Recommended approaches include improving building efficiency, adopting updated 
energy codes, supporting multimodal transportation and vehicle electrification, and expanding recycling and 
composting.

Staff will continue using the inventory to complete City facility energy assessments to connect near-term 
projects with the City’s longer-term resilience goals. Additionally, staff is using the priorities laid out by 
Council to go after grants and external funding to achieve GHG reduction in our community. 
Upcoming Council Policy Work 
Staff and Sustainable Chaffee are working with Colorado Circular Communities (C3) and Cascadia 
Consulting Group on research and policy recommendations intended to improve recycling and composting 
access and operations. Upcoming items anticipated for Council consideration include: 
• Multifamily housing infrastructure: potential requirements for new multifamily developments and 
qualifying redevelopment projects to provide adequate, accessible space for landfill and recycling 
containers. 
• Waste hauler licensing: potential licensing or permitting concepts that could establish consistent 
expectations, improve information sharing, and support implementation of local and statewide 
recycling programs. 
• Continued alignment with Extended Producer Responsibility implementation, including outreach and 
evaluation of opportunities to improve local recycling access. 
The C3 work is currently focused on background research, local conditions, stakeholder engagement, and 
policy considerations. We expect that the policy proposal would return to Council for review and direction 
this fall. 
Work Through the End of 2026 
During the remainder of the EECBG funding period, staff and the regional sustainability contractor will focus 
on completing grant deliverables, advancing projects already underway, and identifying the highest-value 
next steps for 2027. This includes continued energy and waste policy work, follow-up on the greenhouse 
gas inventory and facility assessments, community education, and development of outside funding 
opportunities that can extend the impact of the EECBG investment. 
Requested Direction from Council 
This item is presented as an update. No formal action is requested.

Community Budget Engagement Survey Framework 
Background 
Traditional city budget surveys often ask residents to rank departments or broad budget categories against 
one another, such as public safety versus parks. Staff is proposing a different approach that would both 
educate residents about the City budget and gather input that can help City Council and staff implement the 
City's strategic priorities. 
The proposed online survey would be promoted through City digital channels, and in-person pop-ups at 
community locations and events such as the Farmers Market. 
Proposed Survey Format 
The survey would use an "Inform and Ask" structure. Each section would begin with a quiz-style question 
that provides context about a City budget issue. Residents would then respond to two to three related 
questions intended to identify community values, preferences, and acceptable tradeoffs. 
The attached survey questions are not a final proposal. They are a starting point for Council review and 
are intended to demonstrate how this format could support the Strategic Priorities laid out by Council. 
Draft Survey Sections 
1. Where does the money come from?
• Informs residents that the City currently receives no property tax revenue and relies more heavily on
other sources, particularly sales tax.
• Asks about approaches to a more stable revenue mix, principles for considering new revenue, and
openness to revenue dedicated to a clearly defined community need.
2. What does it take to maintain the Salida we have?
• Explains how deferred maintenance can turn small issues into more expensive repairs.
• Asks how the City should approach maintenance, balance community access with revenue generation
at public facilities, and weigh existing assets against new investments.
3. What does responsible growth look like?
• Explains that development using existing roads, water, sewer, and other infrastructure can generally
be more cost-effective to serve and maintain than development requiring infrastructure extensions.
• Asks residents to identify priorities related to housing, infrastructure, local business, community
character, mobility, natural resources, and tools that help fund growth-related needs.
Requested Direction from Council 
Staff requests Council discussion and direction on the following:

SECTION 3: What Does Responsible Growth look like? 
10. QUIZ: Which type of growth is generally more cost-effective for a city to 
serve and maintain over time? 
a. Development that uses existing roads, water lines, sewer systems, and 
other infrastructure 
b. Development that requires extending roads, water lines, sewer systems, 
and other infrastructure to new areas 
c. Both generally cost the same 
d. It depends only on the number of new residents 
Correct: A. 
Did you know? When growth occurs near existing infrastructure, cities can often serve 
new homes and businesses more efficiently. Extending roads, utilities, and other 
services into new areas can require significant upfront investment and create additional 
long-term maintenance responsibilities. The location and pattern of growth can affect 
housing availability, transportation options, service levels, and future costs for the 
community.
