Proclamation
WHEREAS, the City of Leawood, Kansas, is committed to ensuring the safety and security of all those 
living in and visiting our city; and
WHEREAS, home fires killed more than 2,133 people in the United States in 2025, according to the 
National Fire Protection Association® (NFPA®), and fire departments in the United States responded to 
329,500 home fires; and
WHEREAS, roughly three out of five fire deaths happen in homes with either no smoke alarms or with 
no working smoke alarms; and
WHEREAS, Leawood residents should test smoke alarms at least once a month because working 
smoke alarms cut the risk of dying in reported home fires almost in half, alerting you to danger in the event of 
fire in which you may have as little as 2 minutes to escape safely; and
WHEREAS, residents who have planned and practiced a home fire escape plan are more prepared and 
will therefore be more likely to survive a fire; and
WHEREAS, Leawood Fire Department personnel are dedicated to reducing the occurrence of home 
fires and home fire injuries through prevention and protection education; and
WHEREAS, the national 2026 Fire Prevention Week TM  theme, “Charge into Fire Safety™ Safe 
Charging is a Superpower™,” serves to remind us the importance of all fire safety concepts, including battery 
safety; and
WHEREAS, the Leawood Fire Department annually provides fire safety education to thousands of 
Leawood school children throughout the month of October.
NOW, THEREFORE, I, Marc E. Elkins, Mayor of the City of Leawood, Kansas, do hereby proclaim 
October 2026, as 
FIRE PREVENTION MONTH
throughout this City, and I urge all the residents of Leawood to make sure their homes have working smoke 
alarms and to support the many public safety activities and efforts of the City of Leawood Fire Department.
IN TESTIMONY WHEREOF, I have hereunto set my hand and caused the Seal of the City of 
Leawood to be affixed this 19th day of October, 2026.
      ________________________________
      Mayor Marc E. Elkins

1. INTRODUCTIONS
Director of Public Works David Ley explained the City hired Burns & McDonnell in April 
2026 to develop the Safe Streets for All (SS4A) Action Plan. He introduced Howard 
Lubliner and Maggie Green from Burns & McDonnell and Anthony Gallo from Kimley-
Horn.
2. ITEMS FOR DISCUSSION
A. Safe Streets for All (SS4A) Presentation
Howard for Lead Co-Safety the Lubliner himself introduced Burns as & 
McDonnell’s transportation practice and the Project Manager for the team leading 
this job. He explained the grant was submitted for this by the Public Works staff in 
2023. The two major components include the primary Action Plan itself, and they 
are also working on the Self Propelled Leawood plan in parallel. He spoke about 
what is required in the Action Plan per the grant, including leadership and goal 
setting, having an Advisory Committee, discussing policy changes, examining 
data, and engaging the public, all of which they have accomplished already. 
Beyond the required elements, Leawood has some flexibility within the Plan’s

design to make it work for them. Then, he talked about the “Safe Systems” 
approach; improving road infrastructure is time consuming, so they are also 
looking at elements like speed control, education for drivers, and quality post-
crash care. The City currently invests in crash data software, so they are working 
together to figure out how the City can make more dynamic choices leveraging 
what they already have. They are also reviewing City policies with a safety lens 
and make potential recommendations for updates, priorities, and alignment. 
Anthony Gallo from Kimley-Horn presented the SS4A Action Plan. He started with 
crash data analysis and the High Injury Network (HIN) to highlight that the Action 
Plan is rooted in data. They have been reviewing crash data from KDOT and 
MODOT from 2015-2024, as that’s what is available, and they have been looking 
at City-connected crash data from 2025-present. He showed the data for the busy 
border streets, which include around 500 crashes a year, and more closely 
highlighted the fatal and serious injury crash data, which includes between 5-10 
crashes a year. Using the HIN, they can determine where the most common 
locations for fatal crashes are. The most critical locations in crash history in the 
City include border streets with other cities, such as 95 th and Mission, State Line, 
and Nall. Within the City boundary, they showed the top 15% within the HIN, 
constituting 77% of all fatalities and serious injuries that have taken place. 
Mr. Lubliner said that sometimes, reducing fatal injury crashes can seem daunting, 
but the HIN gives them a path to be focused and strategic, and even addressing 
only 15% can impact more of the surrounding area. 
Maggie Green from Burns & McDonnell clarified the crash data doesn’t necessarily 
encapsulate everything people are seeing and experiencing, which is why the 
community engagement is critical, as it can show gaps or validate the data, 
helping them plan ahead. She said they have been thoughtful about including 
community engagement at every level in multiple ways, as they recognized that 
was a priority of the City. She reported that internal engagement is also a critical 
component of the process, so they had a vision session early on to lay the 
groundwork and get everyone on the same page, and they kept the City Council, 
Public Works Committee, and the Bike/Walk Committee well-informed as they 
moved forward. 
She stated that other big components are the external stakeholders, such as 
neighboring communities, the Mid-America Regional Council, Johnson County 
Transit, the Health Department, the school districts, etc. They had online 
engagement through their website, and they were out in the community through 
several pop-up events. By the end of the online engagement, they had 257 survey 
responses and 327 pin drops on the map. What they heard was there were a lot of 
comments by the Ward Parkway Mall, several along Lee Blvd., and a few along 
Mission Road. They also heard concerns about high speeds along major corridors 
and a desire for traffic calming and speed enforcement. Pedestrian and bike 
connectivity, a demand to fill sidewalk network gaps and connect different links 
and bike infrastructure, making intersection and crosswalk upgrades, and safe 
routes to schools have all been hot topics of conversation as well.

Ms. Green stated that this anecdotal data can be layered over the HIN to provide 
more insight into making the most informed decisions forward and what 
recommendations to provide. They are looking forward to future stakeholder 
roundtables to have open conversations with key partners, and they will continue 
to hold project Advisory Committee meetings; they also have an open-house plan 
where they’ll share recommendations happening later this year. 
Mr. Lubliner said that the next step is to work with their Advisory Committee next 
week. At the open house, they are looking to identify what the appropriate counter 
measures for the City are. He also said they are going to work with Public Works 
to figure out what the right suite of treatments are for Leawood. He pointed to a list 
of typical urban and suburban counter measures that have been proven to 
improve safety in different ways and said to let them know if anyone has a strong 
feeling about any of them. 
Councilmember Larson talked about how e-bikes and e-scooters have exploded in 
popularity and asked how they could incorporate that into the data. 
Mr. Lubliner replied that the HIN cut off in 2024 because of lag, but they have 
incorporated recent crash trends within the City provided by Police and Public 
Works. 
Mayor Elkins said the challenge is that the e-bikes and e-scooters are new, so 
there is not really a statistically significant amount of data in Leawood. So, relying 
on data of past incidents may not be very helpful for future decisions. He asked 
how they could go beyond the data to help with the e-bikes and e-scooters. 
Mr. Gallo said that instead of using historical data, they will look at national trends 
and research that looks into the evolution of the e-bikes and e-scooters. 
Ms. Green said their team works with the County and the work that the Health 
Department is doing to try and consolidate the information and streamline it so 
there is consistency with educational and policy resources for municipalities. She 
clarified that this issue has been on their radar.
Councilmember Harrison asked if they should ignore the nine border street 
intersections because of the required collaboration with other cities. 
Mr. Lubliner said this issue came up and they have been navigating this with their 
Advisory Committee. He said that with the way things are trending, their 
recommendations will split; they’ll have a set of recommendations around the 
issues the City has autonomy to take action on, and they’ll have another set of 
issues that require partnership. 
Mr. Gallo said their team is plugged in with the Safety Plans of Prairie Village, 
Overland Park, and KCMO, so they are aware of the overlaps of the HIN.

Ms. Green said that at one of the Advisory Committee meetings, 94 th and Mission 
was brought up, as it’s shared by three jurisdictions, and there was an idea to 
potentially apply for joint funding in the future through Mid-America Regional 
Council. 
Councilmember Cain said it would be helpful to mark the schools on the maps and 
emphasized that when it comes to safety, anecdotal data is paramount. She asked 
if there was a way to determine whether it was the speed, or if it was another 
factor, such as a substance or a cell phone, that contributed to the crash data. 
Mr. Lubliner said they can look at causation of crashes, and they can filter the 
crash data by different factors. When they go to make recommendations about 
safety measures, they include a crash diagram in which they examine every crash

if there is no historical data to show that it will make any sort of impact. 
Mr. Gallo said that personally owned e-bikes and e-scooters are relatively new, but 
e-scooter rentals have been around in other communities, so there is some 
precedent for safety best practices. He added that it is conceivable that technology 
is evolving at a rate that they will never be able to be concrete, so some of their 
recommendations may be about how the City can stay ahead of the curve.

Stephen Powell, Assistant City Administrator, said that the Governing Body met on 
August 10, 2026 to discuss the options for the current 1/8 cent sales tax, set to 
expire on June 30, 2028. Those funds are primarily used for street and stormwater 
improvements and generate about $1.5 million a year. At the work session, there 
was a general consensus to propose the increase to 5/8 of a cent. The ordinance

improvements like the mill and overlay program, and would allow them to fund and

stormwater is a priority for the City, as well as the replacement of the metal pipes,

replace the remaining pipes. With the proposed increase, 2/3 of the pipes could be 
replaced in that ten-year period. 
For the street maintenance priorities, the increase would allow for all residential 
streets to be resurfaced in ten years, and it currently takes thirteen. Public safety 
includes various needs for the Police Department and Fire Department. Also, the 
PSIII tax ends in March 2027, creating a large funding gap in their Public Safety
