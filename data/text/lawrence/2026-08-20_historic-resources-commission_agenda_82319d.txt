 HRC-26-0049 1657 Indiana Street; Addition; Certificate of Approval. Submitted by Hernly

Executive Summary  
 The applicant requests to construct an attached garage addition to the west elevation of the 
structure located at 1657 Indiana Street. The addition would be 510 square feet and one 
story. The structure would be clad in fiber-cement lap siding with a 4-inch reveal and would 
have a gabled roof with the pitch matching that of the existing structure. Windows would be 
fiberglass. The applicant also proposes adding a 325 square foot wood deck along the south 
elevation of the existing structure. The property is located in the context area of the 
Twenhofel-Eikenberry House (1655 Mississippi Street) and the Benjamin F. Akers House 
(1645 Louisiana Street) listed in the Lawrence Register of Historic Places. 
  
Chapter 22, Standard 5 and Chapter 22, Section 22-508(c)(2) apply to the project.  
  
The proposed new construction meets the majority of the design criteria in Chapter 22. Staff 
typically supports detached garages in historic areas that have a development pattern where 
the majority of historic garages were detached. However, there are some attached garages 
in the context areas, and the architectural design of the proposed addition helps to mitigate 
the impact of the addition.  The project would also maintain an open area to the south and 
east of the addition that would help to maintain the overall development pattern of the 
context areas.  The only concern for staff is the size and design of proposed deck on the 
south side of the structure.  This type of deck is not typical of the historic patterns of the 
area. Staff would recommend the applicant consider a reduction in the size of the deck to 
help mitigate the impact of the atypical element. While the proposed deck is of some concern 
for staff, the overall project meets the intent of Chapter 22 for new additions in context areas.

Project Review 
The existing structure located at 1657 Indiana Street was constructed circa 1920 according 
to the recent update to the University Place Neighborhood Historic Resources Survey 
(https://khri.kansasgis.org/index.cfm?in=045-3517). The structure is a good example of a 
Craftsman bungalow. The structure is not currently listed in any historic register; however, 
the recent survey has identified the structure as eligible as a contributing structure to a 
potential historic district for the National Register of Historic Places and potentially eligible 
for individual listing in the National Register of Historic Place s for its architecture.  The 
applicant and owner should be advised that the proposed project may negatively impact this 
determination. If the owner is interested in listing in the National Register, they should 
contact the State Historic Preservation Offic e to determine if this proposed addition and 
deck would alter the initial determination of eligibility.

The property is located in the context area of the Twenhofel -Eikenberry House (1655 
Mississippi Street) and the Benjamin F. Akers House (1645 Louisiana Street) listed in the 
Lawrence Register of Historic Places. 
According to the nomination, the  Twenhofel-Eikenberry House located at 1655 Mississippi 
Street was built in 1916 for W. H. Twenhofel, a geology professor at the University of Kansas.  
Twenhofel began teaching at the University of Kansas in 1910 and in 1915 he became State 
Geologist.  In 1 916 he sold the house and moved to the University of Wisconsin where he 
remained for twenty -nine years.  W. L. and Florence Eikenberry bought the house in 1916.  
W.L. Eikenberry taught science education in the School of Education at the University of 
Kansas. The Twenhofel -Eikenberry house is a well -preserved example of the Craftsman 
style and is associated with the development and significance of the University of Kansas in 
the Lawrence economy and community during the “Quiet University Town” period in the 
early twentieth century.  The Twenhofel-Eikenberry House is an example of the side-gabled 
roof subtype of the Craftsman style of architecture. 
 
  
Figure 6: Twenhofel-Eikenberry House Figure 7: Akers House 
Although the exact date of construction is not documented, the B.F. Akers house was built 
between 1872, the date when Akers bought the property, and 1874, the date of a loan application.  
A historic photograph dated 1873 shows the structure with scaffoldin g on the east and north 
sides. Akers acquired an additional thirty -six acres from Andrew and Mary Ryan on March 31, 
1873.  At the time of this purchase, the property was adjacent to the City of Lawrence.  A business 
enterprise, a stud farm, stables and a training course, was created with Colonel Amasa Sprague 
of Rhode Island . After the panic of 1873, Colonel Sprague was forced to withdraw from the 
enterprise in 1874, but Akers continued.  In the 1870s, the Stud Farm had approximately three 
hundred of stock there at any time.  Akers held annual sales at the farm where he would sell as 
many as two hundred horses at each sale.  The nomination sites an interview with Ilene Ecord, 
granddaughter of B.F. Akers, that Ulysses Grant stayed overnight in the Akers residence while on 
a horse-buying expedition. The Akers house was listed for its architecture, an elaborate example 
of the Italianate style  that has a cruciform plan with an intersecting hipped roof  and for its 
character, interest, or value as part of the development, heritage or cultural characteristics of the 
community, county, state, or nation  as an example of a large tract of land that was developed 
over time into smaller lots and eventually a residential subdivision.

Standard 5 in Chapter 22, Section 22-507, and the Design Criteria in Chapter 22, Section 22-
508 ally to this project. 
 
Standard 5 states that contemporary design for new construction of primary and accessory 
structures and additions to existing properties shall not be discouraged when such 
construction and additions do not destroy, hide, or otherwise negatively impact hist orical, 
cultural, or architectural material, and the design is compatible with the size, scale, massing, 
setbacks, material, and character of the designated landmark or historic district, or the 
context areas thereof. The proposed new construction will not destroy, hide, or otherwise 
negatively impact historical cultural, or architectural material. The design of the proposed 
addition is compatible with the architectural styles of the context area. The proposed 
materials are compatible with the structures in the context area. The size and scale of the 
proposed addition is  compatible with other structures in the context area. However, 
attached garages are not typical in the context areas although some do exist. The footprint 
map below  shows that the majority of the garages are detached garages in the context 
areas. While attached garages are not typical for the historic patterns, some of the residential 
structures that were built toward the mid -twentieth century have attached garages.  The 
applicant is proposing a small hyphen area to separate the garage area from the existing 
house. This helps to differentiate the attached garage as an addition to the historic 
structure. 
 
 
Figure 8: Map of Area Showing the Development Pattern of Structures  
 
Chapter 22, Section 508(c)(2) identifies that the design for new construction and additions 
shall be compatible to, and take into account, the special characteristics that the landmark, 
historic districts, or the context areas thereof are established to protect. Such consideration 
may include, but should not be limited to building scale, height, orientation, site coverage, 
spatial separation from other buildings, setbacks, façade and window patterns, entrance and 
porch size and general design, materials, te xtures, architectural details, roof forms, 
emphasis on horizontal or vertical elements, walls, fences, landscaping, and other features

deemed appropriate by the Commission. New additions need not duplicate older styles of 
architecture but must be compatible in exterior design, size, scale, massing, setbacks and 
materials with the architecture on the landmark property, within the historic district, or 
within the context area of a designated landmark or historic district. 
 
The Design Criteria in Section 22 -508(c)(2) help to assess the compatibility of new 
construction.  
 
Height 
New additions shall not be taller than the primary structure in the context area unless 
recessed 30 feet from the front wall plane of the primary structure. The new addition will be 
approximately 16 feet tall.  The existing structure is approximately 20 feet from grade to the 
peak of the roof. There is an elevation change on the property from east to west. The primary 
structures in the context areas are one and two stories tall.  The height of the proposed 
addition is compatible with other accessory structures in the context area and is not as tall 
as the primary structure. 
 
Massing  
The mass of new construction or addition should reflect the forms of the listed property, 
structures within the historic district, or structures in the context area. The massing of the 
proposed addition is similar to other structures in the context area.  It is a simple rectangular 
form.  This is typical in the context area. 
 
Scale   
The scale of new construction or additions should reflect the overall building elements and 
details as they proportionally relate to each other and to the landmark, structures in a 
historic district, and structures in the context area. The scale of the pro posed addition is 
similar to other structures in the context area.   
 
Directional Expression  
New construction should be positioned, both vertically and horizontally, to continue the 
appearance of the orientation of the landmark, structures within a historic district, and 
structures within a context area. The proposed addition is a simple rectangle that is 
connected to the west elevation of the existing structure and continues the linear 
expression of the structure along 17 th Street with the front and rear of the structure facing 
east and west which is typical for the context area.  
 
Setback  
New additions should be a minimum of 20 feet behind the front wall plane of the pertinent 
structure and should be compatible with the adjacent structures in context areas.  The 
proposed addition would be located approximately 50 feet behind the existing front wall 
plane and would be recessed approximately 1 foot from the south wall plane.   This is within 
the range of setbacks in the context area. 
 
Platforms  
New construction should reflect similar platform/foundation elevations from the ground 
that are typical and architecturally compatible with structures in the historic district or 
context area. In areas where the average of the structures has pedestal platforms, new 
construction shall be elevated from land grade. In areas where the average of the structures

is at land grade, new construction can be at land grade.  The proposed addition would be at 
grade with the alley like most of the accessory structures in the context area. The existing 
structure is on a raised platform. There is a grade change from the alley to Indiana Street. 
The proposed hyphen would help to blend the addition with the raised structure. There is a 
mix of at grade and raised platform structures in the context areas. 
 
Roof Shapes 
New construction in context areas shall incorporate roof forms and pitches on the primary

context area.  The proposed structure would have a gable roof.  This is typical for structures 
in the context area.   
 
Sense Of Entry  
New construction in context areas should have the same directional expression of the 
primary pedestrian entrance as the majority of the structures in the context area. This 
entrance should have architectural detail(s) to denote the entrance to the structur e. The 
proposed addition would not alter the existing primary entrance to the structure. The new 
addition would have the secondary entry off of the alley that is typical for the context areas.  
 
Rhythm Of Openings 
New construction in context areas should, at a minimum, incorporate fenestration on the 
primary elevation that is compatible with the structures found in the context area. The 
proposed fenestration pattern for the new addition is compatible with the patterns in the 
context areas with the exception of the north elevation.  There is no fenestration on this 
elevation. While that is typical for accessory structures in the context areas, it is not typical 
for the primary structures in the context areas.   
 
Conclusion 
The proposed new construction meets the majority of the design criteria in Chapter 22. Staff 
typically support s detached garages in historic areas that have a development pattern 
where the majority of historic garages were detached . However, there are some attached 
garages in the context areas, and the architectural design of the proposed addition helps to 
mitigate the impact of the addition.  The project would also maintain an open area to the 
south and east of the addition that would help to maintain the overall development pattern 
of the context areas.  The only concern for staff is the size and design of proposed deck on 
the south side of the structure.  This type of deck is not typical of the historic patterns of the 
area. Staff would recommend the applicant consider a reduction in the size of the deck to 
help mitigate the impact of the atypical element. While the proposed deck is of some concern 
for staff, the overall project meets the intent of Chapter 22 for new additions in context 
areas.
