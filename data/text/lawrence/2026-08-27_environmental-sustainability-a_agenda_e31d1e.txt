A Vision for Environmental Governance in 
Lawrence 
The Environmental Sustainability Advisory Board has developed the Climate Policy & 
Implementation Framework and the Environmental Policy & Initiative Compendium to 
establish a long-term vision for strengthening environmental governance within the City of 
Lawrence. Together, these companion documents are intended to complement the Lawrence 
Strategic Plan, the Douglas County Climate Action & Adaptation Plan, Plan 2040, and other 
adopted City policies by providing a comprehensive framework for measuring environmental 
performance, guiding future policy development, and supporting evidence-based decision-
making. 
Rather than recommending immediate adoption of every policy initiative contained within the 
Compendium, the Board's primary objective is to build the institutional foundation necessary to 
support continuous environmental improvement. Central to this vision is the modernization of 
Lawrence's environmental governance through a revised Annual State of the Environment 
Report, a Community Environmental Performance Measurement Framework, a Public 
Environmental Dashboard, and expanded use of measurable environmental indicators to 
evaluate community progress over time. Together, these tools would provide transparent 
reporting, improve public accountability, strengthen the advisory role of the Environmental 
Sustainability Advisory Board, and support more informed policy decisions by the City 
Commission. 
As the City undertakes the Vision 2030 Strategic Plan update, the Board recommends 
incorporating environmental Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) throughout the Strategic Plan 
wherever appropriate. The Framework and Compendium identify numerous measurable 
indicators related to energy, transportation, natural systems, waste reduction, water resources, 
biodiversity, climate resilience, and environmental quality. Integrating these indicators into the 
Strategic Plan would establish a durable governance structure that embeds environmental 
performance within the City's existing planning and accountability framework, creating a long-
term legal and administrative foundation for future environmental policy rather than a separate or 
stand-alone planning process. 
Public discussion during the July 23, 2026, Environmental Sustainability Advisory Board 
meeting further reinforced this direction while highlighting several initiatives that emerged as 
early areas of focus for future policy development and implementation. These included reducing 
expanded polystyrene (Styrofoam) and single-use plastics, expanding native prairie landscaping 
and urban ecological restoration, advancing renewable energy and development of a 
comprehensive Kansas State Energy Plan, improving bicycle safety and multimodal 
transportation, supporting regional passenger rail between Topeka, Lawrence, and the Kansas 
City metropolitan area, addressing unnecessary vehicle idling, and continuing to strengthen the 
institutional infrastructure needed to support long-term environmental stewardship. 
Page 4 of 16
Taken together, the Climate Policy & Implementation Framework and the Environmental Policy 
& Initiative Compendium represent more than a collection of environmental recommendations. 
They establish a roadmap for integrating environmental sustainability into the City's broader 
governance structure through measurable outcomes, transparent reporting, strategic planning, 
and continuous improvement. By incorporating environmental performance into the Vision 2030 
Strategic Plan and modernizing the State of the Environment reporting process, Lawrence has the 
opportunity to build an enduring system of environmental governance that will support informed 
decision-making, community resilience, and responsible stewardship for decades to come.

I'm talking about our pavement policies--specifically, our community's mandate to require parking 
with new buildings. 
  
For nearly 75 years, we mandated that parking be provided with any new buildings. All too often, 
those rules create more pavement and more parking than people actually use. As a community, 
we repealed some of the mandates last year--but not all of them. I'm asking ESAB to propose 
repealing the remaining parking mandates in Lawrence. 
  
Every parking mandate in our code is a requirement to pour concrete--whether or not it is actually 
used. 
  
This abundance of asphalt does three things, as you probably know. It creates runoff, polluting 
our streams and creeks. It holds heat, which makes our hottest blocks hotter. And it takes up land 
that could be trees, homes, gardens, open space, prairie--anything better and more useful than 
impervious surfaces. 
  
Just last week, the state of North Carolina abolished all parking requirements for all land within 
the state except its coast. It was bi-partisan and nearly unanimous (passed their Senate 44-1 and 
House 111-2). The parking reform bill was championed by an environmental coalition led by 
Catawba Riverkeeper. This is an environmental issue. 
  
I've talked with you about parking requirements before. The ask is simple: that this board 
recommend repealing all parking mandates in Lawrence. This is a simple policy change that could 
prevent a lot of concrete from being poured over our lands. Thank you.

We aim to empower communities to make cost-effective decisions that reduce unnecessary regulations,
support smart growth, increase affordable housing, protect natural areas, and improve quality of life.
Local parking minimums are outdated and costly, limiting development opportunities and negatively
impacting community affordability and wellbeing as well as the health of our land, air, and water.
Support H162 to eliminate burdensome off-street vehicular parking minimum ordinances and clarify
parking space width requirements.
WHAT ARE PARKING AND PARKING WIDTH MINIMUMS?
Parking Minimums: ordinances that specify the minimum number of
off-street parking spaces required for new developments based on
their category and size (e.g., 1 space per 500ft of retail space).
Parking Width Minimums: ordinances that mandate minimum parking
space dimensions based on vehicle size and configuration (e.g.,
standard, compact, angled), typically 8-10ft wide and 18-20ft long.
WHY IS H162 IMPORTANT?
Parking has shaped our communities and way of life by prioritizing
cars over people. As North Carolina grows, parking reform is key to
responsibly boosting business, affordable goods and housing, public
transit and walkability, and health of natural areas for the future.
WHAT DOES H162 DO?
H162 breaks the cycle of blindly repeating outdated, costly parking
requirements without evaluating their impact. By nullifying and
outlawing vehicular parking minimums and clarifying vehicular
parking width requirements, H162 reinstates market-based decisions,
fostering new business opportunities, reducing urban sprawl, cutting
administrative burdens, and minimizing impervious surface area to
reduce flooding and protect natural areas.
WHY ARE PARKING MINIMUMS HARMFUL?
Parking minimum ordinances are expensive for everyone!
Minimums impose costs and space allocation on developers and
business owners, increasing expenses and limiting opportunities.
Added development and maintenance costs are often passed on
to consumers through higher prices for goods, services, or rent.
The space required for parking fragments walkable, tight-knit
communities, contributing to urban sprawl and car dependence.
Excess parking harms natural areas by increasing impervious
surface area, which raises flood risk, generates heat, worsens air
and water quality,  and destroys habitat.

We aim to empower local communities to reduce the volume and velocity of floodwater and runoff
before it reaches local waterways where it damages property and infrastructure.
Many buildings in NC predate our awareness of the relationship between runoff and flooding.
Addressing runoff during redevelopment reduces flooding in downstream areas.
Support H162 to empower local governments to address runoff during the redevelopment process.
WHAT IS RUNOFF?
In natural areas, soil and plants absorb rainfall. However, impervious
surfaces like roofs and parking lots prevent this process, causing water
to run off into nearby waterways. This excess water is called runoff.
As it flows over impervious surfaces, runoff collects trash, motor oil,
fertilizer, and other harmful pollutants. Unlike water that goes down
the sink or toilet, runoff in most areas is not treated before it reaches
these waterways. In NC, runoff is the leading source of water pollution.
WHY IS H162 IMPORTANT?
Redeveloping unused or underused buildings, many of which predate
current best management practices for runoff management, is crucial
to North Carolina’s growth. Under current law, communities can not
require or incentive runoff controls on an redevelopment site. This
restriction hampers local efforts to reduce flooding and destruction in
downstream areas. In order to continue to grow, we must reduce our
flood risk by addressing runoff during the redevelopment process.
WHAT DOES H162 DO?
H162 empowers local communities to incentivize the capture of runoff
from preexisting development during the redevelopment process in
order to balance strategic business growth with the protection of our
waters and communities.
WHY IS RUNOFF HARMFUL?
Following a rain event, runoff rushes into nearby streams at
unnaturally high volumes and speeds, carrying pollutants with it.
Flooding: Excess water entering streams causes rapid flooding of
nearby waterways and downstream properties and businesses.
Erosion: Fast-flowing water erodes stream banks, damaging
property, disrupting aquatic habitats, and increasing flood risk.
Pollution: Pollutants deposited into streams degrade water
quality, harm aquatic species, and raise water treatment costs.

To Whom It May Concern: 
 
We moved to Lawrence this spring after spending 42 years in Denver.   I have been reading about the city's sustainability 
efforts, and wanted to chime in regarding one aspect of that topic.  The context for my remarks is a deep knowledge of 
agriculture, particularly with respect to the sustainable use of water and other resources.  In this regard, I spent quite a lot 
of time trying to persuade Denver Water to try some proven, low cost water savings techniques from the agricultural world 
that could be easily scaled up in the urban context. 
 
It has been my observation since moving here is that lots of water is wasted on lawns and other green spaces.   I have 
seen sprinklers running just after a nice rain, or running on a 100 degree afternoon, as well as being so poorly 
designed/installed that runoff is significant.  Those are the same patterns that I saw in Denver time and time again. 
 
In our Denver yard, which was extensively landscaped, we installed a sensor that gave us soil moisture readings at six 
inch intervals from 0 to 18 inches.  The readings went to an app on my phone.  With that sensor, we cut our water use 
during the summer months by 35%.  My "pitch" to Denver Water was to install a couple of sensors in a neighborhood, and 
then to send text alerts (based on the sensor data) to customers in that neighborhood saying yes/no on watering on a 
given day, and how long to run the sprinkler system.   This effort fell on deaf ears.  The truth of the matter is that Denver 
Water is a $350 million annual revenue behemoth that is not accountable to the citizens.   The sensor to which I am 
referring cost about $750. 
 
I cannot see any reason why this same methodology could not be used in Lawrence on public green spaces as well as 
private property.   While I realize that water usage here is perhaps not as critical of a topic as it is in western regions, it is 
still vital that we conserve this resource.  Sustainable use of water cannot be achieved without answering the two 
fundamental questions--when to water, and how much water to apply. The technology that is readily available today, i.e,. 
the sensors, provide the guidance in this regard. 
 
My two cents worth. I would be happy to chat with you about this topic. I believe that the idea has merit.
