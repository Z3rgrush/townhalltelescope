CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION 
1.1 Purpose 
The Strategic Growth Element (SGE) is a required component of municipal comprehensive planning under Colorado 
Senate Bill 24-174 (SB24-174). It establishes a framework for directing growth toward locations that can make 
effective use of existing infrastructure and services, support housing and economic needs, and consider long -term 
fiscal and environmental impacts.  
By promoting targeted growth patterns, the Strategic Growth Element helps communities identify opportunities to 
accommodate future housing, business, and public investment in locations that maximize existing infrastructure, 
services, and community assets. This approach supports efficient land use while preserving the qualities that make 
each community unique.  
The Strategic Growth Element addresses three required components: 
1. Strategic Growth Policies and Tools 
2. Infill and Redevelopment 
3. Greenfield Development 
1.2 Strategic Growth in Fort Lupton 
Fort Lupton is experiencing continued residential, commercial, and industrial 
growth driven by its location within the Front Range and regional transportation 
network. Unlike many communities planning for anticipated growth, Fort 
Lupton is already experiencing active development, with residential 
construction, employment growth, and new development projects occurring 
throughout the community. 
Strategic growth in Fort Lupton means balancing reinvestment in existing 
neighborhoods and commercial areas with carefully planned expansion that 
aligns with housing needs, infrastructure and water capacity, public -service 
delivery, and long-term fiscal sustainability. The Strategic Growth Element 
provides a framework for evaluating growth opportunities and supporting 
decisions that benefit both current and future residents.  
Growth decisions should consider housing and employment needs, infrastructure and water capacity, 
transportation and public services, long-term fiscal responsibilities, agricultural and environmental resources, and 
community priorities. By directing development to appropriate locations and encouraging reinvestment in existing 
areas, Fort Lupton can accommodate growth while advancing its long -term community, economic, and fiscal goals. 
1.3 Coordinated Approach 
The Strategic Growth Element was developed alongside the Comprehensive Plan update, Water Supply Element, 
and Housing Action Plan as part of an integrated planning framework to guide Fort Lupton’s future growth and 
development.  
The SGE uses these companion efforts to connect growth decisions to the Future Land Use Map, identified housing 
needs, water availability and system capacity, infrastructure planning, and long -term service responsibilities. 
Together, these plans help align land use, housing, public investment, and infra structure decisions to support 
coordinated growth. Detailed relationships to these plans and projects are summarized in Section 2.3.

2.1 Community Overview 
Fort Lupton is a growing Front Range community located in southern Weld County at the intersection of U.S. 
Highway 85 (US 85) and State Highway 52 (Highway 52). The city serves as an important residential, employment, 
commercial, and transportation center within northern Colorado , benefiting from regional access to the Denver 
metropolitan area, Greeley, Boulder County communities, and eastern Weld County while maintaining a distinct 
identity rooted in agriculture, industry, and small-town character.

Land Use Pattern 
The Future Land Use Map establishes Fort Lupton’s intended long-term land-use pattern. Land within the planning 
area is categorized by land use designation, providing a framework for understanding the community's development 
pattern and informing future planning and development decisions. This pattern supports flexibility for long-term 
needs while recognizing physical constraints, agricultural resources, and utility service considerations .  
Several distinct land use areas characterize Fort Lupton: 
• Downtown Fort Lupton and the Denver Avenue corridor function as the community's historic , civic, 
commercial, and mixed-use center.  
• Commercial uses are concentrated along major corridors, including Highway 52 and portions of US  85. 
• Industrial and larger employment-related uses are located along US 85, Northland industrial park, and 
South Rollie industrial district. 
• Residential neighborhoods surround the historic core and are supported by schools, parks, public facilities, 
and community services. 
• Agricultural and rural residential areas remain prominent throughout portions of the broader planning area 
and continue to contribute to the community's identity and economy.  
Together, these land use patterns reflect Fort Lupton's role as a residential, commercial, employment, and 
agricultural community while providing context for the analyses presented throughout this Strategic Growth 
Element.

Development Pipeline 
Fort Lupton is experiencing residential, commercial, and industrial development activity. City staff identified 
approximately 3,200 housing units in various stages of planning, review, approval, or construction, reflecting 
continued market interest and sustained residential growth throughout the community.  
Development activity is occurring in multiple areas of the city, including areas east of County Road 29  (CR 29), north 
and south of Highway 52, and within several recently approved or developing neighborhoods. These projects will 
influence future housing supply, infrastructure planning, transportation investments, school enrollment, and 
community service needs. 
Recent development patterns generally align with the residential growth areas  and priority growth areas identified in 
Chapters 4 and 5. Much of this activity has occurred adjacent to existing neighborhoods and utility infrastructure, 
supporting coordinated and efficient growth patterns while expanding housing and employment opportunities.  
Current development trends and staff discussions suggest future growth will occur through a combination of infill 
and redevelopment within established areas, continued expansion of eastern growth areas, and longer- term 
opportunities associated with strategic corridors and future infrastructure investments  to the west and south.  
2.3 Growth Drivers and Development Considerations 
Future growth in Fort Lupton will be influenced by a range of interconnected factors, including housing needs, 
employment opportunities, public facilities, infrastructure capacity, transportation access, water resources, and 
environmental conditions. Understanding these factors helps identify opportunities and constraints associated 
with future growth and provides important context for the redevelopment, infill, and greenfield analyses presented 
later in this Strategic Growth Element. 
Housing  
Fort Lupton continues to experience population growth while facing increasing housing affordability challenges. The 
city added 20% additional housing units between 2018 and 2023 and grew to more than 9,200 residents. Despite 
this growth, housing demand continues to outpace the availability of diverse housing options across many income 
levels.  
The Housing Needs Assessment identified several trends and priorities that influence future growth decisions, 
including:  
• Increasing housing costs and affordability challenges, as 
home values increased 163% over the past decade.  
• Strong demand for workforce, multifamily, senior and 
attainable ownership housing. 
• A housing stock that remains heavily concentrated in 
single-family detached homes.  
• Limited housing diversity compared to neighboring 
communities. 
• The need to preserve and rehabilitate older housing stock. 
• Continued household growth through at least 2050. 
• Opportunities for infill development, redevelopment, and 
coordinate housing and infrastructure development. 
The Housing Needs Assessment provides an important 
foundation for evaluating both redevelopment and infill 
opportunities in Chapter 4 and longer-term greenfield growth 
opportunities in Chapter 5.

Employment  
Fort Lupton functions as both a residential community and an important regional employment center. Its  strategic 
location supports a diverse economy that includes industrial, commercial, healthcare, educational, public -sector, 
logistics, energy, and transportation-related uses.  
The community is part of a broader regional labor market, with residents commuting throughout Weld County and 
the Denver metropolitan area while many employees commute into Fort Lupton for work. These commuting 
patterns highlight the importance of balancing housing growth, job creation, transportation investments, and 
economic development. 
Future employment growth is expected within existing industrial districts, commercial corridors, mixed -use areas, 
and other designated employment centers identified through the City's long -range planning efforts, helping support 
both economic development and future housing growth.

Fort Lupton benefits from a variety of educational, civic, recreational, public safety, cultural, and healthcare 
facilities that support quality of life and future growth. Key facilities include:

• Local elementary, middle, and high schools.  
• City Hall and municipal facilities.

are needed will remain an important planning consideration.

Natural Resources and Environmental Considerations 
Fort Lupton's natural resources, environmental features, and open spaces play an important role in shaping future 
growth opportunities, development patterns, and community character. Agricultural lands, the South Platte River 
corridor, mineral resource areas, parks, and environmental constraints all influence land use decisions, 
infrastructure planning, and long-term growth strategies throughout the planning area.  
Agricultural Resources  
Agriculture remains a defining component of Fort Lupton's identity, 
economy, and landscape. Working lands continue to support local 
economic activity, preserve rural character, and provide an 
important transition between urban and rural development patterns. 
Important agricultural considerations include:  
• Active irrigated agricultural production areas. 
• Dryland farming operations. 
• Agricultural-support businesses and services. 
• Rural residential properties and homesteads. 
• Irrigation ditches and agricultural water delivery systems .  
• Long-term preservation of productive agricultural land . 
• Managing transitions between urban and rural development 
patterns.  
The Land Use Map reflects the importance of these resources 
through extensive Agricultural and Rural Residential designations 
surrounding the community. 
South Platte River and Water Resources  
The South Platte River is Fort Lupton's most significant natural feature and an important environmental, 
recreational, and open-space resource. The river corridor influences development patterns throughout the planning 
area while providing habitat, recreation opportunities, irrigation infrastructure, and community amenities . 
Important features associated with the South Platte River corridor include:  
• Floodplain and floodway areas. 
• Riparian habitat and wildlife resources.  
• Wetlands and drainage corridors. 
• Open space opportunities. 
• Recreation and trail connections.  
• Active and former mineral extraction areas.  
• Irrigation and water delivery infrastructure. 
Previous planning efforts identified the South Platte River as both an environmental resource and a long -term 
community asset. Future growth decisions should continue to balance development, recreation, environmental 
stewardship, and long-term river corridor protection.

2.4 Key Terms 
The Strategic Growth Element uses several terms to describe different land conditions and development 
opportunities. The following definitions reflect Fort Lupton’s local context and provide a consistent framework for 
the analyses in Chapters 4 and 5. 
Term Definition 
Vacant 
A property with no primary developed use or substantial permanent 
improvement based on available assessor and parcel information. This 
classification does not necessarily mean the property is unused or 
available for development. 
Partially Vacant 
A developed property containing a mixture of active development and 
potentially developable residual land. These areas may support future 
expansion, redevelopment, or infill opportunities. 
Underutilized 
A developed or partially vacant property that may have reinvestment or 
redevelopment potential based on its use, intensity, condition, location, 
or surrounding development pattern. The term does not imply that a 
property is blighted or required to redevelop. 
Infill Development 
Development on vacant or underutilized land within established areas 
where infrastructure and public services are generally available, although 
site improvements may still be required. 
Redevelopment 
Reinvestment in an existing developed property through reuse, 
rehabilitation, adaptive reuse, expansion, replacement, reconstruction, or 
a change in use. 
Greenfield Area 
A generalized area containing vacant, agricultural, undeveloped, or 
minimally developed land at the edge of or outside established 
development that may support future strategic growth. These areas do not 
represent parcel-specific development sites or approvals.

CHAPTER 4: INFILL AND REDEVELOPMENT ANALYSIS 
This chapter identifies infill and redevelopment opportunities and evaluates their general suitability  for future 
housing and other compatible uses. The analysis combines assessor -classified vacant and underutilized properties 
with an area-based evaluation of proximity to employment and community destinations, infrastructure, 
transportation, public services, environmental conditions, zoning, and Future Land Use. Site- specific availability, 
capacity, ownership, financing, design, and market conditions must be evaluated as projects are proposed. 
4.1 Analysis Approach 
The infill and redevelopment analysis uses a district- and corridor-based approach rather than focusing solely on 
individual properties. The approach recognizes that infill and redevelopment potential is influenced not only by 
property characteristics, but also by proximity to jobs, services, infrastructure, transportation facilities, and 
community assets. The resulting opportunity areas identify locations where reinvestment, redevelopment, housing, 
and mixed-use development may be supported by surrounding land uses, infrastructure, and amenities.  
Vacant and Underutilized Property Identification 
Vacant and underutilized properties were identified using assessor data, quantitative screening methods, 
mapping analysis, staff work sessions and local review. Vacant properties were initially identified using county 
assessor property descriptions and classifications, then refined based on development activity, site-specific 
conditions, and local knowledge.  
Underutilized properties were screened using building-to-land value ratio methods consistent with state guidance 
to identify properties where existing improvements may not reflect the property's development potential. 
Agricultural properties were excluded because they are evaluated differently than developed properties . Staff 
further refined the results based on redevelopment potential, market conditions, and community priorities.  
The resulting inventory includes vacant, partially vacant, and underutilized properties, as well as staff-identified 
property opportunities. Inclusion in the inventory does not indicate readiness for development or the likelihood of 
redevelopment. Rather, it identifies locations that may warrant further consideration based on redevelopment 
potential, strategic location, infrastructure conditions, market factors, or other site- specific characteristics.

Development feasibility varies among Fort Lupton’s vacant, underutilized, and redevelopment priority properties . 
Financing constraints, utility capacity, infrastructure timing, market conditions, parcel configuration, property

develops. These factors also influence the level and timing of private and public investment that may be needed to 
support infill, redevelopment, adaptive reuse, and mixed-use development. 
The cost classifications used in this scenario evaluation represent planning -level orders of magnitude for 
development-enabling infrastructure and site improvements, rather than project-specific cost estimates or total 
development costs. For comparative purposes, lower-cost improvements are generally anticipated to total less

improvements may range from approximately $2 million to $5 million; and major transformational infrastructure 
investments may exceed $5 million. All ranges are expressed in 2026 dollars and should be adjusted over time to 
account for construction-cost escalation. 
These ranges provide a consistent basis for comparing the relative scale of potential improvements among the 
scenarios. They should be refined through site-specific engineering, utility -capacity analysis, environmental review, 
construction-cost estimating, market and financial analysis, and the development-review process.

Public funding participation may be considered when an improvement:  
• Addresses an existing infrastructure deficiency not created solely by the proposed development . 
• Provides capacity beyond the needs of an individual project. 
• Serves multiple properties or a larger development area . 
• Advances an adopted capital, utility, transportation, drainage, housing, or redevelopment objective . 
• Produces a broader community or corridor-wide benefit. 
• Would otherwise impose a disproportionate cost relative to the development’s anticipated impact .

Comparative Findings 
The three scenarios demonstrate that development costs and serviceability are influenced by more than the 
distance to an existing utility line. Existing system capacity, site access, parcel configuration, building conditions, 
development intensity, drainage requirements, and compatibility with surrounding uses all affect project feasibility.  
Several comparative findings emerge: 
• Downtown, including Denver Avenue and Main Street, generally offer the greatest opportunity to reuse existing 
buildings, streets, utilities, and public facilities, although rehabilitation and adaptive reuse costs may be 
substantial. 
• The Northern Rollie Avenue Mobility Corridor may support incremental housing and neighborhood -scale 
redevelopment, with investments focused primarily on localized infrastructure, drainage, access, and 
pedestrian connectivity. 
• The Highway 52 corridor benefits from regional visibility, transportation access, utilities, employment, and 
commercial activity but may require more substantial access, traffic, drainage, and corridor- related 
improvements. 
• Projects located within established areas may require less geographic expansion of infrastructure and public 
services than development on previously undeveloped land. 
• Existing infrastructure proximity does not guarantee available capacity, an approved connection, or lower total 
project costs. 
• Public investments that serve multiple properties or address corridor- wide needs may provide broader benefits 
than improvements designed for a single project. 
• Development agreements and project approvals should clearly identify responsibility for construction, utility 
upgrades, off-site improvements, maintenance, and eventual replacement.  
• Infill and redevelopment projects may require financial or regulatory assistance when rehabilitation, 
infrastructure, environmental, or site constraints create a gap between project costs and market value.

Lifecycle Funding Needs 
The cost of infrastructure is not limited to the initial construction of a road, water line, park, or public facility. Every 
public asset must be operated, maintained, repaired, and eventually replaced. A lifecycle funding approach 
recognizes these costs and considers the resources needed over the life of the asset to ensure infrastructure 
remains functional and does not deteriorate over time.  
Lifecycle funding requirements vary considerably by infrastructure type. Water and wastewater collection systems 
often have useful lives of 50 years or more and may require annual funding in the range of 2% to 4% of replacement 
value when operations, maintenance, rehabilitation, and eventual replacement are considered.  Roadway systems 
generally require more frequent preservation and rehabilitation activities and may require annual funding levels of 
4% to 8% of replacement value. Public facilities, parks, and treatment systems may require even higher annual 
investment due to shorter equipment replacement cycles and ongoing operational requirements. Consequently, 
communities should evaluate lifecycle costs by asset type rather than applying a single funding factor across all 
infrastructure systems. 
The table below presents planning-level lifecycle funding benchmarks expressed as a percentage of an asset's total 
replacement value. These benchmarks are intended to illustrate the potential scale of long -term infrastructure 
obligations associated with new development. 
 
Asset Type Typical Lifecycle Funding Need 
Water distribution mains 2% to 4% 
Sewer collection systems 2% to 4% 
Streets and pavement systems 4% to 8% 
Stormwater systems 2% to 4% 
Parks 5% to 7% 
Buildings and facilities 4% to 8% 
 
For example, if a community's water distribution system has a replacement value of $10 million, a lifecycle funding 
target of 2% to 4% annually would equate to approximately $200,000 to $400,000 per year for ongoing operations, 
maintenance, rehabilitation, and future replacement.  
Similarly, a roadway network valued at $20 million with a funding target of 4% to 8% annually would require 
approximately $800,000 to $1.6 million per year to preserve pavement conditions, complete major repairs, and 
prepare for future reconstruction.

CHAPTER 6: STRATEGIC GROWTH RECOMMENDATIONS 
AND IMPLEMENTATION 
This chapter translates the findings of the Strategic Growth Element into recommendations for future land use, 
housing, infrastructure, and community investment decisions. The recommendations are organized around 
coordinated growth planning, infill and redevelopment opportunities, and long -term greenfield growth 
considerations (Chapters 3, 4, and 5).  
The potential policies and tools identified in Section 3.2 provide options the City may consider to help implement 
these recommendations. The appropriate tool or combination of tools will depend on the specific issue, available 
resources, staff capacity, development conditions, and future City review and approval. Implementation should 
continue to consider Future Land Use direction, infrastructure and water availability, development feasibility, 
community priorities, and long-term fiscal responsibilities. 
6.1 Strategic Growth Policy and Tool Recommendations 
Recommendation 1: Coordinate Growth with Infrastructure, Utilities, and Water Resources 
Future growth decisions should be coordinated with water, wastewater, transportation, drainage, utility planning, 
and capital investments. Areas with existing infrastructure or planned service improvements should generally 
receive priority consideration for future development. 
Potential implementation tools include:  
• Future Land Use Map consistency review. 
• Utility extension and serviceability review. 
• Capital improvement planning coordination. 
• Water Supply Element implementation.  
Recommendation 2: Use the Growth Boundary to Guide Long-Term Planning  
Continue using the Growth Boundary as the primary framework for evaluating future housing, employment, 
infrastructure, transportation, environmental, and economic development opportunities. The boundary should 
serve as a planning tool for evaluating growth alternatives and coordinating long -term investment decisions, not as 
an indicator of development approval, annexation priority, or infrastructure commitment. 
Recommendation 3: Monitor Growth Trends and Update the Strategic Growth Element 
Future updates should incorporate current data regarding development activity, infrastructure investments, 
housing conditions, environmental resources, redevelopment opportunities, and greenfield growth areas. Periodic 
monitoring and updates will help ensure the Strategic Growth Element remains aligned with community conditions, 
infrastructure capacity, and long-term planning objectives. 
6.2 Infill and Redevelopment Recommendations 
Recommendation 4: Prioritize Infill, Redevelopment, and Reinvestment  
Direct future investment toward established areas that already benefit from infrastructure, transportation access, 
community facilities, and public services. This approach can maximize existing infrastructure investments, support 
economic activity, and reduce pressure for outward expansion. 
Priority areas include:  
• Downtown, Denver Avenue, and Main Street.

• Rollie Avenue and Denver Avenue Corridor.  
• US 85 Corridor.

Recommendation 5: Expand Housing Opportunities and Diversity  
Support housing choices that respond to needs identified in the Housing Needs Assessment, including: 
• Single-family infill development. 
• Townhomes and attached housing.  
• Multifamily housing. 
• Mixed-use residential development. 
• Workforce housing.

uses, and future land use objectives. 
Recommendation 6: Support Downtown Revitalization and Employment Centers  
Continue supporting reinvestment, redevelopment, and economic development within Downtown Fort Lupton, 
Highway 52, US 85, and other identified employment and activity centers. 
Future planning efforts should encourage: 
• Small business development. 
• Commercial reinvestment. 
• Adaptive reuse. 
• Employment growth.  
• Housing near jobs and services. 
• Expansion of community serving uses. 
6.3 Greenfield Development Recommendations 
Recommendation 7: Focus Greenfield Growth in Areas with Existing or Planned Infrastructure  
Not all greenfield areas have the same level of development readiness. Future growth should be directed toward 
locations where infrastructure, utilities, transportation facilities, and public services can be provided efficiently.  
Consideration should be given to: 
• Eastern Growth Areas. 
• Northland Industrial Park. 
• Airport Opportunity Area. 
Recommendation 8: Continue Planning for Strategic Growth Corridors and Employment Areas 
CR 8, CR 19, the Airpark area, Northland Industrial Park, and other strategic employment areas should remain 
important components of Fort Lupton's long-term planning framework. 
Future planning efforts should continue evaluating:  
• Transportation improvements. 
• Utility extensions.

• Industrial and commercial opportunities.

Recommendation 9: Use a Consistent Evaluation Framework for Growth Opportunities 
Future redevelopment, infill, annexation, infrastructure, and greenfield growth decisions should be evaluated using 
a consistent framework that considers infrastructure availability, utility serviceability, transportation access, 
environmental conditions, housing needs, fiscal impacts, and long -term service responsibilities. 
The analyses in this Strategic Growth Element demonstrate that growth areas vary significantly in their level of 
infrastructure readiness and serviceability. While redevelopment and infill opportunities generally benefit from 
existing infrastructure and community assets, many greenfield areas may require additional utility extensions, 
transportation improvements, and public investment before development becomes feasible. 
When evaluating future development proposals, annexation requests, infrastructure investments, and capital -
improvement priorities, the City should consider: 
• Existing and planned utility infrastructure. 
• Transportation access and mobility. 
• Relative infrastructure extension costs. 
• Long-term operations and maintenance obligations. 
• Public safety and service delivery impacts. 
• Housing and economic-development objectives. 
• Agricultural, environmental, and resource considerations. 
• Opportunities to leverage planned public investments. 
Applying a consistent evaluation framework will help Fort Lupton prioritize investments, compare growth 
alternatives, and direct development toward locations where infrastructure and services can be provided most 
efficiently.
