EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
Staff will present key takeaways from a draft Technical Memorandum to the Housing Needs & Affordability 
Assessment (HNA).  The Technical Memorandum supplements the HNA that was accepted in April, 2025 by 
providing additional analysis of the most current data available as well as incorporating new guidance for HNA 
evaluations.  Staff is seeking feedback and direction regarding the content and presentation of the Technical 
Memorandum. 
  
BACKGROUND / DISCUSSION 
In 2024, the Town contracted with Matrix Design Group to complete a Housing Needs & Affordability 
Assessment (HNA) to comply with state requirements stipulated in SB24-174. In April, 2025, the Planning 
Commission accepted the Town's HNA, and the Department of Local Affairs (DOLA) accepted the document 
in January, 2026. The accepted HNA is available for review on the Town's website - Wellington Housing 
Needs & Affordability Assessment. 
 
As the Town anticipates undertaking studies for housing plans and policies, including a Housing Action Plan 
(HAP) required by SB24-174, it is important to ensure that the HNA has the most up-to-date information 
available. The accepted HNA employed the 2022 American Community Survey (ACS) dataset as its principal 
data source. Following the HNA's creation and acceptance, more recent 2024 ACS data has been released by 
the U.S. Census Bureau. The Town engaged with Matrix Design Group to create a Technical Memorandum to 
the HNA to incorporate the more recent data. 
 
In addition to incorporating the new ACS data, the memorandum also incorporates new Department of Local 
Affairs (DOLA) guidance for evaluation of an HNA, including evaluations for displacement risk, employment 
growth projections, and homelessness data. The memorandum does not propose any new policies or 
recommendations or alter any existing policies or recommendations.  The policies and recommendations of the 
accepted HNA are still valid and remain unchanged. The HNA, supplemented by this memorandum, provides a 
good foundation for the upcoming Housing Action Plan anticipated to be completed in 2027. 
 
Staff is requesting feedback from the Planning Commission regarding the memorandum's content and 
presentation. Feedback will be incorporated into the final memorandum and will be brought back to the 
Commission for consideration in December. 
  
STAFF RECOMMENDATION 
This item is for presentation and discussion purposes. No action will be taken.

Wellington Housing Needs & Affordability Assessment Update: Technical Memorandum 1 
Introduction 
In July 2026, the Town of Wellington retained Matrix Design Group to prepare this SB 24 -174-aligned technical 
memorandum updating the Town's 2025 Housing Needs and Affordability Assessment. The purpose of this update is to 
provide an updated evaluation of Wellington's housing market conditions, housing needs, and affordability challenges 
using quantitative indicators recommended by the Colorado Department of Local Affairs (DOLA). The memora ndum 
examines demographic and economic trends, housing inventory and market conditions, homelessness and displacement 
risk, jobs-housing relationships, and current and future housing needs. These analyses provide a data-driven foundation 
to support the Town’s forthcoming Housing Action Plan. 
Key Findings 
◼ Wellington’s population nearly doubled between 2010 and 2024, reaching almost 12,000 residents, while the
number of households increased from 2,188 to 4,316. This sustained growth is placing increasing pressure on the
local housing market and contributing to demand for additional housing across a range of price points.
◼ Approximately 86% of housing units are single-family detached homes, while the overall vacancy rate is just 1%,
substantially below regional and statewide rates. The combination of limited housing diversity and extremely low
vacancy levels restricts housing choice and contributes to competition for available units.
◼ Wellington's median household income remains higher than that of Larimer County, Weld County, and Colorado
overall, yet a substantial share of households experience housing cost burdens. Over one-in-five Wellington
households earn less than 50% of Area Median Income (AMI), and renters are disproportionately represented
among the community's lowest-income households.
◼ Regional homelessness has increased since 2020, while displacement risk indicators suggest that vulnerability to
housing-related pressures varies within Wellington, with greater vulnerability in north-central Wellington. Overall,
however, Wellington exhibits low displacement risk compared with the broader region, although elevated
housing cost burdens and rapid recent rent growth remain important trends to monitor.
◼ Additional housing will be needed to address current shortages and accommodate future demand. Wellington's
current housing need is estimated at 273 to 432 units, while cumulative housing need is projected to reach 877 to
1,034 units by 2034. Need exists across all income levels but is particularly pronounced among households
earning 80% of AMI or less, underscoring the importance of expanding housing options that are affordable to
lower- and moderate-income residents.

Demographic and Economic Trends 
This section examines the demographic and economic characteristics that influence housing demand in Wellington. The 
findings show a community that has experienced rapid population and household growth, maintains relatively high 
household incomes, and supports a diverse employment base. These trends provide important context for understanding 
local housing needs, affordability challenges, and the demand for housing across a range of price points.  
Population 
Wellington has experienced substantial population growth over the past several decades, with the community expanding 
from approximately 1,215 residents in 1980 to nearly 12,000 in 2024 (see Figure 1). Growth accelerated particularly 
during the late 1990s and 2000s, when the population increased from 2,268 in 1998 to 6,295 in 2010. Wellington 
continued to grow steadily through the 2010s and early 2020s, reaching 11,997 residents in 2024. According to the 
Town’s official projections, this growth is expected to continue for the foreseeable future, with the population forecasted 
to reach approximately 25,000 in 2040—an increase of 108% from 2024. Continued population growth will generate 
additional demand for housing, infrastructure, and community services, while also reinforcing the need for a diverse 
housing supply capable of accommodating both existing residents and future households.

Household Characteristics 
Household formation in Wellington has accelerated considerably since 2010. The number of households increased from 
2,188 in 2010 to 4,316 in 2024, nearly doubling over the 14-year period (see Figure 2). The most pronounced gains 
occurred between 2014 and 2017, when Wellington added more than 800 households. The Town’s 2040 projected 
population of 25,000 is projected to be divided across 8,955 households, representing an increase of about 107% 
from 2024. This projected increase equates to roughly 4,600 additional households through 2040. Such substantial 
household growth will influence nearly every aspect of community development, highlighting the importance of proactive 
planning to maintain quality of life, support public services, and ensure that the benefits of growth are broadly shared  
among current and future residents.

Income 
Wellington households have historically reported higher median incomes than households across the surrounding region, 
although the Town experienced a decline in inflation-adjusted median household income between 2019 and 2024 (see 
Figure 3). Wellington’s real median household income was $112,351 in 2019, compared with $88,750 statewide, $88,198 
in Larimer County, and $90,982 in Weld County. By 2024, Wellington’s median household income had decreased to 
approximately $107,000, while typical incomes increased to $95,470 in Colorado, $93,765 in Larimer County, and 
$97,097 in Weld County. However, part of Wellington’s observed decline may be a statistical artifact arising from sampling 
variability rather than a meaningful change in underlying economic conditions, particularly given the relatively small ACS 
sample available for a community of Wellington’s size. Despite the decline, Wellington’s median income remained more 
than $11,000 higher than the statewide figure and $10,000–$13,000 above the two counties. The Town’s 
comparatively strong household incomes indicate a relatively favorable capacity to support housing costs, but rising 
housing prices and other household expenses may place increasing pressure on affordability for lower - and moderate-
income residents.

Median household income varies substantially by tenure in Wellington, with notable differences between renters and 
homeowners and changes in the relative position of each group since 2019 (see Figure 4). In 2019, Wellington renters 
had a real median household income of $113,089, more than twice the levels observed in Larimer County ($52,924), 
Weld County ($53,813), and Colorado ($57,977). By 2024, Wellington’s renter median income had fallen to $74,973, 
though it remained above the comparison areas, where renter incomes ranged from $54,856 in Weld County to $63,537 
statewide. Among homeowners, Wellington’s median household income was $112,102 in 2019 and decreased 
slightly to $108,384 in 2024, while homeowner incomes grew in Larimer County, Weld County, and Colorado. As a 
result, Wellington homeowners’ median income in 2024 was below the corresponding figures for all three comparison 
geographies, although these differences should be interpreted with caution as they were not tested for statistical 
significance. Applying the commonly used standard that housing costs should not exceed 30% of household income, the 
median Wellington homeowner could afford approximately $2,710 per month in housing costs, compared with $1,874 for 
the median renter. Overall, the results show that Wellington’s decline in median income was not experienced equally 
across tenure groups; among households captured by the ACS sample, renters experienced substantially larger income 
declines than homeowners.

Commercial Vitality 
Wellington has experienced continued business openings in recent years, although new registrations have fluctuated 
while business closures have generally increased (see Figure 5). From 2019 to 2023, new businesses ranged from 
roughly 12–13% of all businesses, but in 2024, this share fell to roughly 9%. In contrast, closures represented over 
3% of business activity in 2023 and 2024, a higher share than in earlier years. The fact that registrations continue to 
substantially outpace inactivations indicates continued net business formation. Nonetheless, the recent increase in 
business closures warrants monitoring. While business turnover is a normal feature of a growing economy, elevated 
closure rates can signal shifts in local market conditions and consumer demand. In some communities, changes in the 
mix of active businesses have accompanied broader demographic and economic transitions, including the loss of long -
standing businesses that primarily serve lower-income households and the emergence of businesses catering to higher-
income residents. As a result, business turnover may not only contribute to economic vulnerability for some workers and 
entrepreneurs but may also serve as an indicator that displacement-related changes are already occurring within the 
community. Although the available data do not establish a direct causal relationship, continued monitoring of both 
business activity and housing affordability trends can help identify whether changes in the local commercial environment 
coincide with broader shifts in Wellington's resident population.

Housing Inventory and Market 
This section evaluates the characteristics of Wellington’s housing stock and the extent to which it is positioned to 
accommodate current and future housing demand. The findings indicate that Wellington’s housing inventory is dominated 
by single-family detached homes, providing relatively limited variety in housing types, while exceptionally low 
vacancy rates suggest that available housing supply is constrained. These conditions can restrict housing choice, 
increase competition for available units, and make it more difficult for households with different needs, preferences, and 
budgets to find suitable housing within the community. 
Housing Stock 
Wellington’s housing stock is predominantly composed of single-family homes, with detached units accounting for the vast 
majority of both owner- and renter-occupied housing (see Table 4). Of the estimated 4,469 total housing units, 
approximately 86% are single-family detached homes, while another 7% are single-family attached units. Multifamily 
housing makes up a relatively small share of the overall stock, including 3% in structures with two to four units, 2% in 
buildings with five to nine units, and just 1% in buildings with 10 or more units. The composition varies somewhat by

Jobs-Housing Balance 
Wellington’s jobs-housing balance suggests that the community functions primarily as a residential community rather than

Accessible/Visitable and Supportive Housing Needs 
Notable demand for accessible and visitable housing exists in Wellington. Accessible housing is designed for residents 
with mobility limitations, while visitable housing incorporates basic features that allow people with mobility impairments to  
enter a home and access essential living spaces. The analysis identifies a need for approximately 280 accessible or 
visitable units, equivalent to 6% of Wellington's housing stock (see Table 6). Typical features include step-free 
entries, wider doorways, and a usable main-floor bathroom. This estimate represents the number of units that would 
ideally be available in a balanced housing market, accounting for shared living arrangements and a modest market 
availability factor. The findings underscore the importance of expanding housing options that support aging in place and 
changing mobility needs over time. 
Wellington's estimated need for supportive housing is comparatively small but still meaningful. Supportive housing 
provides long-term, service-enriched housing for individuals experiencing homelessness or housing instability who require 
ongoing assistance to maintain stable housing. The analysis identifies a need for three supportive housing units, 
representing 0.1% of Wellington's housing stock. Because homelessness data are only available at the broader Fort 
Collins, Greeley, Loveland/Larimer, and Weld Counties Continuum of Care level, Wellington's share was estimated based 
on its share of households earning less than $25,000 annually, reflecting the strong relationship between extremely low 
incomes and homelessness risk. Consistent with homelessness research, the estimate assumes that roughly 30% of 
individuals experiencing homelessness require intensive, long-term supportive services to remain stably housed.3 While 
modest in scale, the findings indicate a persistent need for service-enriched housing even in a community where 
homelessness is relatively uncommon and often less visible.

overcrowded household is assumed to generate demand for 0.5 additional housing units. Second, the assessment

rental housing, rates generally associated with a healthy and functioning housing market. Combining these two factors 
indicates that Wellington currently faces a need for approximately 149 additional housing units before 
considering workforce-related demand.

Wellington were able to live within the community. Because future residential preferences and commuting patterns are
