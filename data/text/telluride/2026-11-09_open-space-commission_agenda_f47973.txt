Subject: Valley Floor Goats Pilot Program
Dear Open Space Commission Members and Town Council,
I have reviewed the agendas and minutes from the two most recent Open Space Commission 
meetings and couldn’t find any discussion of the Valley Floor goat grazing pilot, so I am writing 
to request clarification regarding both the results of the program and its future.
Specifically, I would like to know what data were collected during the 2025 and 2026 grazing 
periods, what criteria were established to measure the efficacy of the pilot, whether those 
criteria were met, and what decisions remain to be made regarding future use of goats for 
weed management on the Valley Floor.
Since the inception of this pilot, we have submitted observations, photographs, and video 
documenting our concerns. We have also repeatedly questioned whether goat grazing is an 
appropriate management tool for this particular conservation property. Our concerns include 
its absurd cost, its disturbance to native wildlife especially the elk at calving season, 
consumption and trampling of non-target vegetation, the presence of herding dogs on a 
property where dogs have historically and controversially been prohibited to protect wildlife, 
and, most importantly, whether the program actually provides meaningful control of the 
target invasive species.
Those concerns have not diminished. They have increased.
Attached is video I took on September 11, 2026, at the goat enclosure site taken right by the 
metal fence post where Josh shot his video on Aug 13 immediately following the goats 
removal. The Sept 11th footage shows abundant, vigorous thistles within the treated area,
Page 2 of 8
many flowering and approaching seed production before the first frost. This follows our earlier
documentation of thistles re-emerging in the same treatment footprint after the 2025 grazing.
Visual observation alone cannot entirely establish whether thistle abundance has statistically
increased or decreased, but after all the years working on the valley floor, this batch of thistles
is as thick as ever. After two seasons of treatment, there should now be objective info with
which to evaluate this pilot.
I would like the below bullet points to be addressed:
1)The pre- and post-grazing vegetation monitoring data from the 2025 and 2026 treatment
areas, including measurements of Canada thistle, musk thistle, yellow toadflax, native plant
cover, and bare ground, if collected.
2)The specific success criteria established for the pilot and an assessment of whether those
criteria were met.
3)The total cost of the program for each year, including the goat contractor, transportation,
fencing, shepherd/herding operations, staff time, monitoring, and any other associated
expenses.
4)Any comparison the Town has conducted between goat grazing and alternative methods,
including appropriately timed manual cutting or removal, paid weed crews, volunteer
removal, or other integrated weed-management approaches.
5) Assessment of wildlife disturbance associated with the goats, shepherd, fencing, and
herding dogs, particularly in relation to the Valley Floor's long-standing prohibition on dogs
and the property's importance to the elk herd. (We sent video evidence of this disturbance in
August)
6)The staff and/or Commission recommendation regarding whether goat grazing will continue
in 2027 and beyond, and when that decision will be considered publicly.
The Valley Floor was protected at extraordinary effort and expense because this community
valued its wildlife habitat, wetlands, native vegetation, open space, and ecological integrity. Its
management should reflect those conservation priorities and the "forever wild" principles that
were fundamental to the effort to protect it.
Once a pilot has been conducted for two seasons, continued expenditure of public funds
should depend upon demonstrated results—not simply a continuation of the experiment.
Based upon what we have observed and documented on the ground, we remain strongly
opposed to continuing the goat grazing program. The September 11 footage reinforces our
concern that the program has not produced meaningful control of the target thistles. If the
Town's quantitative monitoring demonstrates otherwise, I would very much like to see those
results.
If it does not, I respectfully urge the Open Space Commission and Town Council to end the
goat grazing program and redirect those funds toward targeted weed-control methods whose
results can be directly measured. Amy Cannon has provided meaningful suggestions and
contact information to work with local botanist in the area (found in her email addressed to
the OSC this summer). We would greatly appreciate a substantive response to these questions
and information about when the future of this program will next appear on a public agenda.

VALLEY FLOOR ANNUAL WINTER USE OPERATIONAL PLAN 
2026/2027 
 
TO: Telluride Open Space Commission 
FROM: Lance McDonald, Town of Telluride 
DATE: 11/09/26 
 
As required by the Valley Floor Open Space Management Plan, following is the proposed Annual 
Operational Plan for Winter Use on the Valley Floor for the 2026/2027 season. 
Grooming and Trail Maintenance Activities 
a. Perform grooming for the Nordic skiing and the walking/snowshoe/biking trails with Snowcat, 
tracked UTV & ATV, and Snowmobiles with associated implements. 
 
b. Perform daily (conditions dependent) snowcat grooming services on the Valley Floor (including USFS 
land under a Special Use Permit (SUP), and limited grooming  of Boomerang Rd. in Mountain Village 
(MV) (if SUP is provided by MV). The snowcat will also maintain a groomed emergency helicopter 
landing pad south of the Shell gas station and west of Boomerang Rd., if requested. 
 
c. Install and remove the temporary bridges consistent with the Plan. 
 
d. Implement annual maps, and signage for ST-1 remediation work as necessary. 
 
e. Continue to develop narrow trail grooming techniques to improve walking/snowshoe/snowbiking 
opportunities. 
 
f. Maintain Town of Telluride Nordic Pulse account for daily grooming reports and status updates 
regarding grooming activities (with link to Telluride Nordic Association). 
 
g. Utilize trail maps at trailheads and Town website. Install trail marking stakes and updated route 
identity signage (bi-lingual where applicable). 
 
h. Assist Sheep Mountain Alliance or other Town designee with conducting the Annual Progressive 
Dinner in January. 
 
i. Schedule Annual San Miguel Bike Alliance (SMBA) Fat Bike Nordic Relay for a 2 -hour race between 
December 15th, 202 6 and March 19th, 202 7 (conditions permitting). The race is limited to 50 
participants. SMBA shall be responsible for providing to the Town’s satisfaction demonstration of its 
nonprofit status; adequate insurance; and management of the event including temporary signage, 
providing safety personnel, porta-pots or other sanitation facilities, and providing cleanup crew. Any 
fee collected for the event shall be used only for documentable costs associated with coordinating 
and management of the event, with any remaining fees to be donated to the Town of Telluride Open 
Space Fund. 
Expenses associated with the activities listed above would be paid from the 2026 and 2027 Open Space 
Set Aside and Administration and Maintenance Funds.

Season 
The planned schedule for the upcoming season would be from early  December 2026 to early April 2027, 
conditions permitting. Grooming will occur generally after a snow depth of one foot is present. 
 
Designated Access 
The trailhead access will continue at the north and south ends of Pearl Property, the Boomerang Rd. gate 
(east of the Shell station), and near the underpass at the west end of the Valley Floor. Continue to improve 
access and signage at the south end of the Pearl Property and ensure snow storage from Mahoney Dr. 
does not impede access for winter users from the sidewalk on the west side of Mahoney Dr. by the Longwill 
Residences. Ensure appropriate location for west trailhead relative to the highway underpass at Lawson 
Hill. 
 
Specific Routes 
The general alignments of the proposed Nordic skiing and walking/snowshoe/biking routes are indicated 
in the attached Exhibit A, and which includes a route generally following the abandoned riverbed in the 
ST-1 Tailings Remediation/River Restoration Project area. 
The walking/snowshoe/biking trail routes may differ slightly from those indicated on the attached map as 
the Town will continue to work with trail users to improve the recreational experience of these trails. 
Hours of Grooming Activity/Grooming Equipment 
All grooming activities will be limited to one hour prior to dawn to one hour after dusk, unless emergency 
dictates otherwise. Nordic ski grooming will be performed by snowcats, tracked UTV & ATV, and 
snowmobiles with associated implements, with most walking/snowshoe/biking trails and “classic only” 
trails maintained by snowmobile or grizzlies with implements. All equipment shall be maintained in good 
working order. Any spilling or leaking of fuel or hydraulic fluids will be reported immediately and cleaned 
up appropriately as soon as practicable. 
Bridge Installation/Removal 
Temporary bridges will be located at crossings of the San Miguel River and other minor tributaries per the 
attached plan. Walking/snowshoe/biking and Nordic ski routes may coincide at bridge locations. Bridges 
are to be installed prior to the grooming season, or with the use of the snowcat after the timing conditions 
listed above have occurred. 
Bridge materials will be removed after the winter season at a time when ground conditions are favorable 
for minimal disturbance. During the summer season, bridge materials will be stored on -site in locations 
shielded from public view, including the river, or removed and stored at the Tilman -Beam Corral for the 
summer season. 
 
Signage 
Town will ensure the various routes are properly marked for segregation of uses. Prohibitive signage (i.e. 
no walking/snowshoe/biking on groomed Nordic ski routes) is authorized pursuant to the Trails Plan. 
Approach is to maintain low-profile signage with color-coded signage identifying trail numbers and uses, 
and to ensure dog permitted/prohibited routes are clearly marked.

Dear TOSC members,  
As your liaison on native engagement, I am continuing to build relationships, learn how to sensitively and 
appropriately engage, listen, and respect our partner’s timelines, which may be different from ours.  
In August, Amy and I met with Ernest House Junior on the Valley Floor to talk about updates to the signage and 
other ways to engage.  
From his bio: Ernest House Jr. is an enrolled member of the Ute Mountain Ute Tribe in Towaoc, Colorado, and a 
leader in Native American affairs. He is the Director for the Center for Tribal and Indigenous Engagement at the 
Keystone Policy Center in Denver. 
Previously, Ernest looked at the Bear Creek kiosk and had some suggestions about incorporating Ute language 
and swapping the contemporary photo for a Mountain Ute image.  I’m working on both of those projects and 
hope to have more suggestions for you in the coming months.  
While we were all on the Valley Floor, I posed the question to Ernest as to whether we should do an 
acknowledgment on the Valley Floor. He said YES, but only if we are prepared to go beyond just the words and 
make efforts to make native people feel welcome here. He suggested incorporating Ute place names in open 
space information, inviting elders to come and tell stories, and identify (but not publicize) places in the valley 
where ceremonies, hunting and plant gathering historically took place.   
I am following up on introductions he made to cultural staff at the Southern Ute and Mountain Ute who might 
help us with place names in Ute language that can be incorporated in signage in Bear Creek and the Valley 
Floor. It has been suggested that we include Navajo and Pueblo people in our acknowledgement language.  
I attended the Traditional Harvest Project in Cortez a few weeks ago, a project of the Montezuma Land Trust 
and the Ute Mountain Ute tribe. Montezuma Land Trust is a leader in native engagement and on private 
conservation land. I learned that the SW Conservation Corps has an Indigenous Work Crew that we could 
specifically request. Perhaps they could help us create a keystone species garden with cottonwood, willow, 
sumac, chokecherry and other native species that might flourish on the Valley Floor. I met Robyn Paulekas 
from Keep it Colorado who mentioned that she has worked with Chris Hazen.  
I also learned about Native cultural easements. Also known as cultural respect easements, cultural access 
easements, or cultural conservation easements, these are voluntary, permanent legal agreements that grant 
Indigenous tribes and native organizations rights to access, manage, or use ancestral lands without 
transferring full fee-simple property ownership. Relating to that, I introduced Mike Bordogna to Ernest to talk 
about giving access to tribal members for an outdoor classroom, a place to practice and ceremonies like a 
Bear Dance on the County’s Diamond Ridge land.  
I am looking forward to hearing your feedback on all of this, and hearing if there is interest in (slowly) pursuing 
Native partnerships and access, reciprocity, volunteerism, land-based learning opportunities, intergenerational 
knowledge sharing on Telluride’s open space.  
Thank you! 
Nancy Craft
