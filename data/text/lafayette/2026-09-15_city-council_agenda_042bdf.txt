My son attends Douglass Elementary, and I’m asking Lafayette City Council to please stand with the families
and community members fighting to keep Douglass open. Douglass is not just a building or a line item in a
district plan. It is a neighborhood school serving Lafayette and Louisville families, and it is also home to
programs and supports that some of our most vulnerable students rely on. My son is one of those students.
Before coming to Douglass, school was incredibly hard for him. I was getting calls about him running out of
the building, struggling to regulate, and even biting and hitting when he became overwhelmed. When he
came to Douglass and entered the ICAN program, the change was immediate. He stopped running out of
school. He stopped biting. He still has hard days, but he finally has a school where he feels safe, understood,
and able to learn. That kind of success does not happen accidentally. It comes from the relationships, staff,
structure, and community that have been built around children like mine. Closing a school disrupts much more
than enrollment boundaries. It disrupts relationships between children and trusted adults, neighborhood
connections, specialized supports, and the stability families have worked incredibly hard to build. For children
with disabilities, that disruption can have an especially significant impact. I am asking Lafayette City Council to
do more than acknowledge our concerns. Please contact BVSD Board of Education members directly and ask
them to vote no on closing Douglass Elementary. Please advocate for a process that protects neighborhood
schools, meaningfully considers students with disabilities before decisions are made, and recognizes that
schools are essential pieces of community infrastructure. So many of our children at Douglass are from
Lafayette. These are your families, your children, and your community too. Please use your voice to help
protect theirs.

I’m reaching out as a Lafayette resident and Douglass Elementary parent with concerns about BVSD’s
Resilient Schools proposal and the impact closing Douglass could have on Lafayette families. Douglass is

community lives in Lafayette, including many families who actively open-enroll at Douglass instead of

outside BVSD altogether. Douglass also provides something particularly valuable: a thriving, high-performing
school community with a strong commitment to serving students with special education needs. Rather than
viewing Douglass simply through the size of its resident attendance area, I hope our community leaders will
recognize the role it is already playing for Lafayette families. I know City Council does not make BVSD’s
school decisions. But this proposal has implications for Lafayette’s families, neighborhoods and ability to
remain a community where families want to put down roots. I’m asking Council members to take a closer look
at the proposal and raise a simple question with BVSD: Why was a school serving so many Lafayette families
excluded from the broader conversation about Lafayette’s future school needs? Thank you for representing
our community and for paying attention to how this decision could affect Lafayette families. Jennifer Lafayette
Resident & Douglass Elementary Parent

obligations under this Agreement shall terminate this Agreement at such time as then existing appropriations

RLUIPA & Relevant Federal Case Law I raise the following not as an accusation but because it is the
standard against which the City's treatment of these parcels will be measured, and because the controlling
legal precedent arose in this county. Equal terms. The Religious Land Use and Institutionalized Persons Act
(RLUIPA) provides that “no government shall impose or implement a land use regulation in a manner that
treats a religious assembly or institution on less than equal terms with a nonreligious assembly or institution.”
42 U.S.C. § 2000cc(b)(1). The provision requires no showing of burden and no proof of discriminatory intent; it
asks only whether religious assembly is treated less favorably than comparable secular assembly. In AMX-2
as adopted tonight, a museum, a government facility, a public recreational facility, and a hospital of any size
are permitted by right. A religious or secular assembly building of 7,500 square feet or more requires Special
Use Review before the Planning Commission and Council. In NC, assembly is permitted by right alongside
those same civic uses. The disparity is on the face of Table 26-3-1. Unreasonable limitations. RLUIPA also
provides that no government shall impose a land use regulation that “totally excludes religious assemblies
from a jurisdiction” or “unreasonably limits religious assemblies, institutions, or structures within a jurisdiction.”
42 U.S.C. § 2000cc(b)(3). The base row for “Assembly, Religious or Secular” in Table 26-3-1 remains blank in
all 21 districts, and the table's key provides that a blank cell means prohibited. Read literally, an assembly
space under 7,500 square feet not located on a Main Street is prohibited everywhere in Lafayette. I do not
believe that is intended. But Section 6 of the Ordinance bars staff from correcting it administratively, because
supplying a use-table entry changes a permitted or prohibited use, so only Council can fix it. Rocky Mountain
Christian Church v. Board of County Commissioners of Boulder County, 613 F.3d 1229 (10th Cir. 2010), cert.
denied (2011). A jury found that Boulder County violated RLUIPA's equal terms, unreasonable limitations, and
substantial burden provisions when it ran a church's expansion through its special use process and denied it
while a comparable secular institution had been approved; the Tenth Circuit affirmed a permanent injunction
ordering the permit issued. The facts bear directly on tonight's 2nd Reading: The court described the county's
Comprehensive Plan as “an advisory land use scheme” implemented through the Land Use Code, and
“accordance with the comprehensive plan” entered the case only as one subjective criterion within the code's
discretionary review. The plan is advisory, but the code operative. This is the same architecture as Lafayette's.
The violation was found in how staff processed the application, not only in the outcome. The court cited
evidence that the county “applied a less advantageous method to calculate whether the Church's proposed
use was over-intensive, and treated the Church's application as a new application, even though it was an
existing use,” and held that “rules which are discriminatorily applied are subject to strict scrutiny, not rational
basis review.” The elected body owned the outcome. The county commissioners had twice approved the
church's prior applications “over the opposition of the County land use office” and “against the land use staff's
recommendation.” The third time they deferred to staff, and the county, not its staff, was the defendant. The
jury heard that another congregation “ran out of money going through the County's special use application
process and abandoned its building project.” That is the practical effect of a Special Use designation on a
congregation without a land-use attorney on retainer, stated as trial evidence. The district court enjoined
approval of the entire application rather than pieces of it, reasoning that requiring the church to proceed in
piecemeal fashion “would impose a different burden on the RMCC, and likely would cause the RMCC to be
treated differently than similar secular institutions.” Applied here: Staff’s “Option 2” narrows the disparity but
does not close it. A 16,000-square-foot museum would still build by right while a 16,000-square-foot sanctuary
would go to a hearing. The remedy that satisfies the equal-terms standard is to treat assembly the way the
table already treats comparable civic uses, which is what NC does today and what the AMX row should do if
any parcel stays AMX-2. I would much rather resolve this as a planning question than as anything else, and
despite how the minutes portray it, that’s what Council already did at the August 18th meeting. 5. Direction
