Date Submitted: 8/16/2026
As a member of the land use code working group, there are three areas I’d like to comment on regarding the
draft LUC. But first I want to make an observation. Both the Old Town overlay district residential zoning and
the LURA and DDA district boundaries recognize the stretch of Public between Baseline and Spaulding, and
E. Simpson as one entity. The original OTX 1 zoning that the working group saw at our last meeting in March
also recognized this as one entity. Along with Baseline, this is the historic core of downtown. Each block along
these streets is different because that’s how Old Town has evolved, but together they represent downtown as
a whole. But staff has chosen to break our historic downtown area into three zones. We all understand why
Baseline has its own zoning, OTX 2. But using staff’s own reasoning regarding building types, sizes and retail
walkability, E. Simpson should also be its own zone (this is how the DDA map cites it). However, for reasons
that haven’t been revealed to the working group, staff has decided to group Public Road from Baseline to
Emma and E. Simpson together into OTX 1—and highlight the differences between the Baseline to Emma
and Emma to Kimbark sections on Public Road by separating them into different zones (OTX 1 and OTX 3). I
have questions and concerns about the reasons staff has given to planning commission and council about the
need for an OTX 3 zone, as I outline below. BUILDING SIZES AND WALKABILITY IN OTX 1 VS. OTX 3 I was
shocked to read this in your packet: OTX1 promotes street-oriented, smaller-scaled buildings with ground-
floor commercial uses along Public Road (North of Emma Street) and East Simpson Street. OTX 3 (transition)
manages the shift from the walkable retail core to larger building forms along Public Road south of Emma
Street. I believe this is inaccurate in several ways: *OTX 3 has as much or more ground-floor commercial
percentage of total lots as OTX 1. All of OTX 3 is commercial except for the historic motel turned affordable
housing at 503 S. Public, the post office and the new pocket park. OTX 1 has its own historic motel turned
affordable housing, two residential ADUs, a house used as a residence, the Starkey building and Festival
Plaza on Public Road. And on E. Simpson, there’s a church, the senior center, the Lafayette history museum,
the Arapaho Center, an apartment building, 18 single family houses and 18 townhomes, *OTX 3 is a “walkable
retail core” just like OTX 1. In fact, OTX 3 is a much more walkable retail area than the E. Simpson section of
OTX 1, where the retail is in separate islands surrounded by housing (I think E. Simpson is a good example of
neighborhood commercial). There are sidewalks along all of Public Road in both OTX 1 and 3. All of the
Public Road sections of OTX 1 and 3 have art on the street sculptures. The pocket park at 701 S. Public in
OTX 3 is being built to encourage people to walk there and hang out, just like they do at Festival Plaza in OTX
1. LURA has earmarked a large sum of money to make the streetscape of the completely retail 700 block
(OTX 3) more attractive and walkable. I think saying that OTX 3 is a transition between walkable OTX 1 and
the supposedly? unwalkable NC zoning to the south is in opposition to the revitalization efforts currently going
on in OTX 3. *OTX 1 does not have smaller-scaled buildings than OTX 3. I believe Bonnie Freeman sent you
her GIS data of every building size in OTX 1, 2 and 3? If you look through that, you can see the largest
buildings in each zone. I’ve listed the ones over 8,000 square feet in OTX 1 and 3 below: *600 S. Public
(Lafayette Florist buildings) Shop and office: 6,734 SF; greenhouse SF unknown. Tebo ad says total rentable
SF is 64,380 (OTX 3) *East Simpson townhomes: Three buildings: 12,750 SF; 13,566 SF; 14,114 SF. Total:
40,430 SF (OTX 1) *400 E. Simpson St. (Lafayette Commons office building): 19,708 SF (OTX 1) *407 E.
Simpson St. (Sunburst Apartments): 13,052 SF (OTX 1) *211 N. Public (Remy’s, salon): 12,726 SF (OTX 1)
*605 S. Public (Post office): main office: 5,912 SF; offices and warehouse: 6,370 SF: Total: 12,282 SF (OTX
3) *300 S. Public (Tangerine): 11,426 SF (OTX 1) *409 S. Public (Noble Treasures, Bare Boulder): 9,445 SF
(OTX 1) *105 W. Emma (The Post): 9,241 SF (OTX 1) *501 E. Simpson (Textile Network): 8,620 SF (OTX 1)
*503 S. Public (historic motel turned affordable housing): 8,401 SF (OTX 3) LOT SIZES IN OTX 1 AND 3 I’d
also like to address staff comments during your workshop and the PC public hearings that there are bigger
lots in OTX 3 than OTX 1, and that’s why OTX 3 zoning is needed. Here are the largest lots on Public
between Baseline and Spaulding and their proposed zones, according to Boulder County Property records
searches. *600 S. Public (Lafayette Florist): 104,218 SF (OTX 3) *503 S. Public (El Mercado/historic motel
turned affordable housing): 60,130 SF (OTX 3) *605 S. Public (post office): 39,980 SF (OTX 3) *607 S. Public
(old Chevy dealership turned office building): 26,882 SF (OTX 3) *200 N. Public (Chase bank): 21,581 SF
(OTX 1) *211 N. Public (Remy’s, salon): 20,019 SF (OTX 1) *709 S. Public (Deluxe Liquors, Rocky Mountain
Legal): 18,577 SF (OTX 3) *502 S. Public (Senor Gomez): 15,128 SF (OTX 3) *210 N. Public (vacant lot at
Baseline and Public): 14,917 SF (OTX 1) *103 N. Public (Teocalli, Stams, violin shop): 14,741 SF (OTX 1)
*300 S. Public (Tangerine): 14,244 SF (OTX 1) *101 E. Cleveland (Efrains): 14,176 SF (OTX 1) *100 S. Public
(Public Wine, insurance, budget glass): 14,080 SF (OTX 1) *705 S. Public (ice cream shop double lot): 12,891
SF (OTX 3) *500 S. Public (mini mart): 12,792 SF (OTX 3) In general, lot sizes are comparable in the Public
Road sections of OTX 1 and 3 except for the Big Three: Lafayette Florist, El Mercado/affordable housing and
the post office. It seems obvious the OTX 3 zoning is meant for them because none of the other lots could
accommodate a building(s) with the 25,000 SF footprint/90% lot coverage allowed in OTX 3. On the other
hand, all of the lots in OTX 3 except 707 S. Public, where the new bakery is being built, and 701 S. Public,
home of the new pocket park, are large enough to accommodate the 10K SF footprint/80% lot coverage
allowed in OTX 1. Another point: All three of these big lots are in the block between Emma and Kimbark.
During the July 8 PC public hearing, planning staff handed out a map (attached) showing their proposal to
make only this block OTX 3, with OTX 1 abutting it on the west side and OTX 1 and MHC next to it on the
east side. I’m wondering how is this not spot zoning, which is potentially illegal? ALLEY ACCESS Finally, I’d
like to disc ss alle access since staff told PC that’s something that disting ishes OTX 1 from OTX 3 Here’s
like to discuss alley access, since staff told PC that’s something that distinguishes OTX 1 from OTX 3. Here’s
the alley access status of the three blocks of OTX 3, along with side street access: East side of Public
between Emma and Kimbark: *El Mercado/old motel turned affordable housing: no alley or side street access
*Post office: no alley access; curb cut access from Kimbark and Roosevelt *Old Chevy dealership turned
offices: curb cut access from Kimbark East side of Public between Kimbark and Spaulding: *All of the lots
have alley access West side of Public between Emma and Kimbark: *Mini mart: no alley access; curb cut
access from Emma *Senor Gomez: has alley access *Lafayette Florist: has alley access If the goal is to take
out curb cuts on Public in OTX 3 and only access parking from behind the buildings or the side streets, it
appears that can be done for all of the lots except the El Mercado/motel lot. I’m assuming the post office
would need to keep its Public Road curb cut due to its function. Another point I think is key: Staff has said they
want to encourage access to lots/parking on Public through the alleys in order to make the street more
walkable and safe. But Old Town alleys aren't meant to be used by multiple cars. They're one lane and have
sections that aren't paved or regularly maintained, making it unsafe for multiple cars to access parking lots
from the alleys. I think it’s a nice idea to eliminate curb cuts on Public, but the street/alley infrastructure isn’t
designed for that. Thanks, as always, for considering my comments.

to provide for a mixed-residential neighborhood of homes for I/DD, missing middle and market residents.
None requires delaying adoption tonight. Each can be handled by staff direction or a follow-up amendment. 1.
Two tables in the code conflict about where smaller single-family homes are allowed. One table says
detached houses and duplexes are allowed only in the core of a new neighborhood; another table provides lot
standards for those same homes in the edge areas along major streets. Since the code requires those edge
areas to make up a third or more of every large new neighborhood, this conflict decides whether modest
single-family homes, a housing type this community has said it wants, can be built there at all. Please direct
staff to confirm that smaller detached homes and duplexes are allowed in both areas. (Tables 26-2-22 and 26-
2-23.) 2. Let variety happen naturally instead of by formula. The code currently tells a builder how many
housing types a neighborhood must have, what minimum percentage each type must be, and how the site
must be divided up. Stacked together, these formulas end up designing the neighborhood before anyone
looks at the land or the market. A simpler rule accomplishes the same goal: no single housing type may
dominate. That guarantees variety while leaving room for good design. (Table 26-2-21.) 3. Treat homes for
people with disabilities like the homes they are. The draft rightly allows group homes for people with
disabilities in new neighborhoods without special permission, and that is to the City's credit. But in several
other districts, and for continuing care facilities even within new neighborhoods, the code requires a special
discretionary review that ordinary homes do not face. Colorado law says that group homes are a residential
use in every residential zone, and federal fair housing law says the same in its own way. The fix is simple:
wherever homes are allowed, these homes are allowed. This also serves the code's own stated purpose of
housing for people of all ages, abilities, and incomes. (Table 26-3-1; C.R.S. 31-23-303.) 4. Tell property
owners which rules apply to projects already underway. The code has rules for applications pending when it
takes effect, but it does not address annexations that are in process on that date, and the effective date itself
is still blank. Anyone mid-process deserves a clear answer. The fairest rule is to let those applicants choose
which code applies. (Section 26-1-6.) 5. Once Council has decided the big questions, let staff handle the
details. Under the draft, a single new neighborhood can require six or more separate applications and roughly
ten public hearings. Council's policy judgment is fully exercised when it approves the annexation, the zoning,
and the master plan for the site (the "regulating plan"). After that point, the remaining steps are technical
compliance, which staff and the Planning Commission are equipped to decide. Combining the early steps and
delegating the later ones would save applicants and the City a year or more per project without removing a
single policy decision from this Council. Relatedly, because approvals will take years to earn, the code should
let a phased project lock in its approvals for longer than the standard three years through a development
agreement, as state law allows. (Table 26-8-1; C.R.S. 24-68-104.) 6. Adopt tonight, and schedule the cleanup.

system is deferred to future work. That is normal for a document this size. The remedy is a commitment
tonight to a cleanup-and-refinement ordinance on a stated schedule, so that applicants and staff are working
from a reliable code on day one. Thank you for your consideration and for the considerable work reflected in
this draft. My team and I remain available to staff on any of these items. Respectfully, Jack Bestall, Principal

Executive Summary 
Lafayette residents volunteer to serve on a variety of boards, commissions, and committees 
(“BCC”) to advise City Council on matters germane to each BCC’s scope. City Council 
established the Human Rights Board (“HRB”) in 2016 and, following nearly a decade of work 
using the original scope, supported a scope assessment to address high membership 
turnover and ensure alignment with future needs of the organization and community. Staff 
and the consultant team facilitated a scope assessment process as part of the Inclusion, 
Diversity, Equity, and Access (“IDEA”) Strategic Plan with significant input and refinement 
from the Human Rights Board.  
Updates to the scope have been presented to and supported by the HRB, and City Council 
indicated support of the amendment at the July 28, 2026, workshop. Approval of this 
ordinance would codify the updated scope and allow staff to recruit members using the new 
board scope and name during the fall 2026 recruitment scheduled to being August 24, 
2026.  
City Council unanimously approved this ordinance on first reading at its August 4, 2026, 
meeting.  

2 
 
City Council Strategic Outcome (most applicable): Affordable, Attainable, and 
Just 
Background Information 
Due to years of difficulty implementing the current scope of the Human Rights Board within 
the advisory board structure and near-constant membership turnover, the board’s scope 
and name were assessed during the IDEA Strategic Plan development process at the 
request of HRB. Following a series of engagement opportunities and analysis over a course 
of approximately 18 months, the IDEA Strategic Plan included a recommendation to change 
the name of the Human Rights Board to the “Inclusion, Diversity, Equity, and Access Board” 
with the following revised scope:  
The inclusion, diversity, equity, and access board shall advise the city council by providing 
recommendations related to:  
(a) City council policies that promote inclusion, diversity, equity, and access for 
Lafayette residents;  
(b) City council initiatives and activities intended to improve inclusivity, diversity, equity, 
and accessibility across municipal services and programs; and 
(c) Community outreach practices, with a focus on removing participation barriers and 
improving resident engagement in city council decision-making. 
Staff has presented this scope to the Human Rights Board for feedback and refinement at 
multiple meetings. At its July 20, 2026, meeting, the HRB approved a motion to advance a 
recommendation to City Council to update the board’s name and scope as presented in this 
meeting packet. City Council also indicated support for this amendment during the July 28, 
2026, workshop.  
Note that since the HRB meeting and City Council workshop, the City Attorney’s Office 
incorporated a minor drafting amendment to align the scope with the City’s advisory board 
structure as outlined in the Lafayette Home Rule Charter. As a board tasked with advising 
the City Council, the scope language above and in the attached ordinance was updated to 
reflect advising on City Council policies (subsection (a)), City Council initiatives (subsection 
(b)), and City Council decision-making (subsection (c)). Amendments to the language 
originally presented during the July 20, 2026, HRB meeting and July 28, 2026, workshop 
are shown as underlined text, above.

Next Steps 
If approved on second reading, staff would recruit for upcoming vacancies on the board 
using the new name and scope as part of the fall 2026 recruitment, scheduled to begin 
Monday, August 24.  
Recommendation 
Staff recommends approval of this ordinance on second reading based on board and City 
Council feedback.

Executive Summary 
The City received one request for its 2026 Private Activity Bond (“PAB”) allocation. Boulder 
County Housing Authority has requested the City’s allocation to be used for its Willoughby 
Corner Project. If approved, Resolution No. 2026-21 would facilitate this request.  
City Council Strategic Outcome (most applicable): Affordable, Attainable, and 
Just 
 
 

2 
 
Background Information 
Private Activity Bonds are tax-exempt bonds issued by or on behalf of local or state 
governments by providing special financing benefits for qualified projects. These bonds are 
used to attract private investment for projects that have specific public benefit. They also 
lower financing costs by exempting federal tax, making them an attractive tool for 
developments like affordable housing.  
Each year, the IRS sets a cap on how much PAB funding each state can issue. For 2026, 
Colorado’s total PAB capacity is $812 million. This total is split roughly in half between 
statewide authorities (e.g., Colorado Housing and Finance Authority or “CHFA”) and local 
governments, with allocations based on population size. Lafayette’s 2026 allocation is 
$2,081,317. 
The City must take action by September 15, 2026, or the allocation will expire. Potential 
actions include assigning the City’s volume cap to:  
• a specific project.  
• another local government or housing authority (recommended action).  
• CHFA to support affordable housing across Colorado.  
The City may also choose to relinquish or carry forward its volume cap.  
Previous allocation assignments have included:  
• Traditions at Sola (2019): Allocation assigned to CHFA to finance this affordable 
housing project for older adults. 
• CHFA (2020): Allocation assigned for unspecified uses.  
• Boulder County Housing Authority (2021 – 2024): Allocation used for Willoughby 
Corner affordable housing development. 
• Related Affordable (2025): Acquisition and rehabilitation of Helios Station 
Townhomes (north of the recreation center).

Next Steps 
If Resolution No. 2026-21 is approved, the City’s 2026 PAB allotment ($2,081,317) will be 
assigned to the Boulder County Housing Authority for its Willoughby Corner project. 
Recommendation 
Staff recommends the City assign its 2026 PAB allocation of $2,081,317 to the Boulder 
County Housing Authority for its Willoughby Corner project.
