Dear City Councilors, I'm writing in support of the proposed moratorium on data center development in
Lafayette. I want to start by saying that I work for an AI-enabled services provider. I understand the economic
and national security concerns around losing the "AI war" with China. And I understand that there are local
and state economic benefits to data center development. And yet — I am concerned and, frankly, disgusted
by the "ends justify the means" narrative around AI and data centers. From a global climate perspective, yes,
there are opportunities for AI to help further the clean economy. But that statement belies the environmental
costs of the AI and data center buildout. Experts at Goldman Sachs estimate that US data center power
demand is forecast to more than double to 66 GW in 2027 (from 31 GW in 2025). The share of total US peak
summer power demand from data centers is expected to jump to 8.5% in the same year. That's a huge
increase in demand on a grid that has seen relatively flat energy demand for the past several decades. As
recent announcements from Meta, Amazon, Microsoft, and other AI providers have shown, much of that
demand will be met with new natural gas generation. The Global Energy Monitor shows that US gas-fired
power capacity in development surged to 378 GW — more than half that capacity is planned exclusively to
support data centers. Lafayette's Climate Action Plan sets out ambitious climate goals, including increasing
the share of clean energy in our City's power supply. Data centers directly threaten that goal — the new
power generation required to support them could make our power grid dirtier, not cleaner. That's to say
nothing of the economic cost reflected in higher power bills or health costs to community members living near
planned data center and power-generation sites. Water is another major concern with data center
development. Lafayette is in a drought — we're currently in the process of passing our integrated water and
drought-preparedness plan, which is meant to cover the next seven years. That plan doesn't leave room for
the massive water needs of a data center. For all of these reasons and more, I support a moratorium on data
center development in the City. At the very least, I think the community deserves to understand how data
center development would affect our energy grid, water resources, and environment. We can't rush those
things because tech billionaires want to get richer — even while they admit the high likelihood that their
technologies will ultimately harm humanity.

I am writing to voice strong opposition to the use of Flock cameras within our city. These cameras, backed by
AI and a searchable database, amount to little more than a mass surveillance system of our city. This is not

become insignificant. I am emphatically not saying a victim is insignificant, and I do appreciate that there were
arrests which were undoubtedly assisted by this technology. But I am of the strong opinion that those wins are
not worth the mass intrusion into the lives of every person who passes by one of these cameras. There is an
every-increasing body of evidence around the abuse of the system by law enforcement from the local level
(not our police, I am speaking in generality) through the federal level. This article documents an investigation
by the EFF where police are bypassing the "oversight" of their use of the system by typing nonsense into the
single question in a form that is supposed to safeguard our civil liberties (https://www.msn.com/en-
us/news/other/us-cops-ignore-flock-camera-oversight-probing-nationwide-surveillance-network-with-bogus-
reasoning/ar-AA2csHsH?PC=EMMX01). In short, the Flock system is a violation of our civil rights. It is a mass
surveillance system being sold across the country as a tool for law enforcement without safeguards or
oversight to prevent its abuse. I strongly advocate for the city to both terminate its contract with Flock and, as
an interim safeguard, to cover the existing cameras to prevent the continued intrustion into the rights of our
citizens.

I write to ask that any and all ALPR budget items, including Flock, are scheduled as a standalone agenda
item, with public comment. Based on the current policies in place, I believe that the use of ALPRs is
dangerous, and until that changes, it is my opinion that Flock funding should be cut from the 2027 budget,
and I would like the community to weigh in on this important topic, of not just police spending, but our
community values around surveillance. Earlier this year, I attended the Lafayette Police Department's
presentation on Flock Safety and the city's use of ALPRs. That presentation was thorough and transparent.
The department walked through exactly how the technology works, step by step, and ran a live
demonstration. That kind of openness reflects well on this department, and increased my trust in the officers
serving this community. It also showed me how powerful the technology is. And the technology, as a crime
investigation tool, works. There is no arguing the power of Flock after seeing the presentation. It has been
applied to cases across a huge range, from large narcotics and theft operations down to locating a single
elderly resident who had wandered from home. That breadth matters. This is not a tool that only serves one
type of law enforcement need. It surfaces patterns, connects dots, and reaches into many corners of public
safety. But that same reach and power is exactly why this council needs to stop and think carefully. In a thirty-
day window, a Flock camera likely records my vehicle over a hundred times. That means law enforcement
has the ability, if they chose to use it, to map exactly where I go, when I go there, and how often. The same is
true for every resident in this city. That capability exists right now and the police department has added even
more cameras recently, including one near my house in front of the Walgreens. And this is not just about
trusting Lafayette's officers, who, again, appear to be using this responsibly. This system is part of a statewide
network. That means officers and detectives from other departments across Colorado may have access to
this data. And any outside agency, whether another department in this state or a federal agency, can contact
Lafayette directly and request access. Once a case enters the courts, that data may be legally required to be
handed over. Here is what that means in practice. Consider a federal agency that is operating under a
different set of priorities than Lafayette's. If that agency pursues a case and it reaches the courts, they may be
legally entitled to everything in our database. We did not hand it over voluntarily. The law required it. But here
is the harder truth: if we did not have Flock, that database would not exist. By collecting this data, we are
creating something that can be taken from us and used in ways we would never choose. We are, in a sense,
building the conditions for that potential abuse ourselves. Or consider a closer-to-home scenario. Another
Colorado police department is conducting an investigation that, back in their jurisdiction, is being carried out
with bias, targeting a particular neighborhood or ethnic group. That bias is invisible to us. All Lafayette sees is
a legitimate-looking criminal case with a connection to our city. Because it appears authorized, we share our
Flock data. But we have no visibility into the larger picture; how that investigation started, who it is really
targeting, or what it is actually being used to do. Our data, collected here with good intentions, becomes a tool
that extends someone else's bias far beyond their own jurisdiction. This is why oversight of what happens to
our data when it leaves Lafayette is not optional, but essential. A powerful law enforcement technology like
this does not automatically mean we must use it, or that we must accept it as it currently exists. Of course,
Flock and ALPRs increase our ability to dissuade criminal activity. So would cameras in everyone's home or
ankle bracelets on every resident. There are significant trade-offs to our civil liberties, and those trade-offs
deserve to be weighed honestly against the law enforcement benefits. So here is what I believe needs to be
decided before we continue with any sort of ALPR in our community. And what needs to be decided before we
continue spending any more city funds on Flock: First, a formal public review process for any changes to this
technology or this contract. Flock is a for-profit company. They will keep developing new capabilities and
offering them to law enforcement agencies. As we speak, they advertise AI-powered video cameras to identify
people, security drones, high powered audio detection, integration with AI-powered crime investigative
dragnet software. Right now, there is no clear process for residents to know when something new is being
offered, accepted, or rejected. One detective mentioned that the department has already declined some of
Flock's newer features, which is exactly the right instinct. But that decision should not live only inside the
police department. It should be visible and accountable to the public. Even the number of Flock cameras
should be public knowledge. I find it disturbing that the number of cameras have continued to increase this
year in Lafayette, despite the increasing public pushback and questioning of the technology. And the
continued exposure of Flock's data safety concerns also needs to be something that a resident could review
and challenge. Second, a demand for clarity on data retention, with the ability for residents to weigh in on it.
The current policy retains data for thirty days. If a resident wanted to push for a shorter window, or ask
questions about that policy, there is no clear path for doing that. That needs to change. I believe, if we do
have ALPRs, they should retain no more than 7 days. Third, create oversight of data sharing. When
Lafayette's Flock data is requested by another agency, there should be a clear, documented process for
evaluating that request; one that goes beyond whether it appears legally authorized. Who is asking, why, and
what is the broader context of their investigation? Lafayette should not be in a position of unknowingly
extending another agency's overreach simply because a request looked legitimate on the surface. There
should be publicly available data on who we are sharing this data with. This is not an argument against law
enforcement. It is an argument for making sure that as this technology grows more powerful, the public's
ability to understand and shape how it is used grows with it. Just because it is an "effective tool", as the police
department argued, doesn't mean we have to use it carte blanche or at all, if it does not align with our values
or if we see the dangers to our civil liberties as more negative than the benefits. I do not believe we are at a
place where the community has been able to see the scope of ALPR usage and has been able to delineate
key policies on how we collect, retain, and record the use and distribution of ALPR data. Therefore, I
recommend that it is separated into its own agenda item and that funding is cut for 2027 until we come up with
better controls for its use.

Public Input - Submission #62470
Date Submitted: 9/27/2026
I am writing to support language in the Land Use Code that allows for flexibility in the height of downtown
buildings in Lafayette. To allow for more residential housing, three story buildings, up to a maximum of 35 feet,
should be included. This would allow for housing above retail establishments. According to national research,
more residential housing in downtown areas would result in more foot traffic. Foot traffic is the life blood of
downtown businesses. Two research studies indicate the need for this housing to support a vibrant downtown.
The first is a National League of Cities article “Why Housing is Key to Main Street Success.” The article was
written by Corrianne Rice and dated January 21, 2025. The article states the following: “Housing revitalizes
main streets primarily through increased foot traffic. Residents living near commercial areas naturally frequent
local shops, restaurants and services. The convenience of amenities within walking distance encourages
residents to spend more time and money locally, which benefits local businesses and entrepreneurs.” “As foot
traffic increases, commercial property owners are more likely to invest in the upkeep and improvement of their
buildings, leading to higher property values and an improved overall aesthetic. This was the experience of
residents in Sylvania, Ohio, where local business owners credit the opening of a new apartment complex for
spurring their downtown resurgence.” “Sylvania’s neighbor, Toledo, Ohio, has seen an increase in downtown
housing in recent years. More than $2 billion has been invested in Downtown Toledo with an emphasis on
beautification, walkability, and of course, housing. Projects to revitalize historic buildings aim to preserve the
city’s heritage, while contributing toward its goal of adding 10,000 housing units over ten years. These efforts
have also attracted major investments from companies like Amazon, Stellantis and General Motors, boosting
the city’s economy.” “Mixed-use developments create integrated urban environments where residents enjoy
the convenience of nearby shops and services, and businesses thrive with a built-in customer base. While
tourism-dependent districts can struggle during downturns, communities with residential cores maintain
stability even during challenging times, which ultimately enhances the functionality and appeal of revitalized
main streets.” “When new construction is necessary, cities like Sault Ste. Marie, Michigan, demonstrate
sustainable approaches. The Osborn Commons, a 65-unit mixed-income development, exemplifies thoughtful
urban design. Located in the downtown area and within walking distance of essential services, the project
earned LEED Gold certification by prioritizing sustainable design and community connectivity.” “Compact,
mixed-use neighborhoods also reduce long commutes, easing traffic and lowering greenhouse gas emissions.
Walkable, bike-friendly neighborhoods not only promote sustainability but also enhance residents’ health and
quality of life.” The second article is the 2026 Global Trends Report, presented by Progressive Urban
Management Associates. They identify the top 10 Global Trends affecting downtown areas. One of the ten is
to view housing as infrastructure. The article goes on to say “housing has become core infrastructure for
downtown - and it remains underbuilt. Downtown housing carries a strong outlook. Office to residential
conversions is gaining momentum, with nearly 100,000 units in the national pipeline.” Please make sure the
Land Use Code includes the allowance of buildings three stories tall that could be up to 35 feet in height. As
quoted in the research examples, this will create the downtown housing and resultant foot traffic that is
needed to ensure the economic well-being of the downtown area. This should be what we all want for the City
of Lafayette. Thank you.
