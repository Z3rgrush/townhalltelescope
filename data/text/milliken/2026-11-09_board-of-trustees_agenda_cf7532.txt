CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION 
1.1 Purpose 
The Strategic Growth Element (SGE) is a required component of municipal comprehensive planning under Colorado 
Senate Bill 24-174 (SB24-174). It provides a framework for directing growth to locations that can efficiently use 
existing infrastructure and services while supporting housing, economic development, and long-term community 
goals. 
By promoting strategic growth patterns, the SGE helps identify opportunities for housing, business investment, and 
public improvements while considering infrastructure capacity, fiscal impacts, environmental resources, and 
quality of life.  
The Strategic Growth Element addresses three required components: 
1. Strategic Growth Policies and Tools 
2. Infill and Redevelopment 
3. Greenfield Development 
1.2 Strategic Growth in Milliken 
Milliken continues to experience residential growth within the rapidly evolving Northern Colorado region. Its location 
near Highway 60, Highway 257, Greeley, and Johnstown creates opportunities for housing, employment, business 
investment, and regional connectivity. At the same time, the community values its small-town character, 
agricultural heritage, established neighborhoods, local businesses, parks, and community facilities. 
Strategic growth in Milliken balances reinvestment within established areas with carefully planned expansion at the 
community’s edges. Infill and redevelopment opportunities may include rehabilitation, adaptive reuse, compatible 
housing, neighborhood-serving businesses, and commercial reinvestment. Longer-term greenfield opportunities 
may support residential, commercial, industrial, mixed-use, recreational, and infrastructure development where 
services can be provided efficiently. 
Future growth decisions should consider:  
• Infrastructure and utility capacity. 
• Water availability.  
• Transportation access and connectivity.  
• Public services and long-term maintenance obligations.  
• Housing and employment needs.  
• Commercial and industrial opportunities.  
• Agricultural and environmental resources.  
• Community character and quality of life.  
• Initial and long-term fiscal effects.  
The Strategic Growth Element provides a framework for evaluating these considerations together. It does not 
establish development rights, annexation priorities, or a required sequence of development. Rather, it supports 
informed decision-making as infrastructure, market conditions, ownership interests, and community priorities 
evolve.

2.2 Growth Framework and Development Patterns 
Planning Geography and Regional Relationships 
Milliken’s planning framework extends beyond the developed portions of Town. The municipal boundary, Urban 
Growth Area, and three-mile planning area provide distinct geographic boundaries for coordinating future land use, 
infrastructure, annexation, transportation, and public services.  
The Urban Growth Area identifies the broader area where future municipal growth may occur. While lands outside 
Town limits remain under Weld County jurisdiction until annexed, the Urban Growth Area helps establish a long-
term framework for evaluating growth opportunities, infrastructure investments, and service needs.  
Milliken's future growth is also influenced by neighboring communities, including Johnstown, Platteville, Greeley, 
Evans, and Gilcrest. These regional relationships affect annexation opportunities, transportation connections, 
utility planning, service areas, and coordinated growth patterns. Ongoing intergovernmental coordination will 
remain important as development and infrastructure projects move forward. 
Land Use and Growth Pattern 
Milliken’s land-use pattern reflects its historic core, established neighborhoods, newer residential areas, 
commercial and employment centers, public facilities, recreation assets, waterways, and surrounding agricultural 
lands.  
Downtown and the Broad Street corridor serve as the community’s civic and local commercial center, supporting 
local businesses, public facilities, services, and opportunities for investment and adaptive reuse.  
Residential neighborhoods extend west, east, and south of the historic core, with newer development concentrated 
east of the railroad. Schools, parks, recreation facilities, and community destinations are integrated throughout 
these areas, while the railroad, Highway 60, waterways, and incomplete street or pedestrian connections influence 
access and mobility.  
Commercial and employment activity is concentrated along Broad Street, Highway 60, and established business 
and industrial areas. These locations support local employment, business activity, and future opportunities to 
strengthen Milliken's sales-tax and employment base.  
Agricultural and rural lands continue to define much of the broader planning area, contributing to Milliken's 
character, economy, and identity while influencing where future growth and infrastructure investments may be 
practical.  
The Land Use and Growth Map illustrates these relationships and provides context for evaluating infill, 
redevelopment, and greenfield opportunities in later chapters.

2.3 Growth Drivers and Development Considerations 
Future growth in Fort Milliken will be influenced by a range of interconnected factors, including housing needs, 
employment opportunities, public facilities, infrastructure capacity, transportation access, water resources, and 
environmental conditions. Understanding these factors helps identify opportunities and constraints associated 
with future growth and provides important context for the redevelopment, infill, and greenfield analyses presented 
in chapters 4 and 5. 
Demographic and Housing Trends  
Milliken experienced significant growth between 2019 and 2024, with its population increasing by approximately 
23%. During this period, the community added housing units, became younger on average, and experienced 
increases in household incomes and home values. 
Milliken’s housing supply is dominated by owner-occupied, 
detached single-family homes. According to the 2024 American 
Community Survey (ACS), approximately 92% of occupied housing 
units were owner-occupied and nearly 89% of all housing units 
were detached single-family homes. Housing values increased 
48% between 2019 and 2024, with the estimated median home 
value of an owner-occupied home rising from $275,000 to 
$406,700.  
The Housing Needs Assessment (HNA) combines ACS data 
trends, market analysis, and community input to identify current 
and future housing needs. The HNA identified needs for a broader 
range of housing options, including ownership and rental 
opportunities, smaller and attached homes, senior and accessible 
housing, and housing affordable to a range of income levels while 
continuing to support family households. The Housing Needs 
Assessment provides an important foundation for evaluating both 
redevelopment and infill opportunities. 
Employment Trends 
Milliken’s labor force and commuting patterns reflect the community’s connection to the broader Northern 
Colorado economy. According to the 2024 American Community Survey, approximately 78% of residents age 16 
and older participated in the labor force.  
Residents work across a range of industries. Educational services, 
health care, and social assistance accounted for approximately 
21% of employed residents, while manufacturing represented 
approximately 19%. Professional and administrative services, retail 
trade, transportation and utilities, construction, and other 
industries also contribute to the employment profile.  
Downtown Milliken, Broad Street, local schools, public facilities, 
Highway 60 businesses, and established industrial areas provide 
employment and services. Future economic growth should be built 
on these assets by supporting local businesses, expanding 
employment opportunities, and strengthening the Town's sales-tax 
base. 
Downtown and Broad Street are well suited for small-scale 
commercial investment, adaptive reuse, civic activity, and neighborhood-serving businesses. Larger commercial,

Transportation and Mobility  
State Highways 60 and 257 provide Milliken's primary regional transportation connections. Highway 60 also 
functions as Broad Street through the community, linking neighborhoods with Downtown, businesses, schools, 
public facilities, and employment areas. Highway 257 provides north-south access to surrounding communities 
and regional destinations. 
Milliken's transportation network includes Town streets, 
county roads, sidewalks, trails, railroad crossings, and 
bicycle and pedestrian facilities. Established neighborhoods 
near Downtown generally have a more connected street 
pattern, while railroad tracks, highways, waterways, rural 
roadway sections, and gaps in pedestrian infrastructure can 
create barriers to connectivity in other areas.  
These conditions influence access to schools, parks, 
businesses, recreation facilities, and employment centers, 
while also affecting the feasibility of future development and 
infrastructure expansion. The Transportation Map provides 
additional context for evaluating transportation conditions 
throughout the planning area. 
Infrastructure, Utilities, and Water  
Infrastructure serviceability is a key consideration for both reinvestment and outward growth. Milliken provides or 
coordinates water, wastewater, stormwater, transportation, and other public services across established 
neighborhoods, newer subdivisions, commercial and employment centers.  
Existing utility systems are concentrated within developed areas. While vacant and underutilized properties near 
existing infrastructure may require limited improvements, proximity to utilities does not guarantee available 
capacity or service. Development in more distant locations may require system extensions, roadway 
improvements, drainage facilities, and substantial public and private investment.  
Water availability, raw-water requirements, infrastructure costs, and long-term system planning can also influence 
development feasibility. The Housing Needs Assessment identified water rights, raw-water dedication, tap fees, 
and infrastructure costs as factors affecting housing and other development opportunities. Staff also indicated that 
water capacity or the cost of providing service has affected development opportunities.  
Water availability, infrastructure capacity, serviceability will continue to influence redevelopment and greenfield 
growth opportunities. A more detailed evaluation of water resources, water conservation, system capacity, and 
infrastructure planning is provided in the Water Supply Element. 
The Utility and Infrastructure Map identifies the general location of existing water, sanitary sewer, and storm sewer 
infrastructure. It does not confirm available capacity, pressure, facility condition, connection approval, or project-
specific extension costs.

Growth affects demand for schools, parks, recreation,

Agricultural, Environmental, and Resilience Considerations 
Agricultural lands, waterways, flood-prone areas, wetlands, topography, soils, and other environmental conditions 
shape Milliken’s development pattern and growth opportunities. These resources contribute to the community’s 
identity and quality of life while also affecting infrastructure requirements, development cost, and long-term risk.  
Agriculture remains a defining feature of Milliken's setting and character. Working lands, irrigation systems, and 
open areas surround much of the community. Future growth decisions should consider agricultural operations, 
irrigation infrastructure, land-use compatibility, water resources, environmental conditions, and the timing of 
potential conversion. 
The Big Thompson River, Little Thompson River, South Platte River, lakes, irrigation ditches, wetlands, and 
drainageways are important natural features that support recreation, habitat, agriculture, and stormwater 
management. 
Floodplain and floodway conditions affect portions of the community and broader planning areas. Development 
within or near these areas may require additional drainage analysis, environmental review, resilient design 
measures, access considerations, or development limitations. 
Other compatibility considerations include highways, railroad corridors, industrial areas, and properties with known 
or potential environmental constraints. Development near these features may require additional attention to 
access, noise, freight activity, safety, emergency response, and the placement of sensitive land uses. 
The Development Suitability Analysis identifies generalized environmental constraints and development limitations 
throughout the planning area. Project-specific engineering, environmental review, and permitting will remain 
necessary.

2.4 Key Terms 
The Strategic Growth Element uses several terms to describe land conditions, development opportunities, and 
evaluation criteria. The following definitions reflect Milliken’s local context and provide a consistent framework for 
the analyses in Chapters 4 and 5. The land classifications are based on available property information, mapping, 
staff review, and the analysis completed for this SGE. They may change as development occurs, infrastructure is 
extended, property conditions change, or newer information becomes available. 
Term Definition 
Vacant 
A property with no primary developed use or substantial permanent improvement 
based on available assessor and parcel information. This classification does not 
necessarily mean the property is unused or available for development. 
Partially Vacant 
A developed property containing a mixture of active development and potentially 
developable residual land. These areas may support future expansion, redevelopment, 
or infill opportunities. 
Underutilized 
A developed or partially vacant property that may have reinvestment or redevelopment 
potential based on its use, intensity, condition, location, or surrounding development 
pattern. The term does not imply that a property is blighted or required to redevelop. 
Infill Development 
Development of vacant or underutilized land within established areas where 
infrastructure and public services are generally available, although site improvements 
may still be required. 
Redevelopment Reinvestment in an existing developed property through rehabilitation, adaptive reuse, expansion, reconfiguration, replacement, reconstruction, or a change in use. 
Greenfield Area 
A generalized area containing vacant, agricultural, undeveloped, or minimally 
developed land at the edge of or outside established development that may support 
future strategic growth. These areas do not represent parcel-specific development 
sites or approvals. 
Priority Growth Area 
A generalized location identified for additional consideration based on its potential to 
support housing, employment, commercial, industrial, civic, recreational, 
infrastructure, or other community needs. Identification does not establish 
development timing or an infrastructure commitment. 
Employment Center 
A location with a concentration of employment-generating land uses, including 
businesses, commercial activity, industrial operations, civic destinations, healthcare 
facilities, and institutional uses. 
Transportation and 
Walkability Center 
A destination or area used to evaluate access to parks, schools, recreation, shopping, 
businesses, services, and community facilities through the roadway and pedestrian 
network. Classification does not indicate the presence of public transit. 
General Feasibility 
A comparative assessment of whether development may be supported based on 
infrastructure, transportation, public services, environmental conditions, land use, 
fiscal considerations, and other available information. It does not determine financial 
viability or replace project-specific review.

CHAPTER 4: INFILL AND REDEVELOPMENT ANALYSIS 
This chapter identifies infill and redevelopment opportunities within Milliken and evaluates their potential to 
support housing, business investment, employment, community services, and other compatible uses. The analysis 
combines a staff-reviewed inventory of vacant and underutilized properties with an evaluation of employment 
centers, infrastructure, transportation, public services, environmental conditions, surrounding land uses, and 
community priorities. 
The identified properties and priority areas provide a framework for future planning and investment. Development 
opportunities remain subject to property availability, infrastructure capacity, environmental constraints, market 
conditions, project design, and applicable review processes. 
4.1 Analysis Approach 
The infill and redevelopment analysis evaluates vacant and underutilized properties within the context of 
surrounding land uses, infrastructure, transportation access, community services, and nearby destinations. The 
analysis identifies areas that may support future reinvestment and compares opportunities using a consistent 
evaluation framework. Priority area identification does not indicate property availability, development readiness, or 
specific future use. 
Vacant and Underutilized Property Identification 
Vacant properties were identified using assessor data, quantitative screening methods, mapping analysis, staff 
work sessions and local review. Vacant properties were initially identified using county assessor property 
descriptions and classifications, then refined based on development activity, site-specific conditions, and local 
knowledge. 
Underutilized properties were screened using a building-to-land value ratio. Properties with an assessed building 
value equal to or less than 50% were identified for additional review. A minimum value threshold was applied to 
exclude undeveloped agricultural land from the analysis. 
Town planning, engineering, utility, parks, and recreation staff reviewed the initial inventory through work sessions. 
Staff added locally identified opportunities and removed properties that were:  
• Developed, active development, or occupied. 
• Dedicated to parks, infrastructure, or rights-of-way. 
• Too small or constrained to support development. 
• More appropriately classified as greenfield 
Partially vacant properties are included with underutilized properties rather than mapped separately. The final 
inventory represents a focused set of sites that may warrant consideration for development, expansion, 
rehabilitation, adaptive reuse, or reinvestment.

CHAPTER 5: GREENFIELD DEVELOPMENT ANALYSIS 
This chapter identifies generalized greenfield opportunity areas along and beyond Milliken’s established

Unlike the infill and redevelopment opportunities evaluated in Chapter 4, greenfield areas generally provide larger,

The analysis evaluates greenfield opportunities based on infrastructure and transportation needs, public services, 
environmental and agricultural resources, development suitability, housing and economic-development objectives, 
fiscal considerations, and long-term community goals. Particular attention is given to employment growth, sales-
tax-generating uses, coordinated infrastructure planning, and long-term service responsibilities. 
5.1 Analysis Approach and Greenfield Opportunity Identification 
Milliken’s greenfield analysis uses generalized opportunity areas 
rather than parcel-specific development sites. These areas identify 
locations where future growth may be considered as infrastructure, 
ownership, market conditions, and community priorities evolve. 
Opportunity areas were identified through a combination of technical 
analysis and staff review. The evaluation considered existing land use, 
Weld County assessor data, infrastructure, environmental conditions, 
the Urban Growth Area, previous planning efforts, and locally 
identified growth opportunities. 
Agricultural land was excluded from the initial value-ratio screening to avoid classifying agricultural properties as 
development opportunities solely because they contain limited building improvements. Such properties were 
included only where staff identified them as appropriate long-term growth opportunities.

map review. Areas were adjusted to reflect current development patterns, infrastructure needs, environmental 
constraints, and local growth priorities.

pedestrian expansion areas. Opportunity areas may support future residential, commercial, employment, 
industrial, public, or mixed-use development, while the corridor and expansion areas help identify locations where

these features provide a long-term framework for coordinating development, infrastructure, and community

5.3 Infrastructure, Transportation, and Serviceability 
Greenfield opportunity areas vary considerably in their existing infrastructure availability, transportation access, 
utility serviceability, and development readiness. While some areas are located adjacent to existing development 
and municipal services, others may require significant public and private investment before development can 
occur.  
As a result, development feasibility should be evaluated based on a combination of infrastructure capacity, utility 
serviceability, transportation access, environmental conditions, land use considerations, and long-term fiscal 
impacts rather than location alone. 
Infrastructure and Utility Services  
Water, sanitary sewer, and storm sewer infrastructure are concentrated within Milliken's developed areas, with 
service coverage becoming more limited farther from the existing community. As a result, many greenfield 
opportunity areas will require utility extensions and coordinated infrastructure planning before development can 
occur. 
Future growth may require water and sewer trunk lines, lift stations, system looping, fire-flow improvements, 
drainage facilities, roadway crossings, easements, and other major infrastructure investments. Water availability, 
raw-water requirements, and system capacity will remain important considerations in evaluating long-term growth 
opportunities. 
Because several opportunity areas are located beyond existing utility networks, infrastructure improvements 
should be planned and phased to serve multiple properties and future growth areas. Particular attention should be 
given to utility expansion associated with the CR 54 strategic-growth corridor and other areas where coordinated 
investment could improve long-term serviceability. The Town should avoid isolated extensions that create 
inefficient service patterns, increase long-term maintenance obligations, or limit future connections. 
Water Supply and Development  
Water availability is a key factor influencing the feasibility, timing, and scale of future greenfield development in 
Milliken. In addition to utility extensions and infrastructure costs, development proposals must address raw-water 
requirements, treatment capacity, system improvements, and long-term water-service obligations. 
Milliken's fee schedule establishes water tap, system-development, raw-water, cash-in-lieu, and non-potable-
water charges; however, these fees do not guarantee that sufficient capacity is available for a specific project or 
growth area. Development proposals should be evaluated based on available supply, projected demand, raw-water 
requirements, infrastructure needs, water-efficiency measures, capital costs, and long-term operations and 
maintenance obligations. 
The Water Supply Element should be used alongside this Strategic Growth Element when evaluating future

General Serviceability Considerations  
The identified greenfield opportunity areas vary in their relationship to existing infrastructure, transportation 
networks, and public services. For planning purposes, they generally fall into three categories: 
1. Areas Positioned for Future Growth: Areas located near existing development, transportation networks,

Fiscal Thresholds and Growth Implications 
 Growth does not increase municipal costs in a perfectly linear manner. Many services can accommodate moderate 
increases in population and development within existing facilities, staffing levels, and infrastructure capacity. Over 
time, however, communities reach tipping points where additional growth requires new personnel, equipment, 
facilities, utility capacity, or capital improvements. These thresholds may result in significant increases in both 
operating and capital expenditure.  
In Milliken, future growth, particularly residential growth, is likely to generate increased demand for police, fire, 
public works, parks, utility, and administrative services. Growth may also require investments in transportation 
infrastructure, water and wastewater facilities, drainage improvements, and community amenities. Actual 
thresholds will vary based on development location, service standards, facility utilization, staffing practices, and 
infrastructure capacity. 
The discussion and ranges in this section are intended to illustrate how growth can affect municipal service delivery 
and should not be interpreted as formal staffing standards or fiscal projections. Understanding potential service 
thresholds can help Milliken anticipate future operational needs and evaluate the long-term fiscal implications of 
growth alternatives.

Lifecycle Funding Needs 
The cost of infrastructure is not limited to the initial construction of a road, water line, park, or public facility. Every 
public asset must be operated, maintained, repaired, and eventually replaced. A lifecycle funding approach 
recognizes these costs and considers the resources needed over the life of the asset to ensure infrastructure 
remains functional and does not deteriorate over time.  
Lifecycle funding requirements vary considerably by infrastructure type. Water and wastewater collection systems 
often have useful lives of 50 years or more and may require annual funding in the range of 2% to 4% of replacement 
value when operations, maintenance, rehabilitation, and eventual replacement are considered.  Roadway systems 
generally require more frequent preservation and rehabilitation activities and may require annual funding levels of 
4% to 8% of replacement value. Public facilities, parks, and treatment systems may require even higher annual 
investment due to shorter equipment replacement cycles and ongoing operational requirements. Consequently, 
communities should evaluate lifecycle costs by asset type rather than applying a single funding factor across all 
infrastructure systems. 
The table below presents planning-level lifecycle funding benchmarks expressed as a percentage of an asset's total 
replacement value. These benchmarks are intended to illustrate the potential scale of long-term infrastructure 
obligations associated with new development. 
Asset Type Typical Lifecycle Funding Need 
Water distribution mains 2% to 4% 
Sewer collection systems 2% to 4% 
Street and pavement systems 4% to 8% 
Stormwater systems 2% to 4% 
Parks 5% to 7% 
Buildings and facilities 4% to 8% 
 
For example, if a community's water distribution system has a replacement value of $10 million, a lifecycle funding 
target of 2% to 4% annually would equate to approximately $200,000 to $400,000 per year for ongoing operations, 
maintenance, rehabilitation, and future replacement.  
Similarly, a roadway network valued at $20 million with a funding target of 4% to 8% annually would require 
approximately $800,000 to $1.6 million per year to preserve pavement conditions, complete major repairs, and 
prepare for future reconstruction.

and long term. Future growth decisions should consider housing needs alongside infrastructure capacity, water 
availability, economic-development objectives, fiscal sustainability, and the efficient delivery of municipal services. 
The appropriate housing pattern will vary by location, infrastructure availability, market conditions, and community 
goals. Larger greenfield areas may be better suited to coordinated master-planned neighborhoods, while areas 
adjacent to existing development may support a broader mix of housing types, densities, and neighborhood-serving 
uses. Housing should be integrated with employment opportunities, commercial services, parks, trails, schools, 
and other community amenities. 
When coordinated effectively, greenfield development can provide: 
• Additional housing supply and housing diversity 
• Employment, commercial, and industrial investment opportunities 
• Expansion of the Town's sales- and property-tax base 
• Coordinated utility, transportation, and infrastructure investments 
• New parks, trails, recreation facilities, and community amenities 
• Improved connections between new and existing neighborhoods 
• More predictable and fiscally sustainable long-term growth patterns

CHAPTER 6: STRATEGIC GROWTH RECOMMENDATIONS 
AND IMPLEMENTATION 
This chapter translates the findings of the Strategic Growth Element into recommendations for future land use, 
housing, economic development, infrastructure, and community investment decisions. The recommendations are 
organized around the policy and tool review, infill and redevelopment analysis, and greenfield development analysis 
presented in Chapters 3, 4, and 5.  
The potential tools identified in Section 3.2 provide options Milliken may consider when implementing these 
recommendations. The appropriate approach will depend on the issue, available resources, staff capacity, 
development conditions, infrastructure and water availability, community priorities, and future Town review and 
approval.  
The recommendations below establish the overall direction for the SGE. Supporting language, priorities, and 
implementation details should be refined as those remaining items are completed.  
6.1 Strategic Growth Policy and Tool Recommendations 
Recommendation 1: Coordinate Growth with Infrastructure, Utilities, and Water Resources  
Coordinate future development with water availability, utility service, transportation access, public facilities, and 
capital investments. Growth decisions should consider both the infrastructure needed to support development and 
the Town's long-term responsibilities for operations, maintenance, rehabilitation, and replacement. 
Locations near existing infrastructure or planned improvements may provide opportunities for more efficient 
development. Areas requiring major utility extensions, roadway construction, drainage facilities, or expanded public 
services should be evaluated carefully and may be more appropriate for longer-term consideration. 
Potential implementation tools include:  
• Utility capacity and serviceability review.  
• Water Supply Element implementation.  
• Capital improvement planning.  
• Development and reimbursement agreements.  
• Infrastructure phasing strategies.  
• Area-wide utility or transportation planning.  
Recommendation 2: Use a Consistent Growth-Evaluation Framework  
Use the Urban Growth Area and a consistent evaluation framework to guide decisions regarding annexation, 
infrastructure investment, development review, and future planning efforts. 
Future growth decisions should consider: 
• Consistency with the Land Use and Growth Map. 
• Water and utility availability. 
• Infrastructure and transportation needs. 
• Public-service capacity. 
• Environmental and agricultural resources. 
• Housing and economic-development objectives. 
• Fiscal impacts and long-term maintenance obligations. 
• Compatibility with surrounding development. 
• Broader community benefits.

Milliken should continue coordinating with neighboring jurisdictions where growth interests, transportation 
networks, utility systems, or service areas overlap. 
Recommendation 3: Monitor Growth and Update the Strategic Growth Element  
Maintain current information on development activity, housing conditions, infrastructure improvements, vacant and 
underutilized properties, and greenfield growth opportunities. 
The Strategic Growth Element should be reviewed and updated at least every five years to incorporate:  
• Changes in development activity and infrastructure capacity 
• Water Supply Element findings 
• Housing and employment trends 
• Capital and transportation improvements 
• Environmental and agricultural-resource information 
• Updates to intergovernmental agreements and service areas 
6.2 Infill and Redevelopment Recommendations 
Recommendation 4: Prioritize Reinvestment in Established Areas  
Prioritize infill, redevelopment, rehabilitation, and adaptive reuse within established portions of the community 
where streets, utilities, public facilities, businesses, and community services already exist. 
Priority areas include:  
• Downtown and Broad Street.  
• Highway 60 and community anchors.  
• Knowledge Quest and eastern community assets.  
• Established neighborhoods near schools, parks, and recreation facilities.  
• Eastern business and industrial areas.  
Future development should respond to surrounding land uses, infrastructure capacity, transportation access, 
environmental conditions, and market feasibility. 
Potential implementation tools include coordinated development review, technical assistance, grant funding, 
development agreements, and targeted incentives. 
Recommendation 5: Expand Housing Opportunities in Compatible Locations  
Support housing opportunities that respond to needs identified in the Housing Needs Assessment and future 
Housing Action Plan. 
Opportunities may include: 
• Detached infill housing. 
• Attached housing and townhomes.  
• Small multifamily development.  
• Mixed-use residential development.  
• Upper-story housing.  
• Senior and accessible housing.  
• Rental housing.  
• Workforce and attainable housing.  
Housing should be located where infrastructure, transportation access, community services, and neighborhood 
context can support long-term success.

Recommendation 6: Strengthen Connectivity and Access 
Support reinvestment and business expansion within Highway 60 commercial areas and existing employment 
districts while improving connections among neighborhoods, schools, parks, businesses, and community 
destinations. 
Future planning and investment should focus on:  
• Highway and roadway access.  
• Freight and truck circulation.  
• Utility capacity and fire flow.  
• Sidewalks, trails, and crossings. 
• Railroad crossings and safety improvements.  
• Internal circulation and site access.  
• Transitions between employment and residential areas.  
6.3 Greenfield Development Recommendations 
Recommendation 7: Coordinate Greenfield Growth with Infrastructure and Serviceability  
Future greenfield development should be guided by infrastructure capacity, water availability, transportation 
access, environmental suitability, public-service needs, and long-term fiscal sustainability. 
Areas near existing development may be capable of accommodating growth through phased infrastructure 
improvements. Larger growth areas should be planned as coordinated districts with integrated utility, 
transportation, drainage, open-space, and public-facility networks. 
Milliken should avoid isolated infrastructure extensions that create inefficient service patterns or limit future 
connectivity. 
Recommendation 8: Advance Employment and Economic Development Opportunities  
Preserve opportunities for employment, commercial, industrial, and sales-tax-generating development in locations 
with regional access and long-term infrastructure potential. 
Key opportunity areas include:  
• Northern Highway 257.  
• CR 54 corridor.  
• Western Highway 60.  
• Appropriate portions of eastern and railroad corridor growth areas.  
Potential uses may include manufacturing, light industrial development, flex space, warehousing, distribution, 
hospitality, commercial services, and regional employment centers. 
These areas should complement Downtown and Broad Street by accommodating uses that require larger sites, 
regional access, or industrial infrastructure. 
Recommendation 9: Create Complete and Connected Growth Areas 
Greenfield development should be planned as complete neighborhoods that integrate housing, employment, 
schools, parks, trails, community facilities, transportation connections, and supporting infrastructure. 
Future growth areas should provide a variety of housing types and neighborhood formats appropriate to their 
location, serviceability, and market conditions. New development should strengthen connections to existing 
neighborhoods, employment centers, recreation facilities, and community destinations while supporting long-term 
quality of life.

inform development patterns, infrastructure design, open-space networks, and community amenities.

Monitoring 
The Strategic Growth Elements should be periodically reviewed and updated as community conditions evolve. To 
support future updates, Milliken should monitor: 
• Housing needs and development activity. 
• Infrastructure and utility investments. 
• Annexations and growth area planning. 
• Transportation improvements. 
• Agricultural and environmental resources. 
• Infill, redevelopment, and greenfield opportunities. 
• Development feasibility and serviceability assumptions. 
Tracking these factors will help ensure growth decisions remain aligned with community priorities, infrastructure 
capacity, housing needs, and long-term planning objectives. 
Five-Year Update 
Consistent with Senate Bill 24-174 requirements, the Town should formally review and update the Strategic Growth 
Element at least every five years. The review should: 
• Update demographic, housing, employment, and development information. 
• Reevaluate infill, redevelopment, and greenfield opportunity areas. 
• Incorporate completed infrastructure, utility, transportation, and capital improvements. 
• Account for annexations, development approvals, and changes in serviceability. 
• Refresh environmental and agricultural-resource information. 
• Evaluate progress on Chapter 6 recommendations and revise priorities as needed.
