ADMINISTRATION REPORT 
FOR SEPTEMBER 2026 
 
ADMINISTRATION/FINANCE COMMITTEE MEETING 
 
The Administration & Finance Committee met on September 30th. 
 
Improvement/Benefit Districts.  City Administrator Nogelmeier detailed the purpose and 
process for creating Improvement/Benefit Districts in the State of Kansas.  The Committee is 
currently discussing the possibility of establishing an Improvement District for a sidewalk 
project along 53rd street.  Nogelmeier will return in October with additional information for 
further consideration. 
 
Mission Road.  In recent weeks, the City has received complaints about speeding on Mission 
Road.  Because it is considered an arterial roadway, it is not eligible for manufactured traffic 
control devices.  Public Works deployed the city’s speed trailer for 11 days and the data showed 
the 85th Percentile Speed was 35 miles an hour.  Per the City’s Traffic Control Policy, in order 
for a street to qualify, the 85th Percentile Speed must be at least 6 miles per hour over the posted 
speed limit.  With a posted limit of 30 miles per hour, Mission Road would not qualify for traffic 
calming even if it were an eligible street.

Outdoor Movie 
Our fall Outdoor Movie was planned for the evening of September 26. Unfortunately, the 
weather did not cooperate with our plans, which led the event being cancelled. However, we 
have a full year to use the movie license we’re required to purchase for the event, so we will be

Touch-A-Truck 
Although there was pockets of rain before and after the event, the Touch-A-Truck event proved 
to be one of the most attended events in our yearly lineup. Jackson took over this event when 
he began his tenure with Fairway and has really taken it to a new level. We had 10 vehicles at 
the Fairway Pool parking lot, including a Fairway PD cruiser, Fairway Public Works Bobcat and 
street sweeper, Fairway Parks & Rec Gator, CFD2 firetruck, Smith Brothers tree truck, GFL trash 
truck, Coleman moving truck and two Johnson County Wastewater vehicles. Children were able 
to sit in these vehicles, talk to the friendly drivers and take some souvenirs home with them. As I 
said above, this annual event has really become one of our most popular events of the year, 
with several hundred attendees (even in the rain!). A lot of the credit goes to the Fairway Police 
Department, Fairway Public Works Department and all the other attendees that take several 
hours during their weekend to be a part of Fairway’s Touch-A-Truck event.  
 
 
 
 

 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 

 
Golf House Ribbon Cutting 
Close to 40 people attended the Golf House Ribbon Cutting event in mid-September, with a few 
of those signing up for memberships before they left. Since opening (3 weeks), we’ve sold 5 
memberships (all Fairway residents), booked 34 bay rentals, and had 6 teams sign up for 
October’s leagues. As the cooler weather begins to make its way in the area and more people 
begin to hear about the Golf House, I believe our membership and bay rentals will continue to 
climb.

a. Resolution to Exceed the Revenue Neutral Rate. 
 
Mayor Hepperly acknowledged that there are a number of people in attendance

minutes per speaker.  
 
Mayor Hepperly opened the Public Hearing on this item.

City Administrator Nogelmeier explained that state law requires a Resolution if 
the City intends to exceed the revenue neutral rate, which is calculated to provide 
the same dollar amount in ad valorem taxes as the previous year.   
 
Mayor Hepperly asked for questions or discussion from the Council.   
 
Councilwoman Buszek stated that she will be voting against the Resolution 
because it will increase residents’ local tax burden.  Between 2019 and 2025, her 
property taxes (and those of most of her neighbors) have increased an average of 
47 percent.  This represents a 6.6 compound annual growth rate. For context, 
property taxes across the country typically grow by about 3 to 4 percent per year 
to keep up with standard inflation. She does not believe this increase is 
sustainable for Fairway residents. She attributes the massive jump to rapidly 
inflated property valuations being met with super majority votes year after year to 
exceed the revenue neutral rate. The consequence is that the fixed income seniors, 
young families and long-term neighbors are being priced out of their own homes.  
Her lone dissenting votes throughout the revenue neutral rate era reflect how these 
compounding spikes threaten vulnerable households. The purpose of the Kansas 
revenue neutral rate law is, among other things, to give taxpayers transparency 
and force local governments to justify budget expansions. This should encourage 
cities to live within their means, just as constituents must do. She thinks the City 
should stop treating the revenue neutral rate override as a routine administrative 
formality and should instead reign in spending and prioritize tax relief for the 
residents who fund the community.  
 
Responding to Mayor Hepperly’s question, Councilwoman Buszek acknowledged 
that the 47 percent increase she referred to is the total property tax and not just 
Fairway’s portion.  
 
Mayor Hepperly asked for additional discussion from the Council.  Hearing none, 
she asked for public comment on this item.  Hearing none, she asked for motion 
and requested a roll call vote. 
 
Councilwoman Henley moved that the Council adopt the Resolution to Exceed 
the Revenue Neutral Rate. Councilman Hafner seconded the motion.

a. Consider Adoption of the 2027 Draft Budget. 
 
Mayor Hepperly opened the Public Hearing on this item.  
 
City Administrator Nogelmeier explained that a 4-mill increase is being proposed 
to the City’s property tax rate.  For the average home in Fairway, the proposed 
increase is about $346 per year or $29 per month.  The average appraised home 
value in Fairway is $754,000.   
 
The last time the City increased the mill levy was in 2016 due in part to the tax lid 
the Kansas Legislature put into effect for future budget years.  Fairway is not 
unique to increases to the mill levy as neighboring cities have also increased their 
mill levies over the last several years ranging from 3 to 6 mills.   
 
The General Fund budget is relatively steady and accounts for inflation.  That 
Fund has increased over the years because costs have risen, but the increase in ad 
valorem tax from the increased valuations has generally covered those increased 
costs.   
 
The Capital Improvement Program (CIP) is subject to market volatility.  Diesel 
prices are at an all-time high and this affects future capital projects.  The CIP is 
used for projects or purchases over $10,000 which include milling and overlaying 
the streets, curb repair and replacement, new vehicle purchase, stormwater 
infrastructure that is not covered by the Stormwater Utility Fund, major pool 
maintenance and major building maintenance, sidewalks and ADA ramps 
throughout the City.   
 
In 2009, voters approved a half cent sales tax, half of which goes into the Debt 
Service Fund to cover long-term debt, and the other half goes into the CIP Fund. 
Historically, the City has relied on transfers from the General Fund to pay for 
large capital projects.  Unfortunately, that is no longer the case as the transfers 
have not been enough to offset the rising costs of capital projects. All of the 
proposed 4 mill increase will be dedicated to the CIP.  A separate revenue line 
item will be created for collection of the mill levy increase, and those monies will 
be transferred into the CIP.   
 
City Administrator Nogelmeier outlined the increased costs affecting the City 
which have led to the proposed mill levy increase.  In 2014 the City entered into a 
lease purchase agreement for a Public Works truck, a trailer, a mower and four 
Police patrol vehicles totaling $207,000.  If that lease purchase was entered into 
today for the same equipment, the cost would be $440,000.   In 2019, the City 
purchased a new patrol car for $35,000.  Those patrol cars from the same 
manufacturer now cost about $70,000 to $80,000 each.  The average annual 
budgeted amount for the CIP for 2015 to 2019 was $386,000.  Keeping that same 
pace of work, the 2020 to 2024 CIP budget averaged about $610,000.  The 2025 
to 2029 budget is averaging $1.4M.  Those costs are expected to be even higher 
for the 2027 to 2031 projects.

Since 2020 the City’s General Operating Fund has increased by an average of 
5.52 percent per year.  This is due, in part, to a large transfer in 2024 of about 
$1M that went into the CIP to cover capital projects.  If 2024 is removed, the 
actual average increase is about 3 percent.  
 
In order to try to address the situation, department heads were asked to identify 5 
percent of their total department budgets that could be reduced or eliminated.  
These reductions/eliminations would have resulted in about $300,000.  The 
proposed reductions were presented to the Administration and Finance Committee 
and there was a consensus by the Committee not to reduce any of the identified 
budget items.  
 
Should the Council adopt the 2027 budget with a 4 mill increase, it would result 
in approximately $687,000 additional revenue in 2027 and, assuming valuations 
continue to increase, additional revenue for years 2028 and beyond will increase 
the value of the mill.   
 
City Administrator explained that with the 4 mills increase, the General Fund 
budget will be 11.35% higher than in 2026, with most of the increase being 
transferred to the CIP Fund.  He also discussed other general fund highlights.  The 
Police budget includes funds for a new records management system because the 
old system is being replaced throughout the county.  The Public Works budget 
includes funds for painting the administrative areas of their facility, a new street 
sweeper, new street sign poles, tree inventory software and sandblasting and 
repairing the City’s water trailer. The Parks and Recreation budget includes funds 
for a new refrigerator/freezer in the pool snack bar, new park trash cans, the 
installation of gutters on the pool pavilion and pump house.  Also included are 
operational costs for the golf simulators as well as the associated revenues. The 
Administration budget includes funds for the property tax rebate program and for 
work required by Water One to cap the basement fire sprinkler system that was 
installed by the previous owner.   
 
He next presented a slide showing an overall summary of the year over year in the 
proposed budget for 2027.  He also discussed the status of the Debt Service and 
other Funds. 
 
City Administrator offered to answer questions from the Council.  
 
Mayor Hepperly explained that when she joined the Council in 2009, the City was 
in poor financial position.  All the reserves had been spent, and it has taken a long 
time for those reserves to be built up again.  The Council has recommended that 
the reserves be a minimum of 20 percent for the General Fund and it is currently 
about 23 percent.  She does not believe in excessive reserves as excess reserves 
should be residents’ reserves.  The 23 percent reserves are necessary because the 
City cannot budget for acts of God or the acts of the federal or state government.
