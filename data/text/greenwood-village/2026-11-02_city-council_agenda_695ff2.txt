Subject:  Discussion Regarding Drone Delivery Regulations  
 
Summary: 
 
Drone delivery of merchandise is an emerging service that is expanding into residential 
communities, which soon could soon include Greenwood Village. Municipalities across 
Colorado are beginning to anticipate how commercial drone landings could aﬀect 
neighborhoods. At the same time, cities face regulatory limits: the Federal Aviation 
Administration (FAA) controls all airspace and delivery routing, leaving municipalities 
the ability to regulate only ground operations, land use, and privacy. Municipalities are 
empowered through their police powers to regulate aspects of unmanned aircraft 
systems (UAS” or drones) operations aﬀecting the health, safety, welfare, and property 
rights of residents, but the contours of this authority remain ambiguous . Considering 
this, it is timely for Greenwood Village to evaluate how best to manage drone delivery 
activity in a way that maintains community character while aligning with federal 
regulations. 
 
Background: 
 
The Federal Aviation Act of 1958, as amended by the Noise Control Act of 1972, 49 
U.S.C. §§ 1301 et. eq. codiﬁed exclusive federal sovereignty over airspace in the United 
States.  The term "model aircraft" falls "within the statutory and regulatory deﬁnitions 
of an aircraft" and were consequently made "subject to FAA oversight and enforcement” 
in the Modernization and Reform Act of 2012 (the "Act"), 49 U.S.C. § 40102(a)(6).  
Local governments are preempted, therefore, from regulating aviation safety or airspace 
eﬃciency. The Airline Deregulation Act, 49 U.S.C. § 41713(b)(1), preempts any law or 
regulation governing the “price, route, or service of an air carrier,” speciﬁcally 
commercial air carriers. Recreational drones and hobbyist aircraft are governed by 
separate regulations.1 Two separate sets of regulations currently govern commercial 
drone delivery, 14 C.F.R. § 135 (“Part 135”) and 14 C.F.R. § 107 (“Part 107”).2  Part 135 
is the only regulation currently authorizing commercial operators to operate drones

Interest in regulating drone deliveries on a local level is increasing as large retailers and 
delivery companies expand programs capable of transporting packages directly to 
homes. Congress passed the FAA Reauthorization Act in 20243 directing the FAA to 
promulgate rules governing commercial drones. The FAA has yet to promulgate rules 
that precisely ﬁt commercial drone delivery, though it published proposed rules last year 
that have yet to be adopted.4 Commercial drone operators, like Wing (subsidiary of 
Alphabet/Google) and Prime Air (Amazon) obtained certiﬁcates to operate under Part 
135. Unlike some states, Colorado does not preempt municipalities from regulating 
drones, leaving federal preemption the primary concern for local governments 
attempting to regulate the eﬀects of commercial drone activity. As a result, several 
jurisdictions, including Aurora, Boulder, Northglenn, and Lakewood, have already taken 
steps to restrict drone activity on parks, open space, and other city-owned land, 
illustrating how drone operations can intersect with residential areas, public spaces, and 
sensitive community uses.  
 
Discussion: 
 
As Greenwood Village considers how drone delivery might ﬁt within the community, it is 
important to understand the interplay between federal and municipal authority. The 
FAA governs all commercial drone delivery operations, sets ﬂight rules, and controls 
airspace regulation, including routing, altitude, and authorization processes such as Low 
Altitude Authorization and Notiﬁcation Capability (LAANC). It is a commonly held 
understanding that municipalities cannot regulate how or where a drone ﬂies once 
airborne.  
 
Despite these limits, cities retain full authority over the ground environment, including 
landings, takeoﬀs, staging locations, and the use of parks or open spaces. Ground-based 
controls present opportunities to create buﬀers around schools, parks, and other 
sensitive areas where drone landings would not be appropriate. Colorado’s privacy and 
trespass statutes reinforce this reasoning, suggesting that careful regulation of local 
landing practices may help prevent conﬂicts between businesses (operators) and 
residents.  
 
In addition, examples from other municipalities demonstrate how cities have 
approached similar issues using their land-use authority.5

Next Steps: 
 
Moving forward, Greenwood Village may beneﬁt from a focused review of drone delivery 
implications on land use, neighborhood compatibility, and public property operations. 
Coordinating with FAA representatives and commercial delivery providers would allow 
the city to understand anticipated delivery corridors, technical constraints, and likely 
landing practices. Based on this information, Greenwood Village could evaluate the need 
for local rules governing takeoﬀ and landing locations on city property, establish 
expectations for quiet-hour delivery limits, and identify sensitive areas (such as 
neighborhoods, parks and schools) where landings should be regulated or prohibited.   
 
As federal regulations continue to evolve, Greenwood Village may wish to maintain 
ongoing communication with the FAA to ensure local policies remain aligned with 
national standards while protecting the community’s character and quality of life. The 
City may also wish to enact a temporary moratorium on building permits for 
commercial drone delivery hubs while it evaluates potential regulations in accordance 
with the information contained in this report.

Executive Summary: 
  
In August 2022, City Council approved the City’s current compensation philosophy, which "unbanded" 
positions by comparing each role individually to the market based on job descriptions and responsibilities, 
establishing pay ranges according to survey data rather than a fixed percentile, and using third-party regional 
compensation survey data rather than a predefined agency list. The current philosophy also ensures employee 
salaries remain proportionate within adjusted ranges, maintains performance-based merit adjustments, and 
includes an annual review of the compensation plan. 
  
Recommendation: 
  
To balance the need for market alignment with greater stability and predictability in salary planning, the 
Compensation Committee, comprising Mayor Lantz, Councilmember Hilton, and staff, determined that the 
organization should implement a compensation review cycle in which comprehensive salary assessments are 
conducted every three years, with inflation-indexed salary adjustments and reviews of employer-provided 
benefits in the intervening years. Unless Council expresses concern or prefers to consider an alternative 
structure, this change will be implemented moving forward. 
  
Attachments: 
  
1. Proposed Compensation Policy 
  
Discussion 
  
The City currently conducts comprehensive salary assessments on an annual basis. While these reviews 
provide detailed information, they can also create volatility at the position level due to variations in survey 
participation, sometimes resulting in abrupt increases or stagnation that pose challenges for both employees 
and budget planning. Because personnel costs represent the City’s largest expenditure, a consistent and 
sustainable approach to salaries is critical. 
 
Combining periodic salary assessments with interim adjustments based on widely recognized indices, such as 
the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners (CPI-W), helps smooth year-to-year fluctuations, align pay 
with broader economic trends, and provide a more stable and predictable structure. CPI-W, published by the 
U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, measures inflation as it affects households of wage earners and clerical 
workers and is widely used to guide cost-of-living adjustments. Using the CPI-W to adjust salaries in years 
when a comprehensive salary analysis is not conducted ensures that wages keep pace with inflation, while 
allowing flexibility to conduct ad-hoc salary reviews for positions experiencing recruitment or retention 
challenges. 
 
Reducing the frequency of full assessments also enables staff to take a more holistic view of total 
compensation, including health benefits, wellness programs, retirement contributions, and other incentives, 
supporting employee well-being while maintaining organizational competitiveness. Other municipalities,

City of Greenwood Village 
Proposed Compensation Policy 
Purpose 
The purpose of this document is to articulate the City of Greenwood Village’s compensation 
philosophy and provide guiding principles for establishing, maintaining, and administering 
employee compensation in a manner that supports the City’s ability to recruit, retain, and reward 
a highly qualified workforce. 
Market-Based Pay 
• Market adjustments will be determined through a comprehensive salary analysis 
conducted every three years and through changes in the Consumer Price Index for 
Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers (CPI-W) in intervening years, subject to the 
overall compensation funding approved by City Council.  
• The City retains flexibility to conduct targeted evaluations of specific positions as 
needed, particularly in response to recruitment or retention challenges. 
• The urban corridor from Fort Collins to Pueblo will serve as the comparative salary 
market, with greater comparative weight given to agencies within the metropolitan area, 
particularly those in and around the C-470 corridor.  
• When necessary, the City may expand its analysis to include comparable jurisdictions 
across Colorado to ensure sufficient and reliable market data. 
• Each job will be evaluated and compared to relevant positions in the market, using 
duties and responsibilities as the basis for comparison. 
• Pay ranges will be established using comprehensive, survey-driven data provided by 
independent third-party sources. 
• Salary survey data will be adjusted using third-party industry and market analysis to 
reflect projected compensation levels for the upcoming year (i.e., “aging”). 
• When pay ranges are updated to reflect the market, employees exhibiting performance 
that meets or exceeds expectations will remain at the same percentile within the 
adjusted range. 
• Employees exhibiting performance that does not meet expectations will remain at their 
current salary or be placed at the minimum of the adjusted range, whichever is greater. 
• If the salary analysis suggests a reduction in the individual range, the range will be 
frozen, and employees’ salaries will remain unchanged.

Pay-for-Performance Merit Pay 
• The City will reward employees based on their performance, using formal evaluation 
procedures and documented appraisal instruments. 
• Merit increases will be determined based on employee performance, prevailing market 
trends, and applicable budgetary and economic conditions, and subject to the overall 
compensation funding approved by City Council. 
 
Employer-Provided Benefits 
• In years when a comprehensive salary analysis is not conducted, the City will evaluate 
the competitiveness and value of employer-provided benefits against regional 
benchmarks to ensure they remain competitive within the defined market. 
• The City will assess benefit utilization and employee feedback to guide cost-effective 
enhancements, eliminate underutilized offerings, and ensure offerings remain 
competitive, valued, and fiscally responsible.
