Executive Summary: 
  
In accordance with C.R.S. 31-25-1211, Fiddler’s Business Improvement District (“District”) shall file an 
operating plan and budget with the City of Greenwood Village no later than September 30 of each year. In 
turn, City Council is required to approve or reject the document within thirty days of its submission, but no later 
than December 5. The operating plan and budget shall identify the services or improvements to be provided, 
any taxes, fees, or assessments imposed, the principal amount of bonds to be issued, and any additional 
information the municipality may require.  
  
Recommendation: 
  
As a separate entity, the Fiddler’s Business Improvement District is solely responsible for the development 
and content of its Annual Operating Plan and Budget. The submission of this document constitutes a 
recommendation by the District’s Board of Directors for approval. City staff has reviewed the document and 
has concluded that it conforms to the criteria set forth by statute.  
  
Attachments: 
  
1. 2026 Amended and 2027 Annual Operating Plan and Budget 
  
Discussion 
  
The Fiddler's Business Improvement District was organized by the City of Greenwood Village via Ordinance 
No. 41, Series of 1999. Since then, the District has provided public parking facilities and other public 
amenities. The District currently owns a 1,436-space public parking garage, a parking easement, an 
undeveloped parcel of land, three floors of parking, the art gallery/museum space on the first floor of the 
Palazzo Verdi Building, and other necessary interests in the common elements of the building. 
 
As submitted, the 2026 Amended and 2027 Annual Operating Plan and Budget proposes an increase in the 
District’s mill levy from 88.000 mills to 104.000 mills. The increase is driven entirely by reductions to the 
anticipated assessed valuation associated with the ongoing Palazzo Verdi property tax appeal. This request is 
consistent with the authority granted by the District’s electors at the November 7, 2000, election, which 
authorized the District to increase revenues annually by up to $500,000 as necessary to meet operating, 
maintenance, and other District expenses. 
 
The 2026 Amended and 2027 Operating Plan & Budget does not request new authorization and does not 
propose issuing any new debt. The Fiddler’s Business Improvement District (FBID) retains authority to issue 
additional debt as outlined in in Exhibit C of the District’s submission. The District’s outstanding obligations 
include the 2022 General Obligation Refunding Bonds, the remaining balance of the 2013B Bonds, and an 
annual‑appropriation note related to the RTD Lease Termination Agreement. While the Series 2022 bonds 
become callable in late 2027, the District does not assume an early redemption in the 2027 plan.

2026 Amended and 2027 OPERATING PLAN AND BUDGET FOR THE 
FIDDLER’S BUSINESS IMPROVEMENT DISTRICT 
PURPOSE AND SCOPE OF THIS DISTRICT 
Introduction.  The main purpose of the Fiddler’s Business Improvement District (“District”) is to 
finance needed public improvements and services in the designated commercial area within the 
City of Greenwood Village (“City”). 
The territory within the boundaries of the District is shown on the map provided in Exhibit A
attached hereto. 
Operating Plan Contents.  Pursuant to the provisions of the Business Improvement District Act, 
Section 31-25-1201, et seq ., Colorado Revised Statutes, as amended, this Operating Plan 
specifically identifies (1) the composition of the Board of Directors, (2) the services and 
improvements to be provided by the District, (3) the taxes, fees, and assessments to be imposed by 
the District, (4) the estimated principal amount of the bonds to be issued by the District, and (5) 
such other information as the City may require. 
This is the Operating Plan and Budget for 2027; it also amends the prior Operating Plans and 
Budgets to the extent necessary to accomplish the BID’s objectives.   
BACKGROUND 
The Fiddler’s Business Improvement District (BID) was organized by the City of Greenwood 
Village by Ordinance No. 41, Series of 1999 dated October 18, 1999.  Since that time, the BID has 
financed and furnished public parking facilities and other public amenities for the community it 
serves.   
The BID has financed and owns a 1,436-space public parking garage, a parking easement on Tracts 
C and D within the District, an undeveloped land parcel within the District, the three floors of 
Page 11 
3 
parking in the Palazzo Verdi Building, the art gallery/museum space on the first floor of the 
Palazzo Verdi Building and necessary interests in the Palazzo Verdi common elements.  With 
necessary restrictions for security, public parking is available for events at the Fiddler’s Green 
Amphitheatre. 
These improvements were financed through a series of bond issues.  The outstanding bonds are 
identified in the Financial Plan below. 
The City has the authority to review and approve this Operating Plan and Budget under Section 
31-25-1211, C.R.S. 
COMPOSITION OF THE BOARD OF DIRECTORS 
As mentioned previously, the Fiddler’s Business Improvement District was organized by the City 
of Greenwood Village by Ordinance No. 41, Series of 1999 dated October 18, 1999.  Under that 
Ordinance and subsequent enactments, the Board of Directors of the District has been appointed 
by the City Council.  All Board members must, by law, be electors of the District.  The current 
members of the Board of Directors as of September 1, 2026, are:

DESCRIPTION OF IMPROVEMENTS AND SERVICES 
Continuation of Prior Authority.  The District continues to have the authority to provide public 
improvements, services, operations and maintenance as approved by the City in the District’s 
previous Operating Plans.  The improvements and services that the District is authorized to provide 
include, but are not limited to, construction, operation, repair, and maintenance of off-street 
Page 12 
4 
parking and roadways, driveways, landscaping, public meeting and event facilities, and park 
improvements. BID improvements are constructed in accordance with plans approved by the City.  
Specific Past Projects.  The BID owns the parking garage located in the District.  The BID has and 
is participating in the financing, ownership, operation, and maintenance of public parking on three 
floors, art gallery/museum, and District portions of the common elements of the Palazzo Verdi 
office building, which has been built as a condominium project in the BID on a portion of the 
property formerly known (prior to the replat) as Lot 1 and Lot 4.  Palazzo Verdi opened to acclaim 
in 2008.  
In January 2016 the BID purchased the 1.8235-acre parcel on the Southern end of Fiddler’s Green 
Amphitheatre. It is anticipated this parcel ultimately will be used to construct a structured parking 
garage which will provide parking for a contemplated new office project on the parcel just to the 
West of it. Currently the lot accommodates surface parking for overflow spaces which benefit 
public parking in the District. See Financial Plan and Report of District activities section for more 
information regarding this parcel. 
The City, Greenwood Metropolitan District, and the BID have entered into an IGA for the 
completion of a park project on land owned by the Greenwood Metropolitan District.  The park 
project was completed in 2023.  
In 2027, the BID intends to continue the parking garage repair and maintenance program identified 
in the 5 year condition assessment prepared by Walker Consultants. Consistent with Walker’s 
recommendations, the planned work includes: 
 Sealing the exposed deck strip on level 2 
 Replacing expansion joints on level 2 
 Concrete repairs throughout the main garage structure 
 Replacing drains on level 3 
These improvements are intended to address deterioration, protect the structure from water 
intrusion, and extend the garage’s useful life.

FINANCIAL PLAN AND REPORT OF DISTRICT ACTIVITIES 
Existing Debt.  The District previously issued debt to acquire and complete the Fiddler’s BID 
parking garage, additional parking and its interest in Palazzo Verdi.  A system of user fees, charges 
to property owners, contractual payments from RTD, and property taxes, together with capitalized 
interest, have been utilized for bond repayment. 
The bonds are general obligations of the District secured by the District’s covenant to levy ad 
valorem property taxes on the taxable property, as well as all other revenue of the District which 
consists primarily of parking revenues and specific ownership taxes received by the District.  
In November 2013, the District redeemed the District’s Series 2008 and 2009 Bonds.  At the same 
time, the District issued its General Obligation Multi-Modal Bonds, Tax-Exempt, Series 2013A in 
the initial aggregate amount of $49,735,000 and General Obligation Multi-Modal Bonds, Taxable, 
Series 2013B in the initial aggregate amount of $2,340,000.   The District issued its $3,500,000 
General Obligation Bonds, Series 2015 in December, 2015. 
In June 2017, the District refinanced the Series 2013A and 2013B Bonds extending the fixed 
interest rate period from November 1, 2018 to November 1, 2021. Additionally, the District issued 
$5,500,000 in Series 2017 General Obligation Refunding and Improvement Bonds, the proceeds 
of which, net of fees and expenses, were used to retire the remaining balance of the Series 2015 
Bonds and to establish a capital reserve of $2,000,000 to be used for garage repair and maintenance 
costs and the paving, striping and landscaping of three undeveloped lots within the District, all of 
which have been completed as of this filing. 
In June 2022, the District issued its Series 2022 General Obligation Refunding Bonds in the total 
par amount of $49,905,000, proceeds of which, net of fees and the reserve fund, were used to fully 
pay off the District’s Series 2013A and Series 2017 Bonds. The Series 2022 Bonds were issued as 
two term bonds, maturing on 12/1/2032 and 12/1/2047 respectively. The term bond due 12/1/2032, 
totaling $7,390,000, bears interest at the rate of 5.00%. The term bond due 12/1/2047, totaling 
$42,215,000, bears interest at the rate of 5.55%.  The Series 2022 Bonds are redeemable on 
12/1/2027 at 103% of par. Additionally, the District extended the maturity of its Series 2013B 
Page 14 
6 
Bonds to 12/1/2025, which bear interest at the rate of 4.50% and are outstanding in the amount of 
$1,460,000. 
By law, the City has no financial obligation whatsoever for bonds issued by the BID. 
The amount of additional debt that a BID may issue is the lesser of (a) the debt approved by the 
City in the Operating Plan, or (b) the amount of debt approved by the BID voters in a TABOR 
election. 
Authorized Additional Debt.  In prior Operating Plans, the City approved the BID’s issuance of an 
additional $3,744,885 of “new money” debt over the amount of debt previously issued by the BID.  
The debt authorization is summarized in Exhibit C. 
In conjunction with the RTD Lease Termination agreement the District issued an $800,000 annual 
appropriation note, subordinate to BID Bonds, to repay the landowner for the advance needed to 
satisfy the initial RTD lease termination payment.   An annual appropriation note does not use 
voter approved or operating plan debt authorization.  In the future, the District may request that 
the City allow the annual appropriation note to be replaced by debt. Required payments under the 
RTD Lease Termination agreement were fulfilled on a timely basis and the related spaces are no 
longer encumbered by RTD.

The BID may refund all or part of its bonds if market conditions are favorable.

BUDGET 
Attached hereto as Exhibit D is a proposed 2027 budget.   The final approval for the budget will 
occur after notice and a public hearing by the District Board as required by the Colorado Local 
Government Budget Law, Part 1 of Title 1 of Article 29, C.R.S.  The primary budget activities in 
the remainder of 2026 and in 2027 will be, repayment of debt, payments made in conjunction with 
improvements to and repairs and maintenance of the parking garage and adjacent surface parking 
lots within the District, payment of the operating costs of the parking and gallery/museum 
facilities, and payments made in conjunction with the park improvements described in the 
Description of Improvements and Services section within this Operating plan. 
Upon dissolution of the District, all of the District's assets remaining after payment of or provision 
for all of its liabilities shall be paid over or transferred to the City of Greenwood Village, Colorado, 
or such other political subdivision, or such other manner of disposition, as designated by the City 
of Greenwood Village, Colorado; provided however, if there is District debt outstanding at the 
time of its dissolution, the City (or the owner of the District's former assets as applicable) agrees 
to abide by the covenants, agreements, or other provisions related to the disposition of bond 
financed property agreed to by the District at the time the outstanding bonds were issued. 
CONCLUSION 
This Operating Plan and Budget for the Fiddler’s Business Improvement District meets the 
requirements of the Business Improvement District Act and further meets applicable requirements 
of the Colorado Constitution and other law.  The types of services and improvements to be 
provided by the proposed District are those services and improvements which best satisfy the 
purposes of Part 12 of Article 25 of Title 31, C.R.S., as required by Section 31-25-1207(5), C.R.S.  
The BID would like to thank the City for its prior support and looks forward to a prosperous and 
healthy remainder of 2026 and 2027.

Executive Summary 
The primary purpose of the City’s budget and budgetary process is to develop, adopt, and implement a fiscally 
sound and sustainable plan for achieving the established goals of the upcoming year while remaining consistent 
with the community's long -term vision. The 202 7 Annual Operating & Capital Improvement Budget focuses on 1) 
maintaining existing levels of service , 2) reinvestment in the capital infrastructure of the community,  and 3) 
safeguarding the financial condition of the organization. The budget continues to maintain the high-quality services 
that residents expect, provides for reinvestment in the community’s infrastructure, and does so without imposing 
new taxes or incurring debt. 
 
In 2027, the City is investing in improvements that make day -to-day services safer, more reliable, and easier for the 
community to access. Public safety will benefit from upgraded emergency communications, more flexible on-scene 
response vehicles, and focused speed -enforcement in key areas. The Municipal Court will complete its move to a 
modern, cloud -based system that streamlines case processing and enhances the experience for community 
members. Across City facilities and technology systems, new access-control infrastructure, scheduled power-backup 
upgrades, improved internal collaboration platforms, and modern communication tools will help ensure services 
remain secure, efficient, and uninterrupted. Finally, core field operations will improve through enhanced stormwater 
system maintenance, targeted fleet and equipment upgrades, and new data-driven transportation analysis tools that 
will help the City respond more quickly and accurately to neighborhood traffic concerns. 
 
The City anticipates collecting approximately $72.6 million in revenues in 2027. Although taxes and collections, and 
investment earnings are projected to remain strong , total revenues reflect a decrease of $ 606,185, or 0.8%, 
compared to the prior year’s amended budget. This decline is primarily due to reductions in intergovernmental 
agreements, grants, and contributions. In 202 6, the City received several substantial grants and contributions in 
support of various capital improvement projects. Although the City actively seeks external funding, such 
opportunities are largely unknown during the annual budget process. Since grants an d contributions are only 
budgeted once they are reasonably certain, the lack of comparable funding in 202 7 is the primary reason for the 
anticipated decline in revenue. 
 
An ongoing exposure facing the City is its dependency on sales and use tax revenues. More than 60% of the City’s 
total revenue is attributable to sales and use tax collections, making the organization especially reliant upon, and 
susceptible to patterns in consumer and commercial spending. Furthermore, as a regional employment center, the 
City’s tax collections depend heavily on business spending, as well as that of the local workforce. In addition to local 
economic data, the City utilizes several national consumer and development -based economic indexes to interpret 
current and future trends, forecast revenues, and adopt financial strategies to maintain the financial condition and 
outlook of the organization. The underlying revenue assumptions in the 202 7 budget are intended to be 
conservative, yet realistic, given the materiality of sales and use tax revenues to the City and their inherent sensitivity 
to consumer and commercial spending. 
 
The City of Greenwood Village is a mature community, primarily surrounded by other jurisdictions with relatively 
limited area available for new development. It is because of this that development -related revenues are often 
inconsistent from one fiscal year  to the next. As such, the City does not use development -related collections to 
support the ongoing operating costs of the organization, but instead, allocates such revenues for the purpose of 
supporting the capital improvement program. 
 
As previously stated, one of the key objectives of the 2027 budget is maintaining current service levels. Forecasted 
city-wide expenditures of $72.4 million are 18.9% below those of the prior year’s amended budget. This reduction 
reflects higher operating costs, which are more than offset by a $20. 6 million decrease in capital improvement 
projects. In accordance with the City’s Charter, appropriations related to capital improvement projects do not expire 
at the end of the fiscal year, resulting in a “carry over” of appropriations from one year to the next. While such practice 
supports the cash fund ing of projects, it also artificially inflates the prior year’s budget appropriation (i.e., unspent 
2025 capital improvement funds carried into 2026 create the appearance of a significant budget reduction in 2027). 
Omitting the capital improvement projects from the comparison results in a $3.7 million, or 6. 7%, increase in

The 2027 budget builds on the City’s commitment to delivering high -quality services and attracting and retaining

Maintaining the public infrastructure and assets is a core responsibility of the City and essential to effective service

home to three major business parks , which  attract a large workforce , resulting in a weekday population of 
approximately 45,000 i ndividuals, making the City one of the largest employment centers in the region. It should 
therefore come as no surprise that regional transportation is of great importance to the community. Although most

City’s local transportation system. The 2027 budget continues to prioritize transportation, allocating $9.0 million for

City’s parks and recreation infrastructure, including the replacement of playground equipment at Sundance Hills, 
landscaping improvements at multiple parks, streetscape work, Orchard undercrossing repairs and the addition of 
restrooms at Arapahoe Station. 
 
By maintaining appropriate fund balances in each of its funds, the City can adapt to changing market conditions, 
capitalize on unforeseen opportunities, and maintain adequate cash reserves,  given the cyclical nature of the local 
economy. In 2027, revenues and other financing sources exceed expenditures and other uses by $ 308,585, 
increasing the fund balance by approximately 0.5%.  As designed, the 2027 budget allocates previously 
accumulated funds specifically to support ongoing capital improvement.  
 
The 2027 budget provides for all externally enforceable restrictions, as well as a 25% Operating Reserve Assignment, 
an Open Space Acquisition Assignment, and lastly, a Traffic Safety Assignment. The 25% Operating Reserve

shore up reserves and assist in meeting the organization's cash flow needs. Conversely, the Open Space Acquisition 
Assignment, as well as the Traffic Safety Assignment, operate more as a savings account to accumulate reserves for 
future expenditures and/or specific purposes.

COMMUNITY PROFILE 
 
 
History 
The area surrounding the City of Greenwood Village boasts a rich 
heritage, marked by early settlers, prospecting, farming, and ranching. 
Although the first pioneers began to arrive in the region in the early 
1800s, it was not until fifty years later that the discovery of gold 
brought about profound changes to the area, giving rise to several 
small communities.  
 
During the early 1900s, the area continued to thrive as prospecting 
gave way to agriculture. Orchards of cherry, apple, apricot, plum, and 
pear trees flourished in the area until 1933, when the Castlewood Dam 
burst, effectively drying up the source of irrigation. While dairy farming 
and ranching remained widespread, by the 1940s , the region had 
attracted several suburbanites , along with seasonal residents from Denver, who sought to escape to the rural 
countryside. As the area’s popularity increased, so did concerns that ongoing 
development from Denver would threaten the pastoral lifestyle enjoyed by 
residents. In response, the City of Greenwood Village officially incorporated as a 
statutory city on September 8, 1950.  
 
Whereas farming and ranching characterized the area during the first half of the 
century, by the late 1960s,  the community had transformed into a predominantly  
residential area. However, the absence of a revenue-generating commercial tax base 
made it difficult to provide the most basic municipal services. Therefore, Greenwood 
Village embarked on a twenty-year period of annexation and growth, incorporating 
several emerging, yet lucrative, business parks and commercial areas, including the 
Denver Technological Center and Greenwood Plaza business parks. As the city's 
population increased, so did the desire for enhanced local control, resulting in the 
adoption of a city charter on July 30, 1968, transforming  the community into a self -
governing Colorado Home Rule Municipality.  
 
Location 
The City of Greenwood Village is a mature community 
situated in Arapahoe County, 9 miles south of 
downtown Denver, Colorado, and surrounded by other 
jurisdictions. The City occupies 8.3 square miles and , 
according to  the US Census Bureau, is home to an 
estimated 15,691 residents. 
 
Climate 
At 5,422 feet above sea level, the area is considered 
semi-arid, characterized by  very low humidity and 
relatively little precipitation. The area is known for its 
abundant sunshine, averaging over 275 sunny days per 
year. During the summer, it is common to have 
temperatures exceeding 90°; however, days with 
temperatures reaching  100° are unusual. 
Thunderstorms are common during the growing 
season, from April to September, and account for 
approximately 75% of the annual precipitation in  the 
area. Winters are normally mild; however, snowstorms 
measured in feet do occur on occasion.

BUDGET PROCESS 
 
 
Overview 
The City of Greenwood Village’s fiscal year begins on January 1st and ends on December 31st of each year. Although 
the City legally appropriates its budget annually, the process includes discussions of  multi-year financial planning, 
such as the City’s Comprehensive Plan, Transportation Plan, Master Regional Drainage Plan, individual Park 
Development Master Plans, and Capital Improvement Plan. Such plans assess the City of Greenwood Village’s short 
and long-term infrastructure needs and strategic priorities. Where appropriate, the recommendations and initiatives 
provided in these plans have been incorporated into the planning and development of the annual budget.  Capital 
project expenditures for the upcoming year are directly associated with the City’s Capital Improvement Plan. 
 
The budget, as prepared by the City Manager and submitted to the City Council by the Mayor, shall provide a 
comprehensive financial plan for all City funds and activities for the upcoming  fiscal year. Departments prepare 
budgets via a line -item method, providing detailed documentation for revenues and expenditures. To present a 
balanced budget, the City Manager works closely with each department to coordinate funding levels. In accordance 
with the Home Rule Charter, the City Manager must present a balanced budget, for which proposed expenditures 
do not exceed available resources, no later than October 15 th, which is subsequently followed by a public hearing 
on or before November 1 st. At any time after the public hearing, the City Council may adopt the budget with or 
without amendments. If City Council fails to adopt the budget before December 31st, the amounts appropriated for 
the previous fiscal year shall be deemed adopted for the ensuing fiscal year on a month-to-month basis.

BUDGET PROCESS 
Budgetary Control 
All organizational expenditures are subject to appropriation by the governing board. Expenditure appropriations 
are made annually and, except for capital expenditures, lapse at the end of the fiscal year. Appropriations for capital 
expenditures continue in force until expended, revised, abandoned, or repealed. City Council's adoption of the 
budget establishes appropriations at both the fund and department level s. Once appropriated, the Finance 
Department monitors and reports  on the organization's financial activity and condition . The City uses financial 
software, policies, and procedures to maintain adequate control over public resources. Each department is 
responsible for controlling expenditures within authorized appropriations; however, City Council retains ultimate 
budgetary responsibility . As allowed by the City’s Charter, requests for supplemental, emergency , or reduction 
appropriations to the budget shall be issued in a public notice and subject to the approval of City Council through 
enactment of an ordinance.  
Basis of Accounting vs. Basis of Budgeting 
Basis of Accounting refers to the specific time at which revenues and expenditures are recognized in the accounts 
and reported in the financial statements. The basis of accounting used often depends on the purpose for which the 
fund has been established. P roprietary and fiduciary funds utilize the accrual basis of accounting , while all other 
funds utilize the modified accrual basis.  Under the accrual basis of accounting, revenues are recognized when 
earned, and expenses are recognized when a liability is incurred, regardless of the timing of related cash flows. 
However, under  the modified accrual basis of accounting, revenues are recognized when they become both 
measurable and available. Revenues are considered available when they are collected within the current year or 
soon enough thereafter to pay current-year liabilities .  Expenditures are generally recorded when a liability is 
incurred.  However, debt service expenditures and compensated absences are recorded only when payment is due. 
Basis of Budgeting refers to the methodology used to include revenues and expenditures in the budget. The City’s 
basis of budgeting mirrors the basis of accounting used to prepare its fund financial statements: the modified accrual 
basis of accounting. Thus, capital costs, debt, and lease payments are recorded as expenditures in the year in which 
they occur. Furthermore, depreciation and amortization costs are not included in the budget because they are non-
cash transactions. 
Fund Structure 
The organization's activities are organized into separate funds to account for specific operations, programs, and/or 
functions. Each fund is considered a separate accounting entity and therefore maintains an independent, self -
balancing ledger subject to appropriation. The three basic fund categories are Governmental, Proprietary , and 
Fiduciary; within each fund category,  there are additional fund types.  A description of the fund categories, types, 
and those employed by the City is included below: 
Governmental Funds 
The General Fund is the City’s primary operating fund  and accounts  for resources associated with general 
government operations that are not required by law or sound financial management to be accounted for in another 
fund. General Fund activities include general administration, community development, police, parks, and public 
works.  
Special Revenue Funds account for the proceeds of specific revenue sources that are legally restricted to support 
specified purposes. The City of Greenwood Village does not currently report a Special Revenue Fund.

Investment Earnings 
The City of Greenwood Village invests in a combination of fixed income securities, local government investment 
pools, collateralized time deposits, and money market accounts. In 2022, the City engaged an investment advisor to 
help manage the organization’s portfolio and implemented cash management practices to optimize idle cash 
reserves. Those efforts, coupled with rising interest rates,  resulted in substantial increases in investment earnings. 
Current-year collections are expected to align with initial forecasts; however, interest rates are expected to decline 
in the future. Investment earnings account for approximately 3.0% of the organization’s income.
