SUBJECT:  Proclamation for National Immigrants Day - Presented by Councilmember Lovisone 
   
Report in Brief 
  
City Council will designate October 28, 2026 as National Immigrants Day.  
     
  Prepared by: 
  Chris Koch, CCO Admin 
    
  Reviewed by: 
   
    
Approved by:    
  
Allison Scheck, Deputy City Manager 10/06/2026 
Don Wick, City Manager 10/06/2026 
  
Enclosure, exhibits & attachments required to support the report 
 
 
 
 
WHEREAS, the United States of America has been shaped by generations of immigrants who have come 
from every corner of the world in pursuit of freedom, opportunity, safety, and a better future for 
themselves and their families; and 
WHEREAS, immigrants have enriched our communities through their cultures, traditions, languages, 
faiths, ideas, talents, entrepreneurship, and commitment to civic life, strengthening the social, cultural, 
and economic fabric of our nation; and 
WHEREAS, immigrants have made enduring contributions as educators, healthcare professionals, 
entrepreneurs, workers, artists, scientists, public servants, community leaders, neighbors, and friends, 
helping our communities grow and thrive; and 
WHEREAS, Congress designated October 28, 1987, as National Immigrants Day, recognizing the 
contributions of immigrants to the United States and encouraging appropriate observances and activities 
and October 28 was selected in connection with the anniversary of the unveiling of the Statue of Liberty, 
an enduring symbol of freedom, welcome, hope, and opportunity for people arriving on America's shores; 
and 
WHEREAS, we recognize that our communities are stronger when we honor the experiences and 
contributions of all residents and when we foster understanding, respect, inclusion, and opportunity for 
people of every background. 
NOW, THEREFORE, BE IT PROCLAIMED by the Mayor and City Council of the City of Arvada, 
Colorado, that October 28, 2026, be designated as National Immigrants Day and we encourage all 
residents to celebrate the many contributions of immigrants, learn about the rich histories and cultures 
represented in our community, and join together in honoring the people and families whose courage, hard 
work, resilience, and dreams continue to strengthen our shared community and our nation. 
Dated this 20th day of October 2026

SUBJECT:  Proclamation for National Homelessness Awareness Month - Presented by Mayor Pro Tem 
Moorman 
   
Report in Brief 
  
City Council will proclaim November 2026 as National Homelessness Awareness Month. 
 
Members of the Homelessness Response team and the local faith community will be present to accept the proclamation.  
     
  Prepared by: 
  Chris Koch, CCO Admin 
    
  Reviewed by: 
   
    
Approved by:    
  
Allison Scheck, Deputy City Manager 10/07/2026 
Don Wick, City Manager 10/07/2026 
  
Enclosure, exhibits & attachments required to support the report 
 
 
 
 
WHEREAS,  each November, jurisdictions and organizations across the country recognize National 
Homelessness Awareness Month; and  
WHEREAS,  regional and local collaboration has contributed to an overall decrease in homelessness 
throughout Colorado this past year, although an increase in youth and family 
homelessness, according to the Metro Denver Homeless Initiative; and  
WHEREAS,  as of January 2026, there were 289 people in Arvada experiencing homelessness; and  
WHEREAS,  the City of Arvada is proud to partner with and support organizations like Mission 
Arvada, The Family Tree, Safeside Recovery, and numerous communities of faith in a 
coordinated response to homelessness; and  
WHEREAS,  this year, that collaboration has resulted in more than 65 people in Arvada finding 
housing through family reunification, permanent supportive housing, market rate or 
subsidized housing, and other outcomes.  
 
NOW, THEREFORE, BE IT PROCLAIMED by the Mayor and City Council of the City of Arvada, 
Colorado, that November 2026 be designated as National Homelessness Awareness Month and encourage 
all Arvada community members to join us in thanking those involved in this important work and to 
continue to offer support where they feel comfortable. 
Dated this 20th day of October 2026

SUBJECT:  City Attorney's Office Update on Legislative Drafting and City Code Modernization 
   
Report in Brief 
  
Since 2020, the City Attorney’s Office has substantially accelerated modernization of the City Code. During that time, of 1,822 
analyzed current sections, 885 sections—or 48.6%—have been revised. During the 12 months ending August 25, 2026, the City 
team revised 511 unique current sections, representing 28.0% of the analyzed Code. This presentation summarizes this 
progress, identifies the remaining backlog, and outlines a sustainable review program. 
  
Financial Impact 
  
There is no immediate direct financial impact associated with receiving this presentation. The current plan assumes that the 
work will be managed primarily within existing departmental resources and coordinated with affected departments. If annual 
workloads, unusually complex chapter rewrites, or unanticipated legal mandates exceed available capacity, staff may return to 
Council through the budget process with options for temporary drafting support or other resources. 
  
Background 
  
The City Code had accumulated sections that had not been revisited for many years, along with provisions for which no prior 
ordinance revision was recorded. The City Attorney’s Office compiled and analyzed amendment data across 31 chapters to 
establish a baseline, measure recent work, and identify chapters with the greatest remaining modernization need. 
  
Discussion 
  
The proposed approach has two phases. First, the City Attorney's Office will coordinate completion of the remaining 937-
section backlog by December 31, 2029. Work already underway on Chapters 2, 46, 54, and 86 will be incorporated into that 
effort, with remaining chapters sequenced according to age, legal risk, operational importance, and project size. The proposed 
approach is to move the City from catch-up work to a recurring ten-year review cycle beginning in 2030. Approximately 185 
sections will be scheduled for review each year, with additional capacity reserved for off-schedule amendments required by 
changes in law, Council priorities, or operational needs. A review will be complete when a section is revised, repealed or 
consolidated, replaced through a broader rewrite, or affirmatively retained after documented review.

Background 
  
The flood risk program, developed by the MHFD, is advancing with cutting-edge technology and modeling methods that will 
more accurately analyze flood risk by inserting collection systems such as streets and pipe networks into the modeling software. 
This will depict not only flooding in waterways (fluvial), but surface flooding (pluvial) that follows how water moves in streets, 
parking lots and around buildings prior to entering the creeks.  
 
The end goal is to assist local governments by developing a district-wide prioritization process as well as information for 
decision making. The Return on Investment (ROI) scoring technique will consider flood impacts to people rather than the value 
of structural losses. This scoring will shape the future of planning, support design decisions, set maintenance priorities, and 
other potential applications in conjunction with our valuable partnership with MHFD. 
 
MHFD will cover half the cost of the study, and local governments in the district will be contributing the other half. Costs were 
calculated for each jurisdiction based on watershed size, development, stream length, quantity of storm drains, and complexity 
of the basin. 
  
Discussion 
  
Traditional floodplain mapping focuses primarily on a 1-dimentional boundary adjacent to waterways. Removing the traditional 
binary view of flood risk (either in or out of the floodplain) and moving toward a graduated risk will help local governments 
and community members understand the percent chance of flooding a specific structure or road in any given year. Graduated 
risk calculations incorporate water depth and velocity to establish a comprehensive score based on direct threats to vehicles, 
people, and property. This scoring technique will be applied to “heat maps” which will show high risk areas as “hot spots.”

MHFD FLOOD RISK EXHIBIT A 
Intent, Assumptions, and Outcomes 
1. Program Overview and Intent 
The MHFD Flood Risk Program establishes a district-wide, technically consistent methodology for quantifying flood 
hazards and translating them into measurable flood risk to people and infrastructure. The Program supports Capital 
Improvement Program (CIP) prioritization, increases transparency in investment decisions, and improves 
communication of flood risk across jurisdictions. The Program does not modify FEMA regulatory floodplains or create 
new regulatory designations. However, the Program develops foundational riverine models that may support future 
FEMA regulatory mapping or local regulatory adoption. 
Guiding Policies Primary Objectives 
• Clarity, accessibility, and transparency 
• Integration of flood risk into broader decision-making 
• Consistent, defensible technical standards 
• Defined governance with flexibility to support 
regulatory needs 
• Achievable lifecycle management and sustainability 
• Communicate flood risk clearly beyond binary mapping 
Quantify flood hazards using consistent modeling 
• Translate hazard into risk using defined receptors 
• Provide defensible risk metrics to evaluate CIP impacts 
• Strengthen alignment of MHFD and Local Government 
CIP prioritization processes 
2. Data Development Workflow 
The steps on the right will be completed by 
watershed, in batches across multiple 
geographies, to develop district-wide 
existing hazard and risk information by 
2029 (Phase 1 Production). Additional steps 
will occur in future efforts at the request of 
local governments (Phase 2 Planning). 
The process begins with foundational 
datasets (Step 1), which are used to build 
hydrologic and hydraulic (H&H) models 
(Step 2). These models produce pluvial and 
fluvial flood hazard maps. The hazard maps 
are then combined with people receptor 
datasets from Step 1 to develop flood risk 
information (Step 3). 
The existing hazard and risk results are used to identify flooding problem areas and hotspots (Step 4) and then 
flood risk is aggregated into problem areas to establish baseline risk values (Step 5). 
Phase 2 builds on the Phase 1 models by updating them to represent future or post-project conditions (Step 6) and 
recalculating hazard and risk (repeating Steps 2 and 3). The difference between existing and post-project risk is then 
aggregated across problem areas (Step 5) and used to calculate cost-based risk-reduction ratios (Step 7). These 
ratios support CIP project selection based on cost-effectiveness, focusing on projects that reduce the most risk for 
the least cost. 
3. Core Assumptions in Modeling Hazard 
Flood Risk will create two-dimensional (2D) Rain-on-Mesh (RoM) models calibrated to historic events, primarily via 
HEC-RAS v7, with flexibility to use InfoWorks ICM based on local government preference. Models will be constructed 
with a detailed mesh, typically 50-foot in developed areas that are further refined to 25 feet along features or 100-foot 
in undeveloped areas. Model details will be based on readily available data (e.g. MHFD FHADs/MDPs, local 
government partner data, Denver Regional Council of Governments (DRCOG) data, etc.) and often require 
assumptions based on engineering judgement where data and attributes are missing, which is common for sewer 
datasets and occasionally for structures in the channel. Assumptions made to address data gaps will be documented 
and may be addressed in a future effort. The storm sewer systems will generally be represented for pipes 2 feet and
