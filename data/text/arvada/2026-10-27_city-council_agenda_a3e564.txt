Background 
  
Significant wildfires have increased in prevalence, severity, and damage caused over the past 2 decades. The recently 
completed Arvada Community Wildfire Protection Plan (CWPP) identified more than 25 significant fires in the Arvada area of 
interest alone in the past 15 years, with several within or in the vicinity of the Northwest Arvada Urban Renewal Area. In 2026, 
the City has experienced multiple vegetation fires in this area or immediately adjacent to it. While these fires were fortunately 
contained before damaging any homes, it highlights the growing risk. This increased risk has led to a significant improvement 
in technology associated with the detection of fire activity and prevention of fire spread. The focus of these technologies is to 
provide early warning of potential wildfires before they would typically be identified and reported through traditional means 
(i.e. someone observing smoke or flames and calling 911), allowing for more rapid response and containment and thus 
minimizing the impact on people and property. Current practice points to the combination of cameras that visually detect 
wildfire indicators coupled with chemical sensors that can detect the presence of chemicals present in wildfire over long 
distances as being effective for early detection. The effectiveness of these tools is further enhanced using Artificial Intelligence. 
These tools are currently in use in other parts of Colorado and across the US in high-risk wildfire areas. The proposed project 
deploys a combination of cameras and chemical sensors to protect the Urban Renewal Area, which was identified as a risk area 
in the CWPP process which Council has previously approved. Working with the Arvada Fire Protection District and leveraging 
information gathered and analyzed by 3rdparty experts through the Community Wildfire Protection process, we worked with 
technology vendors to design an early warning system that would provide effective long-range detection of wildfire activity 
that could impact the area. Cameras would be placed to the east and the south of the area and would be focused on the risk areas 
to the west and north. These would be coupled with a line of sensors in the northern part of the area that are optimized for 
protecting the area. It is important to provide detection capabilities in the areas adjacent to the Urban Renewal Area in order to 
best protect the area itself and provide adequate response times to prevent fire spread and subsequent damage to the area. The 
combination of these two technologies provides an optimized level of protection for the area. In addition, the installation of the 
cameras links us in to a broader network of cameras in the area owned by Xcel and other government entities.

In the May 12, 2026, City Council Workshop entitled Wildfire Mitigation & Response Strategies, Arvada Emergency 
Management and the Arvada Fire Protection District presented a multi-faceted strategy for preventing, detecting, and 
responding to the threat of wildfire in the City, and this IGA helps us significantly advance the detection and response 
components of that strategy in an area known to be at risk. Targeted deployment time would be before the end of this calendar 
year. The City’s Emergency Management Office would be responsible for the ongoing monitoring and usage of the system in 
cooperation with the Arvada Fire Protection District and as a contingency, the Arvada Real Time Information Center (ARTIC) 
managed by Arvada Police, which Council has previously been briefed on. These partners all play a role in ensuring protection 
for the area from wildfire.  The system will provide significantly enhanced detection and response capabilities to address one of 
the most potentially devastating threats that the City faces, and reduce the risk to life and property in an area that has been 
identified as being at risk by outside experts in the Community Wildfire Protection Plan process. After examining results from 
pilot programs and implementations in Colorado, technical analysis by 3rd parties, reviews by government agencies, and 
performance of existing installations, staff has not found an alternative that is likely to be more effective for the particular threat 
and geography of the area.  
 
During the September 1, 2026 Business Meeting, Council directed staff to further review privacy and data ownership concerns 
raised during the public comment portion of that meeting. Staff has completed a number of activities, including vendor review, 
additional vendor due diligence, and public involvement activity to ensure that concerns have been addressed. Through these 
activities, staff believes that the concerns regarding privacy and data ownership are adequately addressed and that there is 
support for these efforts from the neighborhoods most impacted by the threat of wildfire. Specifically, the City has a number of 
contractual terms in place that address data and privacy, and has secured further written assurances from the vendors that 
address the concerns expressed. The City has confirmed that the chemical sensors only detect specific chemical signatures, and 
do not contain any cameras, microphones, or other surveillance devices that would enable functions beyond this chemical 
detection. The City has also confirmed that the particular views and capabilities of the wildfire detection cameras minimize 
potential privacy concerns, but that standard practice in the contract with the vendor is for all spaces and structures where there 
is a reasonable expectation of privacy are pixelated before they are uploaded from the cameras, creating an additional 
safeguard. As part of the implementation process, the City would work with the vendor for all available views of the cameras to 
determine what should be pixelated. We have also confirmed in writing that there are no facial-recognition, license-plate-
recognition, biometric-identification, or similar identification or tracking capabilities in the cameras. For both technologies, the 
City controls who has access to the information and all access is tracked and logged, with the City having full access to those 
logs.   
 
Staff will present additional feedback and information gathered through the public involvement and due diligence process.

SUBJECT:  Homelessness Action Plan 
   
Report in Brief 
  
This workshop will provide City Council with an overview of the proposed Homelessness Action Plan which will guide the 
City's work in responding to homelessness through 2030. The Plan outlines strategies and actions to satisfy five goals: 
1. Reduce imminent risk of homelessness 
2. Respond to short-term homelessness 
3. Respond to long-term chronic homelessness 
4. Mitigate impacts on businesses, residents and visitors 
5. Increase communication and transparency 
  
Background 
  
The existence of homelessness is clear throughout Arvada, Jefferson County and the Denver metro region. Homelessness is a 
complex social challenge facing many communities across the nation with no clear, simple or single answer. In the 2026 Point 
In Time (PIT) count, 289 people were found to be living unhoused in Arvada, with 60% reporting they were experiencing 
homelessness for the first time in their lives. Furthermore, when asked what they believed to be the most pressing need among a 
variety of options, 63% of respondents to the 2025 Community Survey cited addressing homelessness as the top concern. 
 
Staff from multiple teams across multiple City departments work on issues related to homelessness every day, and many 
collaborate with external agencies and partners to deliver results. Interdepartmental and cross-functional work of this level 
requires a plan or strategy that ensures alignment across the organization.  
 
Work on the proposed Homelessness Action Plan began in 2024 when City Council identified homelessness as one of five 
strategic results in the 2024-2030 Strategic Plan. Due to a variety of factors, the climate and discussion around homelessness as 
a social challenge was tense in Arvada at that time, and alignment on a direction for a homelessness strategic plan was difficult 
to achieve. For the past year, City staff have focused on internal alignment and process improvement that resulted in the early 
completion of the City's Wildly Important Goal to support 65 people experiencing homelessness who frequent Olde Town into 
housed exits by December 31, 2026. Building on that momentum, the City reengaged the services of expert Scott Shields of 
Soul Mining, LLC, to assist with the completion of the Homelessness Action Plan. 
  
Strategic Alignment 
  
The Homelessness Action Plan is called for in Goal #1 of City Council's Strategic Result on Homelessness. The Plan provides a 
path forward for collaborating with community and regional partners, advancing available services, coordinating resources for 
those at high risk and safeguarding public safety, security and the health of our community. 
  
Next Steps 
  
Staff is requesting feedback on the draft Homelessness Action Plan so that a final Plan may be brought forward for adoption by 
City Council.

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
 
The City of Arvada’s Homelessness Action Plan builds upon best and promising practices and 
incorporates approaches applicable to Arvada, aligned with other metro Denver communities 
actively implementing similar efforts to address homelessness. 
 
Homelessness Action Plan Purpose 
The City of Arvada’s Homelessness Action Plan helps to: 
￿ Clarify and align staff’s work with City Council’s Strategic Plan; 
￿ Create an implementable design to respond to homelessness with actions that make 
evident changes for people experiencing homelessness, and the community; 
￿ Establish and cultivate strong working relationships inter-departmentally within the City, 
with community-based organizations, the business community, the faith community, and 
other local governments to improve the collective response to, and reduce the instances 
of, homelessness. 
 
Action Plan Development 
The process of developing this Plan included research of national and local practices and 
evidence-based approaches to address homelessness, as well as the review of strategies or 
action plans from multiple communities across the country, including the Denver metro area. 
Additionally, semi-structured interviews were conducted with a diverse group of stakeholders, 
including people with lived expertise with homelessness, government, community-based not-
for-profit organizations, and members of the business community. From these stakeholder 
interviews and meetings, a collection of themes and innovative ideas were identified for 
incorporation into the strategy. Information from past community surveys and the 2025 
Community Conversations on Homelessness was also used to develop the Homelessness 
Action Plan. 
 
To ensure accountability and measure the true impact of this Action Plan, all goals and strategies 
outlined below are underpinned by a commitment to best-practice data collection, continuous 
outcome tracking, and rigorous evaluation. Furthermore, the successful execution of these 
strategies relies on deep integration and active participation with the Metro Denver Homeless 
Initiative’s (MDHI) OneHome Coordinated Entry System, ensuring that resources are allocated 
efficiently, equitably, and in alignment with regional efforts. 
 
In addition to the recommendations of high-level goals and strategies, a detailed action plan has 
been developed to assist with implementation, included below.

THE PROBLEM & CURRENT EFFORTS 
The Need 
The existence of homelessness is clear throughout Arvada, Jefferson County and the Denver 
metro region. Local efforts are being made to increase the quality of information collected and 
reported in Arvada and throughout the Jefferson County region, while acknowledging the 
inherent challenges associated with homelessness data collection. Data that can be attributed 
to the City of Arvada comes from two primary sources: the Homeless Management Information 
System (HMIS), which is administered by the Metro Denver Homeless Initiative (MDHI), and the 
annual Point in Time (PIT) count. Both sources are considered to under-report homelessness 
but are detailed here to provide a scale of the challenge in Arvada and Jefferson County.

Data from the Jefferson County 2024 PIT report show the top self-reported factors contributing 
to the experiences of homelessness among the 785 people who reported. A person or household 
may indicate multiple factors that led to their experiences of homelessness: 
 
1. Unable to Pay Rent/Mortgage (26%) 
2. Relationship Problems or Family Breakup (24%) 
3. Asked to Leave or Evicted (24%) 
4. Lost Job /Couldn’t Find Work (23%) 
5. Abuse /Violence at Home (18%) 
6. Alcohol/Substance Use (17%) 
7. Disabling Conditions (14%) 
8. Mental Health Condition (13%) 
9. Unable to Pay Utilities (12%) 
10. Bad Credit (11%)

It is critical to note that in the 2023 Metro Denver Point in Time report and the MDHI 2023 State 
of Homelessness Report, 91% and 94% of respondents indicated they did not choose to 
experience homelessness. Additionally, the MDHI 2023 State of Homelessness Report noted 
respondents who chose to share information about their previous address indicates 88% were 
last housed in Colorado, countering the notion that significant numbers of people are moving to 
Colorado and becoming homeless. (Note: the 2024 and 2025 State of Homelessness Reports 
and Point in Time counts do not provide comparable updated information). 
 
According to the 2024 Arvada Housing Strategic Plan, developed by Root Policy: “Rental units 
in the city have increasingly shifted to higher price points over the last decade, resulting in a 
sharp decline in units affordable to low- and middle-income households. Limited supply and low 
vacancy rates contribute to an extremely tight market.” The Plan also indicates that currently 
over half of the households renting in Arvada are cost-burdened, meaning they are spending 
more than 30% of their gross income on housing. Additionally, Root Policy states the city has a 
rental affordability gap of 2,790 units for households earning less than 50% of the area median 
income (AMI). 
 
When designing programs and solutions to address homelessness, this information needs to be 
considered, and the voices of people with lived expertise must be incorporated so that solutions 
are developed to authentically meet the needs of those who need our help. 
 
Efforts Underway within the City of Arvada 
Over the past several years, multiple programs provided directly by the City of Arvada, or by 
other governmental entities with operations in the City, have been implemented with the goal 
of addressing and mitigating the issue of homelessness. In February 2024, the Arvada City 
Council held its Strategic Planning retreat and identified homelessness as a top priority, and 
this was renewed in October 2025 when the City stated that supporting 65 individuals 
experiencing homelessness who frequent Olde Town into housed outcomes is one of its three 
Wildly Important Goals (WIGs) in 2026. This concern has been echoed by the community, 
specifically noted in community surveys in 2023, 2024 and 2025, when residents stated 
homelessness was one of the top issues facing Arvada. The 2023 survey also revealed that 
more than half of the residents support increasing partnerships with non-profits and expanding 
programs to address homelessness. Additionally, the City’s 2025 Community Conversations 
on Homelessness further reinforce this focus. Finally, when asked what they believed to be the 
most pressing need among a variety of options, 63% of respondents to the 2025 Community 
Survey cited addressing homelessness as the top concern. 
The Community Conversations on Homelessness surfaced several themes that applied across 
differences:  
●        “Both/and” over “either/or.” Most participants favored a combined strategy that 
balances assistance (housing, services, prevention) with accountability (expectations, 
targeted enforcement). There was repeated emphasis that no single approach will solve 
homelessness and that the City of Arvada should pursue a multi-faceted strategy. 
 
7  
●        Different populations, different needs. Participants repeatedly distinguished 
families/children and situationally unhoused from a smaller subset with acute behavioral 
health needs. Ideally, solutions should reflect segmentation with the recognition that more 
complex cases may require resources that are not feasibly available at the municipal 
level. 
 
●        Public order matters—so does due process. Residents want clean, safe parks 
and business districts while resisting blanket criminalization of homelessness; outreach-
led responses earn more support than enforcement-only strategies. 
 
●        Trust, transparency, and communication gaps. Participants cite communication 
and engagement gaps, unclear roles, and a desire for data and storytelling, especially 
about “unseen” family homelessness. They ask for clearer information on costs, results, 
and shelter-siting logic. 
 
●        Regional coordination and prevention are necessary but slower. There is broad 
support for prevention via rent/utility help, behavioral health access, increased affordable 
housing, and county/state partnerships. Participants acknowledge that prevention efforts 
are less visible and rental assistance funds have tightened in the post-pandemic era. 
 
On May 20, 2024, the Arvada City Council adopted the 2024-2030 Strategic Plan, which 
includes a dedicated focus on homelessness. The plan states that the City will "Collaborate 
and coordinate with nonprofits, local organizations, and regional governments to advance 
services to individuals experiencing homelessness or at risk of experiencing homelessness." 
The first strategic goal under this result specifies that the City will "Develop a Homelessness 
Strategic Plan that will set a path forward for street outreach, 24/7 emergency shelter, 
partnership agreements for the provision of services, and transitional housing opportunities. 
The Strategic Plan will consider the feasibility of and opportunities for a navigation center." In 
late 2024 and early 2025, the City of Arvada pivoted away from the pursuit of a navigation 
center due to community sentiment. 
 
Also on May 20, 2024, the Arvada City Council adopted the Housing Strategic Plan, developed 
by Root Policy with direction from City staff and the Housing Advisory Committee. 
 
This Homelessness Action Plan directly supports the achievement of the City Council's 
strategic goal and result, and ensures key strategies and actions are aligned with the Housing 
Strategic Plan. 
 
In September of 2018 the Arvada Police Department established the Community Outreach 
Resource Enforcement (CORE) unit for the purpose of developing a lasting community alliance 
by providing options, support and outreach for families and individuals experiencing 
homelessness. Specific job duties related to this unit include: 
 
￿ Development of a multi-pronged partnership to increase collaboration of service providers to 
assist people experiencing homelessness in becoming self-sufficient; 
 
8  
￿ Foster ongoing personal contact between the CORE officers and individuals and families 
experiencing homelessness to increase trust and make referrals to service providers; 
￿ Work closely with local organizations to facilitate public and private resources, mental health 
services, treatment programs, housing, employment and medical services. 
 
In 2019 the City of Arvada created One Small Step (OSS), a specialized problem-solving court 
for defendants experiencing homelessness. The primary goal of OSS is to work with defendants 
who committed low-level offenses (e.g. littering, trespassing, theft) to avoid more traditional 
court-related consequences like fines and possible incarceration, by connecting them with 
existing community resources, such as access to food, obtaining vital documents, mental health 
and substance abuse treatment, and job search and housing assistance. 
 
In 2023 the Arvada Housing Authority received a three-year, $1.9M grant from the Colorado 
Department of Local Affairs, Division of Housing. This grant provided funding for transitional and 
bridge housing, outreach services and supplies through September 30, 2026. The contract was 
administered by the Housing Manager in coordination with the Homelessness Program Manager 
and their teams. The Transitional Housing Program, located at Suburban Studios at 6455 
Sheridan Boulevard, was successful in supporting permanent exits into housing and is 
contemplated for continuation in this plan. 
 
Also in 2023, the Arvada Fire Community Support Team began providing co-responder services 
to people experiencing behavioral health crises, substance use disorder, and/or homelessness. 
This program consists of a licensed mental health clinician and paramedic allowing Arvada Fire 
to respond more effectively to immediate needs, while also providing additional wraparound 
support and care to an individual after a medical emergency. Due to current capacity constraints, 
the team is not able to offer proactive outreach, and usually only serves people who are 
unhoused when that situation co-exists with behavioral health or substance use needs.  
 
In October 2025, the City of Arvada named homelessness as one of its three Wildly Important 
Goals (WIGs) for 2026. Specifically, the goal was to support 65 individuals experiencing 
homelessness who frequent Olde Town into housed exits by December 31, 2026. The City met 
its goal on August 31, 2026. 
 
The City hired the Homelessness Program Manager in November of 2025 to convene the 
implementation of the city's homeless strategy, provide support and coordination among all city 
departments, support cold weather operations and expand regional municipal and provider 
collaborations. The Program Manager leads three additional City team members: two 
Homelessness Resolution Case Managers who support, coach and guide people experiencing 
homelessness toward economic and housing stability; and the variable hour Homelessness 
Outreach Navigator, who helps connect people experiencing homelessness in Arvada with 
services and support. 
 
The Jefferson County Public Library, also not part of the City of Arvada, has a Community 
Resource Coordinator who regularly brings organizations to the Arvada Public Library to provide 
services onsite to people experiencing homelessness, including public benefits acquisition and 
troubleshooting, food distribution, RTD passes and seasonal supplies. 
 
9  
 
There are also several community-based organizations operating programs and/or providing 
services directly to people experiencing homelessness in the City of Arvada. Some of these 
organizations are Beyond Home, Community Table, Family Tree, Grace Church, Jefferson 
Center for Mental Health, Mission Arvada and SafeSide Recovery, to name a few. 
 
The base budget for the City’s homelessness program is approximately $1.8 million in General 
Fund expenditures in the 2027 and 2028 biannual budget. This is inclusive of three full time 
employees, one variable employee, program costs (transportation, vital documents, supplies 
etc), a small scale (approximately 15 room) transitional housing program, regional severe 
weather shelter participation and Arvada’s contribution to the Lakewood Navigation Center 
operations. City staff continues to seek grant funding to either grow programming or offset the 
use of City funds. While the costs for three full time employees are included in the 10-year model, 
program costs are not budgeted beyond 2028. In addition to the homelessness program budget, 
there are financial impacts to the Police Department (CORE officers in particular), Municipal 
Court (One Small Step staffing), Housing Division (eviction prevention and housing services), 
and Parks Division (encampment cleanup), along with other support functions. 
 
Challenges in Delivering Services 
Difficulties in delivering services for the City’s residents and addressing the challenge of 
homelessness are not unique. Some of the prominent challenges include: 
￿ People experiencing homelessness often have frequent contact with the criminal justice 
system, including ticketing and jailing for low-level offenses; 
￿ Currently there is no emergency shelter in the City of Arvada, or in northern Jefferson County, 
for people experiencing homelessness; 
￿ Services for people experiencing homelessness are often siloed without strong overarching 
alignment principles and operating partnerships, despite efforts driven by coordinated entry, 
the local Continuum of Care (CoC), and the Jefferson County regional homelessness 
partnership; 
￿ A substantive lack of affordable and permanent supportive housing inventory; 
￿ Community opposition to programs serving people experiencing homelessness, including 
housing efforts, in areas close in proximity to their neighborhoods and homes.

Clarification of Roles 
Several teams are referenced throughout this document, specifically in the Goals, Strategies 
and Actions that follow. Teams include: 
 
￿ City of Arvada: The City of Arvada serves as a convenor to bring providers and services 
together. Additionally, specific City teams have specific responsibilities in this plan including 
Homelessness (navigation services, grants, overall coordination); Housing (eviction 
prevention, affordable housing development, access to housing, grants); Municipal Court 
(One Small Step Community Court); Finance (grants); Police Department, specifically the 
Community Outreach, Resources and Enforcement team (PD/CORE - law enforcement, 
community response); Arvada Economic Development Association (AEDA - assistance with 
business toolkits and response); Parks (parks maintenance including abandoned property 
management); Streets (public right-of-way maintenance including abandoned property 
management); Legal (legal review of policies related to abandoned property); 
Communications (information dissemination, feedback loops and transparency); Data 
(assistance with public facing dashboards.)  
 
￿ Jefferson County: Provide regional leadership to create a county-wide response to 
homelessness and connections to other metro-wide efforts; provide funding for capital and 
operations. 
 
￿ Jefferson County Sheriff's Office (JCSO): Regional/county law enforcement; relates to 
high utilizers of the justice system in this plan 
 
￿ Community Partner Organizations: Organizations, usually but not always non-profits, who 
have expertise in and provide services to people experiencing homelessness; collect data 
and report on impact and outcomes. 
 
￿ Faith community: Provide a variety of support services through volunteers depending upon 
individual church approaches, ranging from donation of food and other supplies, 
companionship, life skills training and other volunteer-driven activities.

GOALS, STRATEGIES AND ACTIONS 
The Homelessness Action Plan consists of five goals with associated strategies and actions. 
This work was centered around stakeholder interviews, guiding principles, alignment with the 
Arvada City Council Strategic Plan, and efforts and strategies of other metro region cities and 
counties. It incorporates best and promising practices implemented in communities across the 
country. 
 
The implementation of this plan will be led by the City’s Homelessness Program Manager, with 
collaboration from all City departments, community provider partners, and other governmental 
agencies. 
 
Note: 
On June 3, 2026, Colorado House Bill 26-1202 was signed into law. This bill requires the Colorado Department of 
Local Affairs to present a proposal for the development of a statewide strategy on homelessness prevention and 
resolution in January of 2027. The proposal, among other requirements, must include recommendations for 
collaboration between state and local partners to facilitate homelessness response. It also allows the creation of a 
new entity when local governments enter into an intergovernmental agreement to establish a multijurisdictional 
homelessness response authority. This authority must be used by the contracting local governments to prevent 
and reduce homelessness within the boundaries of those municipalities. There is not yet sufficient information to 
incorporate this statewide strategy into the Action Plan, however the City of Arvada should monitor future 
developments with the implementation of this legislation, and consider how it may affect the goals, strategies and 
actions that follow.

Connections between Resident Sentiment and Action Plan 
The Community Conversations on Homelessness were designed to help the City move 
beyond binary positions and understand the values, concerns and trade-offs community 
members wanted reflected in Arvada's response to homelessness. Participants considered 
three broad approaches: expanding housing and sheltering services; strengthening 
enforcement and public-order measures; and strengthening community partnerships with 
an emphasis on prevention. The conversations did not seek a vote for one approach. 
Instead, they explored where residents found value, where they had concerns, and what 
combinations of approaches could be broadly acceptable. 
The 2026 Homelessness Action Plan reflects that deliberative direction in important ways. Rather 
than relying on one intervention, it combines prevention, short- and long-term housing responses, 
public-safety and quality-of-life strategies, community partnerships, and communication and 
transparency. 
Community Conversation finding  Action Plan connection 
A balanced response requires multiple approaches 
rather than a single solution. Participants examined 
housing/services, public order/enforcement, and 
prevention/community partnership separately, but the 
deliberations ultimately increased appreciation for the 
complexity of homelessness and the need for a variety 
of approaches. 
The Action Plan deliberately spans five areas: preventing 
homelessness; responding to short-term homelessness; 
responding to chronic homelessness; mitigating impacts 
on residents, businesses and visitors; and 
communication/transparency. 
 
19  
Prevent homelessness before people enter or re-enter 
the system. Prevention and community partnership 
were one of the three principal approaches examined 
during the conversations that received wide support. 
Goal 1 includes diversion/reunification, landlord-tenant 
mediation and enhanced rental assistance. Goal 2 adds 
deposit and arrears assistance for people experiencing 
short-term homelessness. 
Housing and services are necessary components of an 
effective response. Expanding housing and sheltering 
services was a distinct approach considered in the 
forums. While residents were mixed on the level of 
support needed, most were supportive of services to 
assist community members into more stable 
circumstances. 
The plan includes transitional housing with case 
management, peer support and treatment access; 
individualized coordination through a By Name List; 
housing-retention services; and development of additional 
permanent supportive housing. 
Public safety and accountability matter, but residents 
expressed concern about overly punitive responses to 
homelessness itself. Participants with differing 
perspectives showed common discomfort with 
approaches such as criminalizing homelessness and 
routine camp sweeps, even while some participants 
supported greater interaction between public-safety 
personnel and people experiencing homelessness. 
Goal 4 evolves CORE toward outreach and interaction 
with unhoused Arvadans, coordinates CORE with 
navigators and peers, and establishes an 
encampment/property mitigation process that includes 
notification to unhoused residents. The plan also develops 
a system for high utilizers of the justice system. 
Residents and businesses experience real quality-of-
life impacts that should not be dismissed in the name of 
compassion. The engagement emerged from concerns 
involving safety, businesses, neighborhoods and 
quality of life alongside recognition of the need for 
humane responses. While residents acknowledge the 
need for services, no one supports ignoring 
neighborhood impacts.  
Goal 4 explicitly focuses on mitigating impacts on 
businesses, residents and visitors through business 
training, de-escalation resources, an ambassador 
program, business partnerships, encampment mitigation 
and coordinated CORE outreach. 
Community institutions should have meaningful roles in 
the response. One of the clearest areas of cross-
perspective support was providing greater structure 
and support so nonprofit organizations and faith 
communities could participate more strategically and 
effectively. 
Strategy 4.3 specifically organizes and empowers 
Arvada's faith community to support severe-weather 
response and projects involving social connection, job 
skills and interaction with unhoused Arvadans. Community 
partner organizations are also embedded throughout 
Goals 1–4. 
 
20  
The City's role can include convening and 
strengthening a broader community response rather 
than acting as the primary service provider. The 
Community Conversations helped identify support for 
the municipality acting as a convener and supporter of 
existing community efforts. 
Many actions are explicitly shared among City navigators, 
the Housing Authority, County partners, nonprofits, faith 
organizations, Police/CORE and other municipalities. 
Responses should recognize the individual 
circumstances and complexity of people experiencing 
homelessness. Deliberation increased participant 
appreciation for the complexity of homelessness and 
the need for different responses rather than a universal 
solution. 
The plan differentiates imminent risk, literal/short-term 
homelessness and chronic homelessness, with different 
interventions for each population. It also uses peer 
support, individualized By Name List coordination, 
intensive case management and wraparound services. 
Compassion should be paired with practical pathways 
to stability and accountability. The conversations 
surfaced tensions rather than requiring residents to 
choose exclusively between compassion and 
community order. 
Services paired with accountability are embedded in One 
Small Step expectations, transitional housing, treatment 
access, peer support, justice-system high-utilizer 
coordination and CORE/navigator outreach. All these 
programs provide alternatives that combine assistance 
with structured intervention. 
Trust requires transparency about what the City is 
doing, what is working and what remains difficult. 
Closing the feedback loop was intentionally built into 
the Community Conversations; participants were 
promised a report showing that their input had been 
received and used. 
Goal 5 calls for a public-facing homelessness dashboard, 
an annual progress report, a consistent messaging 
framework and a community/partner communication 
toolkit. 
The community should continue to have a meaningful 
role as implementation evolves. The deliberative 
process treated residents as contributors to 
understanding the problem rather than simply 
respondents to a City proposal. 
Community organizations and partners appear throughout 
the Action Plan, but an ongoing mechanism for resident, 
business and lived-experience input is not explicitly 
identified. 
People directly experiencing homelessness should 
inform solutions. The process sought to consider likely 
effects on people experiencing homelessness, but later 
evaluation also acknowledged challenges with 
representative participation. 
Peers and people with lived experience appear in several 
implementation roles, particularly outreach and long-term 
housing retention.

balance of service need, neighborhood impacts and 
transparent process.

NEXT STEPS 
The continually shifting landscape requires the time horizon of this Homelessness 
Action Plan to be relatively short-term, with steps to be taken into 2030. The intention of 
this Action Plan is to create traction and momentum to help generate meaningful and 
lasting solutions, and it is considered to be a dynamic and living document. The City 
team should regularly update this Action Plan to maintain relevance with ever-changing 
conditions, modify or eliminate approaches that have proven to be less effective or 
impactful, and add solutions that may not have been contemplated in this version. 
 
Implementing an enduring, multi-agency strategy requires diligence and evolving 
design, yet failing to adapt creates compounding costs for the community. The 
execution of this Action Plan underscores the City of Arvada’s commitment to regional 
collaboration, data-driven accountability, and the creation of sustainable housing 
pathways that strengthen the makeup of the entire city.
