SUBJECT:  Proclamation for Disability Awareness Month - Presented by Councilmember Ambrose 
   
Report in Brief 
  
Accepting the Proclamation will be community members, Jennifer Keelan-Chaffins and Xochitl Vargas (pronounced SO-
CHEE) 
 
Jennifer Keelan-Chaffins was born with cerebral palsy and started her life of activism for disability rights at the age of six. At 
age eight, she climbed out of her wheelchair and up the steps of the US Capital Building during the Capital Crawl, in support of 
the Americans with Disabilities Act. The media coverage of her iconic act earned her a seat at the table within the disability 
rights movement, and helped galvanize Congress into passing the first ever civil rights legislation for disabled Americans in 
1990. Today, she’s an educator, motivational speaker and co-author of the children’s book “All the Way to the Top”. Jennifer 
lives in Arvada where she continues to champion for equality, alongside the next generation of change makers like Xochitl 
Vargas (SO-CHEE).  
     
  Prepared by: 
  Chris Koch, CCO Admin 
    
  Reviewed by: 
   
    
Approved by:    
  
Linda Haley, Deputy City Manager 09/10/2026 
Don Wick, City Manager 09/10/2026 
  
Enclosure, exhibits & attachments required to support the report 
 
 
WHEREAS, Disability Awareness Month, celebrated throughout the month of October, provides an opportunity 
to increase public understanding and celebrate the many achievements of people with disabilities 
and people who are neurodivergent; and 
 
 
WHEREAS, more than 660,000 people with intellectual and other developmental disabilities live across all 64 
counties of Colorado; and 
 
 
WHEREAS, people with disabilities have long strengthened our economy and expanded our Nation's 
possibilities; and 
 
 
WHEREAS, all Arvadans can contribute to improving the lives of people with developmental disabilities by 
becoming advocates and supporters of their increased independence, capacity, and leadership; and 
 
 
WHEREAS, the City of Arvada recognizes that these individuals add great value to our community, demonstrate 
leadership, and are no longer defined by outdated notions of limitation, but instead celebrated for 
their strengths, perspectives, and contributions. 
 
 
 
NOW, THEREFORE, BE IT PROCLAIMED by the Mayor and City Council of the City of Arvada, 
Colorado, that October 2026 be designated as Disability Awareness Month and call its observance to all 
Arvada residents. 
 
Dated this 6th day of October 2026.

WHEREAS, approximately one in eight women will be diagnosed with breast cancer during her lifetime,

WHEREAS, barriers such as cost, transportation, insurance coverage, and access to timely screening and

treatment and their caregivers, and remembers those whose lives have been lost to the disease.

Colorado, that October 2026 be designated as Breast Cancer Awareness Month and encourage all 
residents to increase awareness, discuss appropriate screening with their healthcare providers, support 
equitable access to high-quality care, celebrate survivors, and remember those we have lost.

The parties are participants in the Jefferson County Regional Crime Laboratory (“JCRCL”) Intergovernmental Agreement, 
dated May 14, 2013, as amended (“Lab IGA”). Previously the Arvada Police Department assigned one employee to JCRCL. By 
virtue of this IGA, the Parties wish modify this arrangement and instead establish a reimbursement agreement for salary and 
benefits for one full-time employee employed by the JCSO and working in the JCRCL. 
 
 The Arvada team recommends that the City Council approve  R26-073, A Resolution for an Intergovernmental Agreement 
between Jefferson County, Colorado and the City of Arvada for Reimbursement of Salary and Benefits. 
  
Financial Impact 
  
There is no financial impact to the City of Arvada.  Previously, Arvada would assign an employee to JCRCL. That employee 
has since retired. Rather than hiring a new employee and assigning an employee to the lab, Arvada will now reimburse 
Jefferson County for the salary and benefits for a full-time employee. Thus, by virtue of this IGA the financial impact is net-
even. 
  
Background 
  
The parties are participants in the Jefferson County Regional Crime Laboratory (“JCRCL”) Intergovernmental Agreement, 
dated May 14, 2013, as amended (“Lab IGA”). Previously the Arvada Police Department assigned one employee to JCRCL. 
This IGA allows for Arvada to reimburse Jefferson County for the salary and benefits for one full time employee, rather than 
assigning an Arvada employee to the lab itself. Pursuant to the Lab IGA, Arvada is required to assign a certain number of 
employees to the JCRCL. By virtue of this IGA, the Parties wish modify this arrangement and instead establish a 
reimbursement agreement for salary and benefits for one full-time employee employed by the JCSO and working in the 
JCRCL.

Report in Brief 
  
PRESENTER: ADA Coordinator Ann Foster 
 
The 2026 ADA Transition Plan provides a Citywide framework for identifying, prioritizing, and addressing barriers to 
accessibility in City facilities, parks, public rights-of-way, programs, services, and communications. The Plan builds on the 
City’s previous ADA Transition Plan and documents progress made since the initial assessment, while establishing a 
coordinated approach for addressing remaining barriers and incorporating accessibility into future planning, projects, and 
funding decisions. Staff and community members have provided input throughout the update process, and the Plan supports the 
City’s strategic priorities related to infrastructure, connectivity, and equitable access. Formal adoption will provide continued 
Council support and direction for implementation and ongoing updates to the Plan. 
 
The Arvada team recommends that the City Council approve R26-074, A Resolution to Adopt City of Arvada's Americans with 
Disabilities Act (ADA) Transition Plan. 
  
Financial Impact 
  
There is no direct financial impact associated with adoption of the 2026 ADA Transition Plan. Adoption of the Plan does not 
appropriate funds or authorize specific expenditures. Future costs associated with accessibility improvements identified in the 
Plan will be evaluated and addressed through the City’s normal budgeting and capital planning processes, as funding becomes 
available. 
  
Background 
  
The City of Arvada’s ADA Transition Plan is grounded in the City’s responsibilities under Title II of the Americans with 
Disabilities Act (ADA) and federal regulations requiring public entities with 50 or more employees to develop and maintain a 
transition plan when structural changes are necessary to achieve program accessibility. In 2018–2020, the City worked with 
Meeting the Challenge (MTC) to complete an ADA self-evaluation and develop its initial ADA Transition Plan, establishing a 
framework for identifying and addressing accessibility barriers throughout the City. While the original plan was not formally 
adopted by City Council, it provided the foundation for the City’s ongoing ADA program and accessibility improvement 
efforts. 
 
City staff provided updates to City Council through Council Workshops in January 2025 and June 2026, regarding the City’s 
ADA program and development of the updated Transition Plan. The June 2026 Workshop included an update on progress since 
the original assessment, results of the updated accessibility evaluation, remaining barriers, and the proposed approach for 
prioritizing and addressing accessibility improvements. These discussions provided Council with an opportunity to receive 
information and provide feedback as staff continued development of the 2026 ADA Transition Plan.  
 
Following the June 2026 Council Workshop, staff continued to finalize the 2026 ADA Transition Plan, including updating 
barrier information, documenting progress, establishing priorities and implementation timeframes, and incorporating GIS-based 
tracking to support ongoing management of accessibility improvements. The updated Plan provides a Citywide framework for 
coordinating accessibility work across departments and integrating accessibility considerations into future projects, programs,

and funding opportunities. Formal Council adoption will establish the Plan as an official City policy framework and provide 
clear direction and support for continued implementation, prioritization, and accountability. 
 
Stakeholders involved in the Plan update have included City staff and departments responsible for facilities, parks, public 
rights-of-way, programs, services, communications, and other areas affecting accessibility. Residents and community 
organizations have also provided input regarding accessibility needs and priorities. Staff will continue to engage community 
members as the Plan is implemented and updated. 
  
Discussion 
  
Adoption of the Plan formalizes the City’s approach to accessibility and provides a consistent framework for prioritizing 
improvements based on factors such as safety, legal requirements, key destinations, pedestrian demand, connectivity, and 
available resources. It also establishes a process for tracking progress and maintaining current information to support future 
Plan updates. 
 
Council approval will demonstrate the City’s continued commitment to equal access and provide clear direction for 
implementation of the Plan. The Plan is intended to guide future planning, projects, and funding decisions rather than commit 
the City to completing all identified improvements within a specific timeframe or appropriating funds through this resolution. 
 
Implementation of the Plan will occur through existing City programs, projects, and future budget processes. Adoption of the 
resolution does not itself appropriate funds or create a new expenditure. Individual accessibility improvements identified in the 
Plan will be evaluated and prioritized through the City’s normal budgeting, capital planning, grant opportunities, and other 
available funding sources.

Executive Summary
The City of Arvada (“the City” or “Arvada”) is committed to ensuring that residents 
and visitors of all abilities can fully access its programs, services, facilities, and 
public infrastructure . In accordance with Title II of the Americans with Disabilities 
Act (ADA), this updated ADA Transition Plan identifies accessibility barriers, 
prioritizes their removal, and establishes a roadmap for the City’s continued 
progress .
This update reflects a multi-year effort that included:
• Self-Assessment (2020): A comprehensive evaluation of City-owned 
buildings, outdoor recreation facilities, pedestrian rights-of-way (including 
sidewalks and curb ramps), transportation facilities, and communication 
technologies and assets .
• Public Engagement: Community input gathered in 2021 and again in 2025 
and 2026 through public meetings and surveys . This engagement ensured that 
the Transition Plan reflects current accessibility experiences, evolving mobility 
needs, and the priority concerns of Arvada residents .
• Interdepartmental Coordination: Collaboration among the ADA Coordinator 
and staff from Infrastructure, Parks, Facilities, Communications, and other key 
divisions . This coordination helped identify barriers, develop implementation 
strategies, and integrate accessibility considerations into planning, budgeting, 
maintenance, and capital project delivery .
The 2020 self-assessment included 24 facilities, 113 outdoor recreation facilities, 
796 miles of public sidewalks, and 10,874 curb ramps . Remedies for barriers 
identified in the self-assessment range from simple, low-cost, maintenance and 
repairs, to extensive redesign and renovation of publicly accessed spaces . As of 
the end of 2025, there are 3,201 evaluated and addressed City-owned building 
attributes now compliant, with 1,570 remaining barriers to be addressed .

Sidewalks in the City’s right-of-way are essential to pedestrian transportation 
throughout Arvada. The City makes efforts to make its transportation inclusive of all 
modes by using the best practices available to establish design and construction 
standards to ensure that pedestrian walkways are accessible to and usable 
by people with disabilities . To that end, the City uses the Public Right-of-Way

The 2021 Transition Plan (“2021 Plan”) established a schedule for installing curb 
ramps at sidewalk–curb crossings . Long-term priorities were determined based on 
pedestrian traffic patterns and conditions that created access barriers for people 
with disabilities . Since the creation of the 2021 Plan the City has integrated curb 
ramp construction and upgrades into existing Capital Improvement Program (CIP) 
projects . As of January 2026, there were 2,239 of the 9,946 (22 .5 %) City owned and 
managed curb ramps that have passed inspection and are compliant . However, due 
to inaccurate and inconsistent past inspections, all curb ramps identified in the 2021 
Plan were labeled noncompliant . As a result, the City has begun comprehensive 
curb ramp re-inspections using uniform standards, which incorporated the 
PROWAG . The number of compliant curb ramps is expected to increase as these 
updated inspections are completed .
In addition to improving curb ramps, Transition Plan updates and related City 
efforts have focused on adding missing sidewalks, removing sidewalk barriers, 
completing pedestrian signal and control upgrades, relaunching the Sidewalk 
Assistance Program (previously the 50/50 Program), and maintaining the Arvada 
Curb Ramp Request Program (ACRRP) . This focus aligns with Arvada’s commitment 
to reasonable modification requests and responds directly to the 2025 community 
survey, emphasizing the need for more focus on pedestrian routes and services .
The 2026 update to the Transition Plan also identified gaps in the City’s digital and 
communication accessibility . In r esponse, the City developed, and is following, a 
separate Digital Accessibility Transition Plan (“DATP”), aligned with WCAG 2 .1 
Level AA standards . The DATP complements the ADA Transition Plan by addressing 
the accessibility of the City’s digital assets, such as websites, online services, 
digital documents, and electronic communications . The DATP establishes priorities, 
timelines, and responsibilities to ensure that accessibility extends beyond the 
physical environment and is integrated into all of the City’s digital assets .
Overall, updates to the Transition Plan reaffirm the City’s meaningful progress 
since 2020—both in the number of barriers corrected and in improved internal 
processes—while also identifying remaining challenges across facilities, outdoor 
recreation assets, pedestrian networks, and digital platforms . The update provides 
a strategic framework to guide future investment, prioritization, and monitoring, 
ensuring that accessibility remains an ongoing and integral responsibility across all 
City operations .
The findings within this updated Transition Plan demonstrate the City’s meaningful 
progress in removing accessibility barriers over the past several years, while 
also highlighting areas where continued investment and strategic planning are 
necessary . This 2026 update to the 2021 Transition Plan (available by request) 
provides a framework that prioritizes improvements with the greatest impact on 
community access, aligns City efforts with current ADA standards, and reinforces 
Arvada’s long-term commitment to creating an inclusive, equitable environment 
for people of all abilities . Through a phased implementation strategy and ongoing 
evaluation, the City will continue advancing accessibility across its facilities, 
infrastructure, programs, and services to meet the needs of its growing and diverse 
population. The Transition Plan is viewed as a living document and identified 
improvements will be contingent on the City’s budgetary resources from year to 
year, for the duration of the plan .

As part of Arvada’s strategic goals, the City strives to create an accessible and 
inclusive community that preserves and improves quality of life, and ensures a safe 
and thriving place for all community members . The City is committed to ongoing 
efforts to improve the City’s services, programs, facilities, and other infrastructure to 
ensure they are inclusive and accessible to everyone . 
The City contracted with ADA-consulting firm, Meeting the Challenge, Inc. (MTC), 
to assist with the City’s development of a transition plan . Beginning in May 2019, 
MTC conducted on-site reviews of as-is conditions for compliance with the ADA 
at the City’s buildings, parks, and pedestrian facilities in the City’s right-of-way . 
MTC then drafted the Transition Plan, which included data collected from 24 
facilities (buildings, including golf course clubhouses), 113 outdoor facilities (parks, 
sports complexes, equestrian centers, golf courses, etc .), and 796 miles of public 
sidewalks .
This 2026 update to the 2021 Transition Plan reflects the City’s broader equity and 
accessibility goals by ensuring that all residents—regardless of ability—can fully 
participate in community life . By prioritizing barrier removal and equitable access, 
the City reinforces its commitment to fairness, inclusion, and the creation of a 
community where everyone has the opportunity to thrive .
Transition Plan Need and Purpose
The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) is a civil rights law prohibiting 
discrimination against individuals on the basis of disability . Title II o f the ADA applies 
to state and local governments, requires that all programs, services, and activities 
be accessible to individuals with disabilities when they are viewed in their entirety, 
and requires the City to have a transition plan. This ADA Transition Plan satisfies 
Title II’s requirements and outlines the actions the City will take to ensure ADA 
compliance. Specifically, the plan:
Identifies physical and 
programmatic barriers 
to accessibility
Establishes a schedule 
for implementation
Describes methods to 
remove those barriers
Identifies the official 
responsible for oversight 
and coordination
The purpose o
f the Transition Plan is to identify those physical conditions in City 
facilities where the City must undertake structural changes to facilities to achieve 
program accessibility (i .e . barriers) . T he City’s primary goal under Title II of the ADA 
is to verify that its services, programs, and activities, when viewed in their entirety, 
are accessible to and usable by qualified individuals with disabilities. When physical 
barriers prevent or might prevent access to a program, the City must either remove 
such barriers, or provide access through other methods and means to ensure that 
individuals with disabilities can access those programs in the most integrated 
setting appropriate to the needs of the individual .
Many accessibility improvements also make the community easier to navigate and 
use for people of all ages and abilities, including older adults, parents with strollers, 
children, and people with temporary injuries .

Regulatory Requirement for a Transition Plan
As required by ADA Title II, which is enforced by the U .S . Departmen t of Justice 
(DOJ), the City conducted a self-evaluation of its programs, services, activities, 
facilities, and public right-of-way . Based on this evaluation, the City developed this 
2026 Transition Plan outlining the steps and methods needed to ensure compliance 
with ADA accessibility requirements .
The ADA Title II requires a transition plan when physical changes to facilities are 
necessary to achieve program accessibility . The purpose of Title II is not to require 
broad infrastructure renewal, but to ensure that individuals with disabilities can fully 
participate in community life .
While there is no mandate to update the Transition Plan at set intervals, ADA 
compliance remains a continuous responsibility . Arvada voluntarily updated its 2021 
draft to reflect ongoing efforts to meet and exceed accessibility standards. The 
original draft Transition Plan is available upon request . 
Transparency and public participation are essential to the process . The ADA requires 
public entities to provide opportunities for individuals with disabilities and their 
representatives to comment on the Transition Plan, and make the plan available for 
public inspection .
Required Transition Plan Components
The ADA establishes four key requirements that must be included in the Transition Plan:
• Identify physical barriers that limit access to programs, services, or facilities;
• Describe how each barrier will be corrected (e.g., renovations, policy changes);
• Provide a timeline with target dates for making changes; and
• Name of person or office responsible for carrying out the plan.
Transition Plan Updates
This Transition Plan, as well as any updates, is considered a living document . The 
City will publish annual progress reports, regularly update its inventory of barriers, 
track implementation of improvements, and continue to engage the public to 
validate access barriers, prioritizing the barrier removal or mitigating the existing 
barrier and the barrier outcomes . Adjustments will be made as conditions change, 
new facilities are added, or technologies evolve .

Engineering Standards
Title II regulations require that local governments with responsibility or authority 
over streets, roads, or walkways include in their transition plans a schedule for 
installing curb ramps or other sloped areas where pedestrian routes cross curbs . 
Priority must be given to walkways serving public facilities and places of public 
accommodation .
Regardless of whether formal standards exist, public entities have an obligation 
to ensure that pedestrian facilities in the public right-of-way (provided as part of 
the pedestrian transportation network) are accessible to and usable by qualified 
individuals with disabilities . The City therefore incorporates the Public Right-of-
Way Accessibility Guidelines (PROWAG) into its Engineering Code of Standards and 
Specifications. The PROWAG is published by the U.S. Access Board and is considered 
as a best practice by FHWA for constructing and altering pedestrian facilities in the 
City’s public right-of-way .

2020 Self Evaluation Findings
In 2020, the City, completed a self-evaluation of its services, programs, activities, 
and facilities on public property and within public rights-of-way . The inventory and 
findings from that evaluation reviewed:
• 24 City buildings
• 113 parks and outdoor recreation facilities
• Approximately 796 miles of sidewalks
• More than 9,900 curb ramps

In addition to facility accessibility, the City ensures program accessibility through a

accommodations . When barriers cannot be immediately removed due to feasibility 
or cost, they are documented and scheduled for future correction .
Implementation Strategy
Project Prioritization
Accessibility improvements are prioritized based on a combination of how often a 
facility is used, how serious the accessibility barrier is, and how improvements can 
be made efficiently and responsibly over time. Because resources are limited, the 
City focuses first on improvements that benefit the greatest number of people and 
address the most significant barriers.
• Level of public use
• Safety concerns
• Severity of the barrier
• Opportunities to coordinate with capital projects and planned renovations
• Availability of funding
For parks, athletic complexes, and golf courses, the City prioritized maintenance 
needs and improvements at entrances to improve access for visitors .
For pedestrian facilities, priority is generally given to locations with higher levels 
of pedestrian activity and community use . This may include areas near schools, 
civic and cultural destinations, transit stops, commercial areas, parks, and other 
key public facilities, while also considering factors such as connectivity, safety, 
community requests, and identified accessibility barriers throughout the public 
right-of-way .
9
Many accessibility improvements take time to plan and complete . Projects may 
require procurement, property acquisition, coordination with other construction, or 
additional funding . As a result, even high-priority projects may take several years 
to schedule . Projects already included in the City’s Capital Improvement Plan (CIP) 
generally move forward first and may be expanded to address accessibility needs 
identified in this Transition Plan.
Improving pedestrian facilities represents a significant long-term investment. The 
City is committed to making steady, measurable progress toward accessibility 
through a phased implementation approach . Consistent with Department of Justice 
(DOJ) guidance, the City will continue to identify and remove barriers over time, 
prioritizing improvements based on need, safety, and available resources . 
Project Phasing
Priority projects are generally organized into short-, mid-, and long-term 
implementation timeframes to support ongoing accessibility planning and 
integration with maintenance activities, capital improvement projects, 
redevelopment, and available funding opportunities .
Short term (Years 1–3)  
Focus on addressing identified accessibility barriers through ongoing maintenance, 
planned projects, programmatic improvements, and coordination opportunities . 
Efforts may prioritize barriers affecting access to public services, safety, 
connectivity, or community-identified needs.
Midterm (Years 3–10) 
Continue implementation of accessibility improvements through coordinated capital 
planning, infrastructure upgrades, and facility improvements . Work may include 
upgrades to pedestrian facilities, parks, trails, public buildings, and other City assets 
as opportunities arise .
Long term (Years 10–20) 
Advance broader systemwide accessibility improvements through long-range 
planning, major rehabilitation projects, redevelopment, and continued integration 
of accessibility and universal design principles into City programs, projects, and 
operations .
Project Financing
The ADA Coordinator will work with the Finance Department, the Infrastructure 
Department, Facilities Division, Vibrant Community and Neighborhoods, City leaders, 
City Council, and other departments to identify funding sources (e .g  .  general fund, 
grants, capital improvement program, developer contributions, holding accounts) for 
implementation and monitoring .
• Programmatic integration: The City will integrate accessibility considerations into 
planning, design, procurement, project delivery, maintenance, and community 
outreach to ensure that new barriers are not introduced, and existing ones are 
removed .

4 .1 Communications, In formation and

Communications Overview
The City will take appropriate steps to ensure that communications with members 
of the public with disabilities are as effective as communications with others. The 
City will provide appropriate auxiliary aids and services, when necessary and upon 
request, to afford individuals with disabilities an equal opportunity to participate in 
City programs, services, and activities .
The City will provide accessible signage at permanent rooms and spaces in City 
facilities and will ensure that information about the location and availability of 
accessible features is available to members of the public . When signage is replaced 
or new signage is installed, the new signage will comply with applicable accessibility 
standards .
Other important communication elements include installing Accessible Pedestrian 
Signals (APS) at intersections, as well as signs, pavement markings, and other 
traffic control devices used to provide advance warning and positive guidance in 
construction, maintenance, or utility work zones that affect sidewalks, crosswalks, 
or other pedestrian access routes . These elements are addressed in greater detail in 
the Pedestrian Facilities Section .
Communications Recent Improvements
In 2025, the City established an internal, multi-departmental Digital Accessibility 
Steering Committee (DASC), which focused on improving the accessibility of the 
City’s digital assets . The DASC partnered with an outside consultant, AccessAbility 
Officer, to complete the final version of the City’s Digital Accessibility Transition Plan 
(Version 2 .1), and inv entory, test, and remediate the City’s most widely used digital 
assets . Such digital assets included the City website, golf course website, Police 
Department website, GIS maps, AskArvada, and other similar digital assets . The 
City’s work to improve digital accessibility is ongoing in accordance with the DATP .
Digital Accessibility
The City of Arvada is committed to advancing the accessibility of its Information and 
Communication Technology (ICT) or digital assets . In support of this commitment, 
the City implemented the DATP to guide efforts to identify and remove accessibility 
barriers in ICT that is actively used by the public and City staff. 
12
The DATP aligns with Version 2 .1, L evel AA, of the Web Content Accessibility 
Guidelines (WCAG 2 .1 AA), which is the current digital standard established by Title II 
and the State of Colorado’s Office of Information Technology. The DATP is reviewed 
and updated annually .  Improving the accessibility of ICT enhances access to City 
programs, services, and activities for all users, including individuals with disabilities .
In accordance with the DATP, the City continues to develop and refine a system

4 .4 Pedestrian Facilities/Public 
Rights-of-Way
Pedestrian Facilities Overview
The City’s public rights-of-way include an extensive pedestrian network that 
supports safe and accessible travel throughout the community, including 
approximately 796 miles of sidewalk and 10,874 curb ramps . These facilities, along 
with crosswalks, pedestrian push buttons and signals, pedestrian refuge areas, 
transit access connections, and other related features, help provide access along 
and across public streets .
Pedestrian Recent Improvements
Arvada is committed to creating safer, more connected walking routes so people 
of all abilities can comfortably travel throughout the community . Common barriers 
within the pedestrian network include missing or noncompliant curb ramps, 
sidewalk displacements, spalling or deteriorated concrete, excessive slopes or cross 
slopes, gaps in the sidewalk network, and inaccessible pedestrian signal features .
The City continues to improve pedestrian accessibility through a combination of 
the City’s Pavement Program, as part Transportation related Capital Improvement 
16
Projects (CIP), developments, the Missing Sidewalk Program, and in addressing 
ADA service requests and grievances. Together, these efforts help remove barriers, 
improve pedestrian connectivity, and upgrade pedestrian facilities over time .
The Pavement Program supports pedestrian accessibility by replacing non-compliant 
curb ramps in coordination with street resurfacing and reconstruction work . As part 
of this work, the City also reviews related pedestrian crossing elements, including 
crosswalk striping, pedestrian push button locations, and, where applicable, pedestrian 
signal timing . Improvements to these elements are implemented as appropriate to 
improve crossing safety and support accessible pedestrian crossings . Over the past 
five years, the Pavement Program has contributed to over 90% of the curb ramp 
replacements/upgrades that have been completed .
Accessibility improvements are incorporated into all transportation CIP projects 
and, where applicable and feasible, adjacent areas to said projects . When streets, 
intersections, bridges, trails, or other public infrastructure are improved, the City 
evaluates affected pedestrian facilities within the project area and upgrades curb 
ramps, crossings, sidewalks (including sidewalk gaps), pedestrian push buttons, and 
related features to current ADA standards to the maximum extent feasible . These 
improvements are supported through a combination of project-specific funding 
as well as dedicated transportation holding accounts, including ADA Compliant 
Streets and Missing Sidewalks account, which are used exclusively to fund, support, 
or provide local matching funds for eligible pedestrian accessibility improvements 
within the public right-of-way . R ecently completed and ongoing CIP projects with

A2 . Pedestrian Facilities/Public Rights-of-Way 
The City has conducted a detailed accessibility evaluation of pedestrian facilities 
within its public right-of-way .  The results are listed as follows: Sidewalks in the 
City’s right-of-way are essential to pedestrian transportation throughout the 
City .  However, the existing sidewalks create a network that is not consistent 
across all locations within City limits . Title II regulations do not necessarily require 
the construction of new sidewalks where none currently exists . However, the 
regulations do require the City to include in its Transition Plan a schedule for 
providing curb ramps or other sloped areas where pedestrian facilities cross curbs . 
The regulations do not provide similar language specific to sidewalks between the 
corners. The City must, in its efforts to make its pedestrian transportation program 
inclusive, choose the best practices available to establish design and construction 
standards that result in pedestrian walkways that are accessible to and usable by 
people with disabilities . For this reason, the City selected the PROWAG as its best 
practices for pedestrian facilities in the City’s right-of-way .  Most existing residential 
neighborhoods and all new residential developments have sidewalks . Of the 32,001 
findings in MTC’s data for pedestrian facilities, 26,601 (83 percent) were located 
within three blocks of an ADA-covered entity, and 14,724 (46 percent) were located 
within one block of a facility of an ADA-covered entity .  The City, in establishing its 
scope and priorities for pedestrian facilities, considered a variety of factors that 
ultimately focus on program demand by location . As the ADA regulations invoke 
priority based on walkways that serve entities covered by the ADA, the City’s data 
collection, analysis, and prioritization of remediation needs have been assessed 
based on the nearness of a given pedestrian asset to a covered entity in the 
following order of priority: 
1. State and local government offices and facilities
2 . Transportation 
3 . Places of public accommodation 
4 .  Employers 
5 .  Walkways serving other areas
In addition, where it is readily apparent that sidewalk width (i .e  . , less than 36 
inches) is insufficient to provide minimum accessibility, other sidewalk attributes 
(e .g  . , slopes, vertical discontinuities, etc  . ) will be deemed nonessential  .  In these 
locations, all accessibility requirements will be incorporated when new sidewalk 
replaces the existing sidewalk . Incremental repairs to sidewalks in these locations, 
prior to major replacement projects, will necessarily be limited to specific public 
requests for accessibility improvement or hazard removal . When such segments of 
sidewalk are in areas serving entities covered by the ADA, segment replacement 
will be prioritized based on the location, not by the severity of deficiencies. This 
model assumes that minor deficiencies in heavily traveled sidewalks have a greater 
probability of presenting barriers to program access than major deficiencies 
26
in seldom-traveled sidewalks . The City will disregard this assumption in those 
instances when the public specifically requests removal of a barrier to access in an 
otherwise low priority location .
