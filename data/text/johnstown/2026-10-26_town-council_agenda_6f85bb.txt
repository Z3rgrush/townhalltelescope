Executive Summary 
Purpose of the Housing Needs Assessment 
The Colorado Department of Local Affairs (DOLA) supported Johnstown’s Housing Needs 
Assessment (HNA) through a Housing Planning Grant that also supports ongoing 
Comprehensive Plan Elements and the Housing Action Plan (HAP) to follow. This HNA aims to 
develop an understanding of housing needs and characteristics of that need in Johnstown in 
accordance with Colorado Revised Statutes. It draws insights from available data that will help 
inform future housing policies, land use strategies, and housing programs to address 
affordability and supply gaps. 
The summaries below provide an overview of key population, demographic, economic, and 
housing findings from the HNA process. 
What Does the Data Say? 
• Johnstown’s population is growing. The town’s population grew 45 percent between 
2014 and 2024, and growth projections anticipate that the number of households in 
Johnstown today will more than double by 2050. New residents will drive the need for 
more housing. 
• Approximately 79 percent of the housing stock in Johnstown in 2024 was single-unit 
detached homes, with a near identical portion of households that own their home. 
However, Johnstown has seen noticeable increases in multi-unit development, with 
almost 2,000 units built between 2015 and 2026. 
• About 27% of households in Johnstown experience “cost burden.” This means that 
those households spend 30% or more of their income on housing. This can include 
rent, mortgage payments, utilities, and other fees.  
• More than 40% of renters in Johnstown today are cost-burdened, in addition to 
about 20% of homeowners. A survey for this project reflected that a lack of 
affordability represented Johnstown’s largest housing challenge.

1. Introduction 
Study Purpose and Context 
The Town of Johnstown (Town) undertook this Housing Needs Assessment (HNA) to develop 
an understanding of key local housing needs. Johnstown has experienced substantial growth 
over the past 20 years, with population increasing by more than 100 percent since 2006. This 
growth has led to new considerations and potential strategies for meeting housing needs. 
Johnstown is primarily situated in Weld County, with its northwest area as well as some of its 
Growth Management Area crossing into Larimer County. The core of the Town’s activity 
focuses around its downtown located north of Colorado State Highway 60 on Parish Avenue, 
with recent development spreading northwest from that area and attracting larger mixed-use 
and retail developments. These changes have also contributed to rising local employment. In 
recent planning processes, the Town has identified a need for more diverse housing types 
better aligned with local income as it continues to experience residential growth. With that 
continued growth, current residents face challenges in accessing housing that they can afford. 
As the Town grows, these challenges may also reflect similar challenges for those seeking to 
become residents of Johnstown.  
State and Local Context 
The Colorado Department of Local Affairs (DOLA) awarded Johnstown a Housing Planning 
Grant on March 9, 2026 to produce an HNA as well as Housing Action Plan (HAP). This grant 
also included addressing a required Strategic Growth Element (SGE) and Water Supply 
Element (WSE) in updating the Town’s Comprehensive Plan. Per Colorado Revised Statutes 
(C.R.S.) § 24-32-3701, certain local jurisdictions must complete a State-compliant HNA by 
December 31, 2026, and at least every six years thereafter. This HNA intends to follow both 
the State guidance for the development of HNAs as required by C.R.S. § 24-32-3701 and the 
requirements of the Housing Planning Grant. 
The findings of the HNA create a foundation for strategies and action through the HAP, which 
will establish policies and programs to meet housing needs specific to Johnstown and meet 
the new statutory requirements in C.R.S. § 24-32-3705. This will include a greater focus on 
strategies to mitigate displacement risk, expanding the view of standard and long-term 
affordability strategies, considerations for housing near transit, identification of opportunities 
for intergovernmental coordination, and a deeper dive into the impacts of zoning and 
housing development activity. 
Regional Context 
Johnstown participates in regional housing affordability discussions through the NoCo 
Foundation. The Larimer County Regional Housing Needs Assessment and Weld County 
Housing Needs Assessment inform this plan through regional context, but do not identify the 
specific housing needs for Johnstown.

2. Community Engagement  
Engagement Structure and Approach 
Community engagement for this HNA process involved attendance at six different in-person 
events from May 2026 through July 2026, an online survey that ran from early June through 
early August, and focus groups in mid-July with housing developers and advocates. Some of 
the in-person events represent large, annual gatherings, while others reflected efforts to go to 
where community members regularly spend time. Engagement efforts focused on what types 
of housing participants would like to see more of in Johnstown and what housing affordability 
meant to them. 
The in-person events included the following: 
¨ Cinco de Mayo Fiesta: May 2nd 
¨ BBQ Day: June 6th  
¨ Movie in the Park: June 19th  
¨ YMCA Tabling: July 8th  
¨ Senior Center Tabling: July 8th  
¨ Neighborhood Celebration: July 24th  
These “pop-up” style events received feedback from various parts of the community by 
meeting people at existing community gatherings to frame local housing perspectives. Each 
interaction included information and activities written in both English and Spanish and 
encouraged participation through material giveaways.  
The survey offered a flexible digital format for participation, also offered in both Spanish and 
English. The Town advertised the survey in its ongoing newsletter, social media platforms, at 
the engagement events, and through word of mouth. A total of 119 people provided survey 
responses, with 113 of them fully completing it. 
Two virtual stakeholder focus groups solicited input from housing developers, housing 
providers, and other housing resource groups, with one geared towards market-rate 
development and the other towards affordable housing. Representatives from 17 local and 
regional organizations joined these focus groups. 
This chapter provides a summary of engagement information gathered across the three main 
opportunities, with Appendix A containing a complete engagement report.

Engagement Findings 
Community and stakeholder participation pointed to affordability as 
Johnstown’s central housing challenge. 
Residents, survey respondents, and housing resource providers consistently identified rising 
home prices, interest rates, property taxes, metro district taxes and fees, utilities, and other 
ongoing costs as elements that make it increasingly difficult for people to stay in or move to 
Johnstown. Exhibit 1 shows that through the online survey, respondents identified 
affordability as Johnstown’s largest housing challenge (33%). 
Exhibit 1. Housing Challenges and Concerns, Johnstown HNA Survey Participants 
 
Source: Johnstown HNA Survey 
In-person engagement echoed similar shares of opinion, with housing located far from 
transit, jobs, or services as the second most prevalent concern for residents. Additionally, a 
lack of multi-generational housing, housing preservation, and limited supply emerged as 
other notable challenges. 
The collective and rising costs of housing in Johnstown are driving 
affordability concerns. 
Engagement respondents spoke to the bundle of housing costs (including mortgage or rent, 
utilities, property taxes, HOA dues, and metro district taxes and fees combined) as an 
overwhelming contributor to barriers towards housing affordability. Households who own their 
home may pay for each component individually, while households who rent their home may 
pay for some of those elements collectively through increases in monthly rent. Participants 
identified some of these costs as indirect housing costs, feeling as though everything has 
added up in a way that makes housing that might otherwise be affordable to them no longer 
so.

residents in difficult financial situations to be able to absorb those costs.

For developers, Town requirements for upfront water dedication rather than at the time of 
building permit means that they have significant financial resources tied up in housing that 
may not receive building permit approvals for years. Other water-related development

Advocates also spoke to a lack of options for people to move into communities that meet their 
needs. Older adults cannot find options to downsize their housing, while buyers in newer 
developments may feel isolated and without connections to a close-knit community. They 
spoke to some general misunderstandings about local housing availability, recognizing 
confusion about denser housing options has led to some community resistance to 
development.

3. Demographic Trends 
Geographies and Planning Context 
This section provides a profile of households and residents in Johnstown. It intends to develop 
a deeper understanding of demographic characteristics to better describe local housing 
needs. Household and population changes reflect the primary drivers of demand for housing, 
with the rate of population growth as well as key household characteristics heavily influencing 
specific housing types that can best support a local context. 
Johnstown is incorporated in both Weld and Larimer Counties, with its core surrounding the 
downtown at the east and connecting to the I-25 corridor to the west. Comparative towns 
included in this analysis (Berthoud, Mead, Milliken, and Windsor) have similar characteristics 
as communities along the North Front Range corridor, accommodating fast population growth 
over the past decade. This chapter includes sociodemographic information from these two 
counties and four municipalities to provide comparative geographies.

Johnstown has a vacancy rate of 4.2 percent, less than both Larimer 
and Weld Counties. 
The balance of vacant units in a community speaks to local availability of housing and 
demand pressures. Typically, a “healthy” vacancy rate falls between five and seven 
percent. When a community has more vacant units than people looking for a home, the 
additional units available can help reduce these pressures. More vacancies can result in 
lower rents and housing costs, contributing to a more affordable housing stock. Low vacancy  
rates often reflect a high local demand for housing without enough units available for people 
looking for a home. This constrained availability of housing can lead to higher rents and 
housing prices, decreasing relative affordability levels while disincentivizing owners of rental 
properties to update units. Low vacancies also represent limited options, limiting households 
from finding housing that best fits their financial means and needs. 
Johnstown’s vacancy rate is roughly one-half that of Larimer County (9.1%) and almost one 
percent lower than Weld County (5%). Berthoud (7.4%) and Windsor (8.1%) both have 
vacancy rates above seven percent, while Milliken and Mead have vacancy rates below three 
percent. Shown in Exhibit 31, this vacancy rate would suggest a somewhat tight housing 
market in Johnstown as well as more constraints finding a home than some surrounding 
communities like Berthoud and Windsor.

Regulated Affordable Housing in Johnstown  
Regulated affordable housing describes properties that have legal mechanisms in place that 
require housing to be available only to residents who earn certain levels of the Area Median 
Income (AMI). This type of housing is a vital component of affordable housing supply in a 
community because market rate housing is often out of reach for many residents. Naturally 
occurring affordable housing (NOAH) is not regulated through affordable housing programs, 
but is attainable for households with lower incomes, usually due to the age, condition, 
location, or housing type of the unit, which this analysis reviews in the next section. 
Regulated affordable housing is sometimes referred to as government-assisted housing, 
speaking to how public funds often support the production and maintenance of these 
developments. Affordability restrictions vary between properties in both financial support and 
length of time a property is subject to affordability standards.

Naturally Occurring Affordable Housing (NOAH) 
NOAH provides units affordable to lower-income households without any form of public 
subsidy. This type of housing does not have price limitations for rental or ownership 
opportunities. Typically, this type of housing is older, smaller, and/or of lower quality with 
fewer amenities, older appliances, or appears older than newer, more recently updated units 
(which makes them more attainable for households earning below 100% of AMI). Often, these 
properties have some deferred maintenance or capital needs that the owner has not 
addressed. Because a general lack of enough regulated affordable housing exists across the 
country, NOAH represents an important part of the housing stock in a community.  
While NOAH units play a critical role in providing affordable options, they also have inherent 
vulnerability to rent increases, reinvestment pressures, or redevelopment, which can lead to 
the displacement of lower-income residents. Maintaining and preserving this stock often 
emerges as an important housing policy goal, as it represents a significant share of the 
naturally affordable rental housing available in most communities. 
Johnstown has two manufactured home parks, with one operating as a 
resident-owned cooperative.  
Manufactured home parks are a source of naturally occurring affordable housing, but they 
face unique risks that other housing types do not. Residents are particularly vulnerable to 
sudden rent increases, land sales, and redevelopment pressures, which can result in 
displacement. Furthermore, inadequate maintenance by park owners or managers can lead to 
deteriorating infrastructure and possibly unsafe living conditions. 
There are approximately 66 lots across the two parks in Johnstown,3 providing sources of 
naturally occurring affordable housing near the central downtown area. Paradise Village is a 
resident-owned cooperative, generally reflecting an effort to ensure stability for park residents. 
As of June 2026, neither park was for sale.

7. Housing Challenges 
The residents of Johnstown face a range of housing conditions and challenges in the local 
landscape. Additionally, integrating economic, demographic, and housing insights in this 
chapter helps to provide an overview of specific populations at risk of displacement in 
Johnstown. Households at risk of displacement are vulnerable to losing their place of living 
and face distinct barriers to finding other housing that fits their needs. 
Housing Cost Burden 
State and federal sources that address affordable housing define households spending more 
than 30 percent of gross annual income on housing as “housing cost-burdened.” When a 
household spends more than 30 percent of its income on housing costs (e.g., rent/mortgage, 
utilities, insurance, property taxes, etc.), households have less disposable income for other 
necessities like health care, food, and clothing. The share of households experiencing housing 
cost burden may rise when housing costs increase faster than household income. A 
household that spends more than 50 percent of its income on housing costs experiences 
severe cost burden. 
In the event of unexpected circumstances such as the loss of employment or serious health 
problems, lower-income households with a burdensome housing cost have a higher likelihood 
of finding themselves unhoused or forced to “double up” with other households (e.g. move in 
with friends, family, or roommates). While homeowners with a housing cost burden have the 
option of selling their homes to downsize or become renters, households that rent face more 
vulnerability and constant changes in the housing market. 
Cost burden has decreased for homeowners but increased for renters in 
Johnstown. 
The overall share of all households (including homeowners and renters) who experienced 
some form of cost burden remained even at 27 percent between 2014 and 2024. During this 
period, there was a slight shift with more of these households experiencing severe cost 
(paying 50% or more of their income towards housing) as shown in Exhibit 43.

Homelessness 
The Northern Colorado Continuum of Care (CoC) serves Larimer and Weld Counties, officially 
recognized by HUD in January 2020. A CoC provides collaborative support for households 
and individuals experiencing local homelessness, coordinating emergency, transitional, and 
permanent housing opportunities among providers with capacity. A CoC also supports 
prevention and diversion strategies, outreach and engagement, and manages a Homeless 
Management Information System (HMIS), a local data source for monitoring how the region 
has supported individuals experiencing homelessness. 
The Colorado Homelessness Management Information System defines different experiences of 
homelessness as such: 
• Sheltered: persons staying in emergency shelters, transitional housing, and safe havens. 
• Unsheltered: persons living in tents, cars, and other places not meant for human habitation. 
• Chronic: persons who have experienced long-term, persistent homelessness, often due to a 
lack of access to healthcare, affordable housing, and supportive services.

Displacement Risk 
Housing displacement happens when someone is no longer able to remain in their 
community due to rising costs, loss of housing, or changing conditions that make it difficult to 
stay where they are. Although it is not possible to fully predict where displacement will 
happen, identifying populations at risk of experiencing displacement can help identify those 
who may need additional support in finding or maintaining their housing.  
Direct displacement pressures include redevelopment, eminent domain, lease non-renewals, 
evictions, and other pressures that have the immediate effect of forcing residents to move. 
Economic displacement pressures include rising rents, loss of income, and other factors that 
affect housing affordability. 
Cultural displacement pressures include neighborhood change, loss of cultural assets, 
language barriers, and other conditions that affect a sense of belonging to a place and a 
community. 
Displacement can also occur when housing market conditions change in communities with 
elevated shares of populations at risk of displacement. This analysis provides an overview of 
some populations in Johnstown who may face displacement: 
¨ Of the estimated 6,525 households in Johnstown in 2022, 2,015 earned less than 80 
percent of AMI ($79,200 for a family of four in Weld County in 2022).9 HUD 
describes these households as low-income. 
¨ Approximately 680 renter households experienced cost burden in 2024. This count 
represents roughly 43 percent of the renter households in Johnstown, who are more 
vulnerable to increases in their housing costs than households that own their home 
when rents increase.  
¨ Of the 66 manufactured home park lots in town, 26 of them are not a part of a 
resident-owned cooperative. Leasing space in these lots does not have the same 
protections associated as those within a cooperative. An outside park owner could sell 
or redevelop the park for other purposes. Proximity to Johnstown’s downtown and an 
industrial employment base could encourage that transition.  
¨ There are approximately 200 spaces available for Recreational Vehicle (RV) parking 
in two locations in Johnstown. While not all those who stay are long-term residents, 
these spaces provide more affordable places to park an RV and live in the town but 
could be vulnerable to external conditions like property ownership changes.

often navigate stability pressures, often without the ability to obtain long-term leases that 
lock-in current prices. Changing rental prices can strain budgets if household incomes do not 
increase at the same pace.  
A key challenge many low-income households face (particularly in communities like 
Johnstown with limited inventories of regulated affordable housing) lies in competition for 
naturally occurring affordable housing from higher-income households. In many instances, a 
higher-income household may choose a less expensive housing option if available and it 
meets household needs. Other households may already have secured their home, choosing to 
remain even as their household income increases. Low-income households do not have the 
same ability to compete upward for housing without becoming housing cost burdened. Low-
income households have clear disadvantages in competing for lower-cost housing given entry 
costs (down payment for owner units, advance rent, and deposits for rental units) and 
required credit reports. 
Aging households and households with at least one member that has a disability also present 
a population vulnerable to housing displacement. This population often has a fixed income 
and cannot afford more expensive housing than their current home. Additionally, the need for 
ground-level entry, visitable units, and other specific features can reduce available housing 
options.

support. Uncertain housing support funding landscapes may strain the capacity of the 
authority to provide an adequate level of care to those living in that space. Since that 
population crosses over individuals earning less than 50 percent of AMI, those residents 
would struggle to find housing that meets their financial and accessibility needs if displaced 
from that building.

Community engagement survey results indicate that displacement pressure extends beyond 
rent and home prices. Residents frequently identified other factors like property taxes, 
metropolitan district charges, utilities, insurance, HOA fees, and transportation costs as factors

affect older adults with fixed-income, lower-income homeowners, renters facing cost increases

9. Housing Development 
Challenges and Opportunities 
This chapter synthesizes findings from the Johnstown Housing Needs Assessment focus 
groups from July 2026 and community engagement, supplemented by findings from other 
previous Town planning processes. 
Finance Challenges and Development Costs 
Building new housing relies on capital resources to fund development for design, 
construction, and borrowing costs. Both market-rate and income-specific housing developers 
have identified affordability gaps in providing housing in Johnstown. Additionally, elements of 
the supply and demand landscapes have led to a lack of diverse housing options. 
Development costs have continued increasing in recent years. 
Rising development costs in Johnstown reflect national trends for key factors like materials, 
land acquisition, and infrastructure that contribute to overall higher housing construction 
costs. One developer reflected that they cannot build a house that will cost less than 
$450,000 at this time, echoing a NoCo Foundation report that estimated the cost of building 
a single-unit home regionally to have risen by more than 50 percent since 2015.12 Additional 
local changes in the cost of building include development fees and changes in building code 
requirements over the past five years.  
Nationwide, elevated interest rates have also contributed to increasing construction loans and 
carrying costs at a larger scale, with at least one developer trying to increase sales by offering 
temporary interest rate buydowns. This buydown program offering lower interest rates for a 
defined period saw more transactions, but also noticeable losses in revenue. 
Affordable housing developers reflected that state and federal finance support are not 
sufficient to deliver regulated housing locally. In addition to those funding resources, 
developers often rely on local support through subsidies, fee waivers, and incentives to 
provide housing that serves residents earning below the area median income. The Columbine 
Complex which serves some residents receiving Rural Development Vouchers is the only 
housing in Johnstown that receives federal support for income-specific housing. 
Focus group participants also expressed that building an ADU has felt out of reach for 
homeowners, with the cost of construction for a detached unit sometimes representing more 
than half the cost of building a new standalone home and requiring additional water tap fees.

Regulatory Challenges 
Johnstown adopted a new land use and development code in 2023, responding to the 2021 
Comprehensive Plan’s outlined goals to facilitate more diverse housing options. The Town has 
also adopted the 2024 International Building Code (IBC), which updated residential and 
energy requirements in construction. While new codes increase construction costs, the 
updated codes intended to support community goals, although other unrelated challenges, 
largely related to administering water delivery, have created some development barriers. 
Water dedication requirements contribute to burdensome carrying costs 
for developers. 
Focus group participants noted that Johnstown’s requirement for pre-development water 
dedication is a challenge in development, also presenting limitations for supporting regulated 
affordable housing. With water dedication needed before recording a plat, rather than at the 
time of building permit, developers take on substantial added costs before receiving any 
income. This requirement helps the Town to understand guaranteed water supply to make 
sure that future use does not strain its current capacity or create confusing accountability 
structures once individual property owners purchase new homes, with this concern palpable 
during times of drought. However, associated carrying costs can be even more difficult for 
affordable housing developers that operate on slim margins.  
Additionally, developers expressed a feeling that water dedication expectations exceeded 
actual use. Since Johnstown uses gross demand projections rather than consumptive data 
projections, they perceive increased costs in the amount developers need to dedicate to new 
housing. Those dedications also involve water rights allocated to temporary landscaping 
establishment that does not have flexibility to extend to housing. One developer identified

Water and sewer services are both central to housing development. Johnstown is currently

Land use and building permit processes have contributed to 
development challenges. 
Focus group participants expressed frustration in navigating review processes that have 
included conflicting feedback and interjurisdictional code conflicts. One developer noted that 
the Town increased the length of its initial round of review with good intentions: to compile a 
comprehensive list of comments for the applicant to address and avoid excessive rounds of 
review and revision overall. This developer still experienced shifts in direction on subsequent 
submittals, leading to additional soft costs for design changes and project carrying costs from 
a longer timeline. One advocate spoke to how internal disconnects in permitting may reduce 
the efficacy of overall Town initiatives to make housing more affordable. One developer 
assigned the increase in cost to build a single-unit home due to the adoption of the 2024 IBC 
at approximately $20,000. This jump, combined with other process related challenges, has 
contributed to more barriers in development. 
Individually negotiated Planned Unit Developments regulate housing 
options in most of Johnstown. 
Johnstown updated its Land Use and Development Code in 2023. Systematic changes allowed 
for more housing options in more zoning districts. However, the reach of these changes is 
limited with much of Johnstown carrying custom Planned Unit Development zoning. 
The Housing Action Plan following the HNA will further assess the impact of zoning and density 
on the production of needed housing types in Johnstown. 
Community Sentiment 
Previous plans informed by engagement identified a low vacancy rate 
and collective interest in housing that served various income levels. 
The 2021 Johnstown Area Comprehensive Plan included potential solutions for facilitating 
population growth that had exceeded 100 percent between 2006 and 2020 within the Town’s 
Growth Management Area. It identified a vacancy rate below one percent (with this analysis 
reporting a 2024 update using SDO data rather than ACS at 4.2%), indicating little 
opportunity for the housing stock to accommodate that growth. With feedback from the 
community, the plan oriented its attention around Activity Centers, key locations that could

10. Current and Projected 
Housing Need 
This chapter reports both current and future housing needs over the next 10 years, 
considering where gaps exist today and where needs have expectations to grow. Consistent 
with the State’s guidance on HNAs, this chapter presents estimates of current housing needs 
(catch-up) and future housing needs (keep-up). Housing need estimates describe need by 
income level and tenure (owner/renter). The estimates address needs across housing types, 
ranging from extremely low-income and vulnerable populations through middle- and upper-
income households.  
The analysis provides a breakdown of: 
¨ Underproduction/Current Need (“Catch-Up”): Underproduction occurs when there 
are too few housing units to meet demand, leading to scarcity, higher prices, and 
greater cost burden, especially for low-income households. Subsequent low vacancy 
rates can limit the ability of households to find suitable and accessible housing 
options. 
¨ Future Need (“Keep Up”): This category describes the total housing units needed to 
accommodate the expected population by 2036 (a 10-year planning period).
