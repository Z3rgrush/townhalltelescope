Executive Summary  
In 2026, Huerfano County created their first Community Wildfire Protection Plan (CWPP) to take advantage of 
recent advances in fire science and address changes to fire risk, home construction, and other characteristics of 
the community. The CWPP defines ro les and responsibilities for implementation, sets priorities, 
and identifies locations for fuel reduction, while also providing a wildfire risk analysis, outlining mitigation 
activity priorities, and offering implementation recommendations.  
  
Located in southeastern Colorado, Huerfano County encompasses approximately 1,591 square miles of land, 
which consists of forests in the western and southern portion of the county and grass, prairie, and rangeland 
within the northeastern region. The elevation ranges from 4,860 feet to 14,345 feet above sea level, which allows 
for a diversity of temperatures and large amounts of wind and seasonal variations. As of 2022, the population is 
roughly 7,100, with a majority residing in Walsenburg. Three fire prote ction districts (FPDs) cover the county: 
the Huerfano County Fire District, the Upper Huerfano Fire Protection District, and the La Veta Fire Protection 
District.   
 
  
Map of Huerfano County, Colorado  
 
 
  
Page 26 of 270
 
9 
 
Historically, Huerfano County has been influenced by wildfire activity. Approximately 14 percent of the county is 
composed of piñon pine ( Pinus edulis ) and juniper (often referred to as piñon -juniper), specifically in the 
southwest portion of the county in areas of transitioning elevation, from 4,000 to 8,000 feet (Colorado Natural 
Heritage Program, 2018). These forests burn infrequently at moderate- to high- (stand replacing) severity due to 
resin content and encroaching conifers and grass ecosystems, and are generally not fire -resistant, which allows 
them to act as a contiguous fuel source between ground fuel and canopy (CFRI, 2021; McIntosh et al., 2023).   
  
This CWPP is designed as a strategic guide for wildfire preparedness, mitigation, response, and recovery. While 
it does not mandate specific actions for any entity, it serves as a framework for collaborative community efforts 
to reduce wildfire risks, enhance emergency preparedness, and improve resilience. The plan aligns with national 
strategies such as the Healthy Forests Restoration Act and the National Cohesive Wildland Fire Management 
Strategy, state strategies such as the 2020 Colorado Forest Action Plan  with local strategies such as the 2024 
Huerfano County Multi-Hazard Mitigation Plan, the 2017 Huerfano County Community Planning Assistance for 
Wildfire (CPAW), and the 2016 La Veta CWPP.   
  
The mission was to create a CWPP for Huerfano County that will prepare and decrease risk for the people, 
property, and resources of Huerfano County from wildfire through education, prevention, mitigation, and 
collaboration. The CWPP aims to identify action s and strategies Huerfano County stakeholders can pursue and 
implement to enhance community and landscape resilience and contribute positively to the Huerfano County way 
of life.    
  
Stronger wildfire resilience in Huerfano County comes by building a self -resilient community. Key actions 
include maintaining and improving defensible space around homes, improving access 
routes, modifying vegetation, including mitigating noxious weeds that exacerbate wildfire behavior, thinning 
along road edges, and suggestions for improving emergency communication systems. The CWPP also provides a 
county-wide assessment of past and ongoing initiatives, identifies stra tegic treatment zones, and highlights 
priority expansion areas where future investments can deliver the greatest benefit to the community.

county was divided into 6 plan units  for comparing relative risk  across the county and making specific

This document is a tool for Huerfano County, land managers, communities,

projects that will make the county a safer and more resilient community to wildfire. The objectives of this project 
are to: 
 
• Produce a living, actionable CWPP based on analyses and community values across the county. 
• Provide recommendations  for individuals, communities, and agencies  for reducing fire hazards, 
hardening homes, and increasing evacuation safety. 
• Set the stage for planning and implementation by Huerfano County, agency partners, HOAs, and residents 
to mitigate hazards and promote community preparedness with clear, strategic, and prioritized projects 
for all levels of implementation. 
• Provide information on individual and community wildfire history and risk, actions that can be taken to

• Engage community members during the CWPP process to ensure local needs and concerns are addressed.

2. Huerfano County, Colorado: Background 
2.a. General Description  
In southeastern Colorado, where the plains meet the Sangre de Cristo Mountain Range, lies Huerfano County. The 
county covers 1,591 square miles with elevations ranging from 4,860 feet to 14,345 feet above sea level at the 
4th highest peak in Colorado, Blanca Peak . The county is home to approximately 7,063 residents as of 2023. Of 
these residents, approximately 44% are over the age of 60, 18% live below the poverty line, and 77% of residents 
occupy and own their home. (U.S. Census Bureau, 2023). In Huerfano County, around 9% of the residences occupy 
manufactured homes, in Walsenburg, manufactured homes in the piñon-juniper woodlands make up about 6% 
of resident housing, and in the town of La Veta, this amount increases to 19%, (Baker, M. International, 2024). 
Over the past decade, Huerfano County's population has experienced notable shifts. In 2010, the county recorded 
6,948 residents, which declined to 6,502 by 2015 before rebounding to 7,096 in 202 2 (Department of Local 
Affairs, n.d.). The City of Walsenburg, the county seat, comprises half of the county’s total population, with many 
living in La Veta and the remaining residents residing in unincorporated areas. The county is made up of three 
major fire protection districts (FPDs): the Huerfano County Fire District, the Upper Huerfano Fire Protection 
District, and the La Veta Fire Protection District.  
Community Values & Historic Structures 
In addition to historic resources, Huerfano County contains essential infrastructure and community assets 
(Figure 2.a.1). The CWPP Advisory Committee was asked to identify HVRAs (high-value resources and assets) in 
their specific jurisdictions . Assets mentioned included the Spanish Peaks Veterans Community Living Center, 
infrastructure for communications with the Huerfano County Communications Center being noted specifically, 
the Spanish Peak Regional Health Center, wetlands, and sewage treatment and water purification facilities. Water 
is one of the most critical infrastructure concerns in a county that is very arid and essential for agriculture, 
wildfire mitigation, and residential use . Improvements are underway to upgrade current stream -flow gauges, 
alluvial monitoring wells, and facilities for water -rights administration (HCWCD, n.d.) . Each municipality in 
Huerfano County uses the Cucharas river for drinking water and each have their own water purification and 
sewage treatment facilities. HCWCD actively engages in county -wide partnerships and collaborates with the 
Arkansas River Watershed Collaborative (ARWC), as well as the Arkansas Basin Roundtable (ABRT) (Wildfire 
Planning International, LLC et al., 2017). 
The county has many historic structures including ranches, homesteads, schools, resorts, private residences, and 
multiple museums. The Francisco Fort Museum, located in the historic Francisco Plaza in La Veta, is a good 
example of early structures and is the oldest and last surviving adobe fort in Colorado (Spanish Peaks Country, 
2023).  
Safety and security infrastructure in the county include the Huerfano County Sheriff’s Office (HCSO) a long with 
six strategically placed fire stations, and communication towers  dispersed throughout the county as seen in  
Figure 2.a.1 below.

Geography 
Today, Walsenburg stands as the county's seat and central hub, located at the crossroads of major highways - I-
25, US 160, and State Highways 10 and 69 (Baker, M. International, 2024). Huerfano County is bordered by Custer 
County to the north, Pueblo County to the northeast, Las Animas County to the east, Costilla County to the 
southwest, Alamosa County to the west, and Saguache County to the northwest.  
The county’s headwaters are fed by snowmelt and seasonal precipitation in the eastern slopes of the Sangre de 
Cristo and Spanish Peaks ranges along with the Huerfano River, the Cucharas River, their tributaries, and ground 
water which are all a part of the  Arkansas River Basin. The Huerfano County Water Conservancy District 
(HCWCD) serves both local and regional interests by managing the county’s water resources and its boundaries 
largely align with those of Huerfano County, with minor deviations along the eastern edge where some areas fall 
outside the district (Wildfire Planning International, LLC et al., 2017).  
Climate 
The dramatic changes in slope and elevation around Huerfano County allow for a diversity of temperatures , 
copious amounts of wind, and seasonal variations, ranging from warm and sunny in the summer to severe snow 
and wind in the winter. The consistent wind, specifically north of Walsenburg, allow Huerfano County to be home 
to Colorado’s largest and highest producing wind farm since 2013 which supports alternative and sustainable 
energy. The Huerfano Wind Farm creates about “4% of San Isabel E lectric Association (SIEA) annual energy 
needs” serving about one-third of its members (Distributed Wind Energy Association, 2025). 
Vegetation and Wildlife 
With the Sangre de Cristo Mountains range on the western boundary of the county, the Spanish Peaks on the 
county’s southern border, and the Greenhorn Mountain on the northern border, Huerfano County offers a variety 
of landforms, vegetation, and climatic c onditions due mainly to the wide elevation range (about 6,000 –14,000 
feet). The irregular terrain up high is shaped by Spruce -fir forests (Engelmann spruce and subalpine fir) and 
alpine tundra with grasses, and sedges adapted to cold, windy conditions.  Piñon-juniper woodlands are 
widespread and mark the transition into lower elevations (Colorado Natural Heritage Program, 2018), especially 
in the southwestern parts of the county which are made up of Piñon pine ( Pinus edulis ) and Utah juniper 
(Juniperus osteosperma ), mixed with Gambel oak and mountain mahogany. Species of sagebrush like big 
sagebrush (Artemisia tridentata), blue grama grass ( Bouteloua gracilis), and western wheatgrass are drought -
tolerant and found lower in elevation thriving in semi -arid areas ( Figure 2.a.3).  Historically, these grasslands 
supported ranching and grazing, reinforcing their dominance. Three percent of the total landscape is made up of 
critical wetlands that are “essential to wildlife and agriculture and support a number of rare plants and animals” 
(Colorado Natural Heritage Program, 2018).  
The Colorado Natural Heritage Program (CNHP) has identified rare plant communities and species in Huerfano 
County, and their surveys help define Potential Conservation Areas (PCAs) to protect biodiversity. The lands in 
Huerfano County are a habitat for many native species of wildlife including mule deer, mountain lion, black bear, 
bighorn sheep, multiple bird species and raptors including golden eagles and red-tailed hawks.  Warmer parts of 
the grasslands also allow homes for western rattlesnakes and a variety of amphibians (Colorado Parks and 
Wildlife, 2024).

An ISO rating (sometimes known as a Public Protection Classification (PPC)), is given to a fire department by the 
Insurance Services Office (ISO) , which is a company that scores a department's preparedness level to evaluate 
risk. To determine a department's score, the ISO considers community risk reduction, operational readiness (like 
staffing level and apparatus availability), water supply (like n umber of hydrants, flow rates, reliability), and 
communications (like dispatch and response time). The ranking system is standardized, and ISO ratings range 
from 1 (highest) to 10 (lowest).  
The ISO rating for HCFPD is a Class 4/4Y. HCFPD's ISO rating indicates moderate fire protection for some areas 
(Class 4), with sufficient proximity to fire hydrants. The split rating "4Y" normally applies to some parts of the 
response area that rely on alternative water sources other than a fire h ydrant, like cisterns, tanks, or shuttles. 
This can affect response time, efficiency, and flow rates.

The La Veta Fire Protection District (LVFPD), serves and protects

east of town which include Cuchara. This is the oldest fire district in

2.c. Wildland-Urban Interface 
Every year, wildfires result in billions of dollars in fire suppression costs and destroy thousands of homes across 
the United States (Bayham et al., 2022; Higuera et al., 2023). Some of the most destructive, deadly, and expensive 
wildfires occurred in the past several years, partly due to construction of additional homes in the wildland-urban 
interface (WUI). Wildfire risk in the WUI is further exacerbated by severe fire weather perpetuated by climate 
change (Caton et al., 2016) . Some nearby examples include the 2023 Trujillo Creek Fire which destroyed 118 
acres near Aguilar  and started in an unoccupied cabin  (human cause) and the 2026 Aspen Acres fire  in Custer 
County (human cause) with at least 324 homes and several commercial buildings lost  (Aspen Acres Fire Map | 
Custer, Colorado, 2026). See Appendix A for a discussion about how wildfires can threaten and destroy homes. 
The WUI is any area where the built environment meets wildfire -prone areas—places where wildland 
fire can move between natural vegetation and the built environment and result in negative impacts on 
the community (Mowry & Johnston, 2018) . The built environment includes homes, businesses, infrastructure, 
services such as utilities, roadways, and geographic features that aid in wildfire suppression, such as roads or 
ridgetops (Healthy Forest Restoration Act, 2003).  
The WUI exists along a continuum of wildland to urban densities ( 
 
 
Figure 2.c.1). The WUI is often subdivided into intermix, interface, and occluded types. Wildland -urban intermix 
refers to places where the built environment intermingles with wildland vegetation; wildland -urban interface 
refers to places where the built environment abuts large area of wildland vegetation; and occluded refers to places 
where wildland vegetation is surrounded by the built environment (Johnston, 2018).

2.d. Firefighting in the WUI 
One of the standard firefighter orders is to “fight fires aggressively, having provided for safety first” (NWCG, 
2018a). Firefighters are committed to protecting lives and property, but firefighting is particularly perilous in the 
WUI. The firefight ing community is committed to wildland firefighter safety, which can require them to cease 
structure protection when conditions are exceedingly dangerous, particularly around homes with inadequate 
defensible space, safety zones, and egress routes. 
Firefighters are often forced to perform structure  triage to effectively allocate limited resources during an 
incident, and more importantly, to protect the lives of firefighters.  The Incident Response Pocket Guide (IRPG), 
which is carried by all firefighters certified under the National Wildfire Coordinating Group, explicitly states, “Do 
not commit to stay and protect a structure unless a safety zone for firefighters and equipment has been identified 
at the structure during size-up and triage” (NWCG, 2018a). The IRPG outlines four categories of structure triage: 
1. Defensible – prep and hold. 
2. Defensible – stand alone. 
3. Non-defensible – prep and leave. 
4. Non-defensible – rescue drive-by. 
Do not count on firefighters staying to defend your home—your home should be able to stand strong on 
its own during a wildfire. There are never enough firefighters to stay and defend every single home during 
large incidents. Section Mitigate the Home Ignition Zone  of this CWPP provides recommendations for how 
residents can increase the chance of their homes standing strong during  wildfires and enhance the safety of 
wildland firefighters.

2.e. Fire History in Huerfano County 
Whether started by Indigenous peoples or naturally, frequent, low -severity fires were common in grasslands, 
shrublands, and ponderosa pine , and dry mixed -conifer forests before European settlement in the 1850s, and 
other forest types, particularly lodgepole and subalpine forests at higher elevations, experienced infrequent but 
high-severity wildfires (Error! Reference source not found. ). Many of the region's plant and animal species 
evolved around fire. Heat from wildfires opens the cones of lodgepole pine, releasing seeds into freshly cleared 
ground. Gambel oak and aspen resprout vigorously after fire -caused mortality, and a range of wild life species 
thrive in the more open, sunlit understories that follow burns (Kalies et al., 2012; Pilliod et al., 2006). 
Ponderosa pine forests with mixtures of Douglas-fir, Gamble oak, Rocky Mountain juniper, and aspen were fire -
adapted ecosystems and very resilient to wildfires. Low - to mixed-severity fires occurred every 7 to 50 years, 
creating a mosaic of widely spaced t rees and small tree clumps interwoven with grasslands and shrublands, 
particularly on drier south-facing slopes. North-facing slopes often supported denser forest stands (Addington et 
al., 2018). Frequent fires killed many tree seedlings and saplings, thereby preventing the accumulation of ladder 
fuels and reducing the potential for surface fires to transition into crown fires. Fire spread was more rapid 
through understory grasses but released far less heat, so many larger trees survived unscathed. Ponderosa pine 
ecosystems with fewer trees support more abundant and species -diverse understories of grasses, forbs, and 
shrubs and provide habitat for a variety of wildlife that prefer more open forest  structure (Kalies et al., 2012; 
Matonis & Binkley, 2018; Pilliod et al., 2006). 
At higher elevations, mixed-conifer forests with an abundance of Douglas-fir and variable mixture of ponderosa 
pine, lodgepole pine, Englemann spruce, Colorado blue spruce, subalpine fir, and aspen , burned less frequently 
and experienced wildfires every 20 to >100 years. These forests burned less frequently due to cooler, moister 
conditions at higher elevations, particularly on north -facing slopes, and they had higher tree densities than 
ponderosa pine ecosystems (Addington et al., 2018). High-severity wildfire could kill patches of trees and create 
a mosaic landscape with recently burned forests and dense unburned forests.  
Lodgepole pine forests are part of fire -adapted ecosystems that are resilient after infrequent, stand -replacing 
wildfires. Research on historical conditions in lodgepole pine forests suggest they experienced high -severity 
wildfires every couple of centurie s in northern Colorado and southern Wyoming (Higuera et al., 2021) . Young 
stands that are in recovery and regeneration stages after wildfires do not have the resources to regenerate after 
a second wildfire event, so frequent stand-replacing fires are detrimental to this ecosystem (Dennis et al., 2009; 
Turner et al., 2019) . Fires are becoming more common in high elevation lodgepole pine and wet mixed -conifer 
forests due to climate change (Higuera et al., 2021). 
A significant portion of Huerfano County  – approximately 14% - is composed of piñon pine ( Pinus edulis) and 
juniper (often referred to as piñon -juniper), specifically in the southwest portion of the county in areas of 
transitioning elevation, from around 4,000 to 8,000 feet (Colorado Natural Heritage Program, 2018) .  Piñon -
juniper forests burn infrequently at moderate - to high - (stand replacing) severity due to resin content and 
encroaching conifers and grass ecosystems (CFRI, 2021; McIntosh et al., 2023) . Piñon pine and juniper  are 
generally not resistant to fire and tend to grow between grass/shrubland and ponderosa pine stands, making it a 
flammable and contiguous fuel source between ground fuel and canopy (CFRI, 2021) . Nonnative cheatgrass 
encroachment upon piñon-juniper areas creates ladder fuel (fuels providing vertical continuity between strata,  
allowing fire to carry from surface fuels to the crowns of trees or shrubs), which further creates an environment 
prone to more frequent and more severe fire activity and outcompetes native vegetation. Piñon -juniper stands 
have been found to grow after ecological disturbances (e.g. stand-replacing fire, drought, or beetle kill). Due to a 
combination of climatic conditions, livestock grazing, and fire exclusion, piñon -juniper woodlands have both 
diminished and expanded in size throughout the 20th century (Romme et al., 2009). 
Annual and seasonal variation in the abundance and moisture content of grassland vegetation is extreme in 
Huerfano County, and this variation significantly impacts the risk of wildfire in grassland ecosystems. Wildfires 
occur more commonly in grasslands during especially dry years in Colorado, and they can be more likely one to

Wildfire behavior is vastly different today than it was over a century ago in many ecosystems of Colorado. As the 
initial farming, ranching, and logging activities of Euro-American settlers subsided in the region and government-
mandated fire suppression began in the late 1800s, forests filled in with trees (Addington et al., 2018) . Tree 
densities in lower -elevation forests, average 4.5 times higher today than they were in the mid -1800s, and tree 
densities in mid-elevation forests average 2.3 times higher today (Battaglia et al., 2018). Although many residents 
consider dense forest as “natural,” these conditions are vastly different from the fire -adapted and fire-resilient 
ecosystems that existed before.  Where fire once moved quickly through open understories with relatively low 
intensity, it now encounters continuous fuel loads, dense canopies, accumulated dead wood, and ladder fuels, that 
create more intense and destructive fires.  
With historic fire suppression resulting in unprecedented dense forests, as well as uncertainty in fire modeling 
predictions for piñon -juniper forests, it is important to maintain situational awareness amongst piñon -juniper 
woodlands and plan fuel treatmen ts accordingly to create resilience in a changing climate. Treatments include 
thinning and prescribed burning to disrupt fuel load and cheatgrass populations within woodlands.

This combination of dense wildland vegetation, extreme heat and high winds, unplanned ignitions, and housing 
developments in the WUI can create catastrophic wildfire scenarios (Haas et al., 2015). Climate change is making 
high-severity wildfires more frequent, intense, and larger in extent (Parks et al., 2016) . Many catastrophic 
wildfires in Colorado’s history have occurred on dry and windy days, resulting in rapidly spreading fires that 
outpace the ability of firefighters to respond.  
Examples can be seen with multiple and significant fires in 2026, underscoring the county's ongoing wildfire risk 
during a very dry season. The Big Sheep Fire (also called the Sheep Head Fire), was reported June 30 near Sheep 
Mountain about 22 miles northwest of Walsenburg, burned in steep terrain and prompted a pre-evacuation notice 
for residents nearby. It grew to a final size of approximately 1,322 acres before reaching 100% containment on 
July 6, with no injuries, fatalities, structure losses, or power outages reported; the cause remains under 
investigation (Watch Duty - Wildfire Maps & Alerts, n.d.). Just two days later, on July 8, the Pole Canyon Fire, which 
was believed to be naturally caused (Watch Duty - Wildfire Maps & Alerts, n.d.)  ignited at the eastern base of 
Greenhorn Mountain, roughly 17 miles northwest of Walsenburg and seven miles south of Rye.  
The most significant event, as of the writing of this CWPP,  was the Aspen Acres Fire, which began mid -July and 
burned across Custer, Pueblo, Fremont, and Huerfano counties. The fire grew to approximately 101,961 acres 
with 71% containment as of August 6, 2026, and was still burning, making it by far the largest and most impactful 
incident affecting the region this year. The fire triggered mandatory evacuations across multiple communities, 
including Colorado City, Beulah, Rye, San Isabel, Wetmore, Red Creek, and Apache City. Within Huerfano County 
specifically, three mandatory evacuation zones were established.  
Firefighters in Huerfano County suppress a vast majority of ignitions in the county before they exceed 0.25 acres 
in size, but fires can escape the initial capacity of firefighters under high, dry, and windy conditions. As confirmed 
by Huerfano County fire data, human -caused ignitions predominate in the county, with the most human - and 
lightning-caused ignitions occurring in June and July.
