Background:  
In 2023, the Superior Cultural Arts and Public Spaces advisory committee (CAPS) worked with 
staff to hire 23.4 Degrees LLC, to engage Superior residents about the possibility of a Marshall 
Fire Memorial. In January 2024, the Marshall Fire Memorial Subcommittee was formed, 
comprised of representation from Superior’s advisory groups, to further engage residents and to 
deeply assess the collective data. In the fall of 2024, the subcommittee and project were renamed 
to Marshall Fire Remembrance (MFR) and the group conceived of a potential location and 
concept plan for how a Remembrance might take shape.  
 
The MFR subcommittee worked with staff on the scope of an RFQ for the design of a 
remembrance project that included a Place to Gather, Pet Remembrance, and Dispersed 
Remembrance Components; collectively called the Marshall Fire Remembrance Project (MFRP). 
Design firm finalists were interviewed and Davis Partnerships + LandDesign were selected to 
create a design concept. Town Council reviewed the preliminary design from the consultants in 
November 2025 with support to continue moving forward with the design and fundraising 
planning. 
In early 2026, the Town contacted Boulder County to initiate needed approvals for locating the 
MFRP on a Town-owned open space parcel that includes County Conservation Easement 
requirements. The County responded with a letter of general support, with some design 
parameters and the request to keep them informed on the progress of the project. The Town has 
completed a survey of the project site and geotechnical report, which have been shared with the 
design firm.  
Town staff met with Louisville Cultural Services staff in February 2026, to bring them up to date 
on the project and inquire about Louisville's interest in the involvement. Design concepts were 
Page 2 of 77
shared with Louisville understanding that the Marshall Fire impacted all of our residents and the 
MFRP would be important to all. The project team of Davis Partnerships + LandDesign 
presented the MFR concept design to Louisville City Council on August 11th, 2026. Louisville 
staff indicated they were conducting a public survey for residents to explore priorities for 
expenditure of Louisville City funds in support of Marshall Fire events and/or projects. The 
results of this survey are expected to be presented to the Louisville City Council on September 
10th.  
Funding The Project  
Town staff reached out to several non-profit organizations to inquire about fundraising assistance 
and were successful in receiving a positive response from the Colorado Gives Foundation to 
serve as the non-profit entity/fiscal partner for the project. Colorado Gives is currently working 
on the formal agreement for the MFRP to be a Donor Advised Fund under their umbrella. This 
would be a fund dedicated to this specific project and would hold funds for the project, but not 
any future maintenance. The Town also reached out to local fund-raising consultants that could 
help create the fundraising campaign, and conduct the actual fund-raising for the project. The 
Town sent Requests for Proposals to the top four of six consultants recommended by Colorado 
Gives. The Town anticipates opening the donation portal soon with a formal kick-off coordinated 
with the December 18th Marshall Fire 5-Year Anniversary Resilience Celebration to take place 
in Downtown Superior Civic Space and Plaza.  
 
Marshall Fire 5-Year Anniversary Resilience Celebration

18th, 6-9pm at the Superior Civic Space and Downtown Plaza. The event offers a bit ‘more’ than

that carried us forward, connect with those who helped us make recovery possible, and celebrate

Background:  
 
LANDSCAPE TRANSFORMATION ROADMAP STUDY 
Because of the importance of conserving water in our arid region and our community’s desire to 
be a good steward of our natural resources, the Town recently finished a year-long process on 
strategies and policy recommendations for identifying and converting non-functional turf (NFT) 
and upgrading aging irrigation infrastructure on Town-owned properties. This process, which 
resulted in the Waterwise Landscape Transformation Roadmap (Attachment 1), will help the 
Town advance our sustainability and conservation goals, as well as to support more consistent 
and strategic decision-making on where water conservation is most appropriate through 
Council’s annual budget process. It will also better set the stage for successful grant applications 
in the future.  Matt Rarick, Superintendent of Parks and Open Space, will also attend this 
evening's Work Session to discuss current and planned project items with Council. 
Historically, the Town has successfully completed a number of turf replacement projects across 
Superior, particularly in medians, but these efforts have been implemented on a project-by-
project basis, resulting in varying approaches to site selection, landscape design, and project 
implementation. Town staff applied for and were accepted into WaterNow’s Project Accelerator 
program, which provided over a year of no-cost technical support to complete a comprehensive 
roadmap guiding future NFT conversion and irrigation system upgrade projects based on the 
Town’s specific circumstances and priorities as summarized below. 
Defining Non-Functional Turf 
Developing an internal definition of functional and non-functional turf for use on Town-owned 
properties was a key component of this work. The project team reviewed definitions used by 
Page 5 of 77
peer communities and state regulations across the western United States before working with 
Town staff to develop definitions that reflect Superior's landscape management goals and 
operational needs. Interviews with peer communities were also conducted. More on the 
definitions can be found on page 5 of the Waterwise Landscape Transformation Roadmap 
attachment. 
Mapping Town Turf 
As part of the process of creating the Roadmap, Town staff identified potential project sites, 
developed preliminary landscape approaches and created criteria for prioritizing projects. This 
work included the creation of a map of all Town-owned turf in Superior, with different colors for 
non-essential turf, essential turf and other categories relating to Town priorities, like turf along 
roadways (See Roadmap, Attachment 1, pages 10 and 11).  
The mapping found that there are 158.8 Acres of Town-Maintained Turf (6% of Town Land) and 
27.6 Acres of Town-Maintained Xeriscape/Non-Turf (1% of Town Land) for a total of 186.4 
Acres (7% of Town Land). Additionally, that turf was categorized and mapped with designations 
for right-of-way (ROW) Turf (.5 acres), Functional Turf (33.5 acres), Non-functional turf (83.1), 
Park Turf (42.2 acres), ROW Adjacent/Tract Turf (69.6 acres) and areas with Xeriscape/ No 
Turf (27.6 acres).  Total non-functional turf (ROW adjacent/tract turf, park turf and ROW turf ) 
measures 125.3 acres. 
The map distinguishes functional and non-functional turf based on the Town's internal 
definitions and provides a consistent spatial basis for identifying potential NFT conversion 
opportunities. In addition to supporting project identification, the map illustrates the big picture 
of landscaping types and uses across the Town and can be used to quantify existing turf and 
water-efficient landscapes and track changes as projects are implemented. 
  
Turf Removal Project Prioritization  
 
Additionally, there is an internally facing project prioritization tool for staff decision making. As 
part of the Project Accelerator, Town staff used the Irrigated Turf Map, existing Town priorities, 
and their institutional knowledge to identify an initial portfolio of NFT conversion projects to 
advance over the next five or more years. 
  
The initial portfolio includes a variety of NFT site types. Most of the selected projects are 
located within medians or other public ROW landscapes, reflecting Superior's decision to focus 
first on clearly non-functional turf with limited recreational use. The remaining projects include 
ROW adjacent landscapes and a small number of park sites that staff identified as strong 
candidates based on existing conditions and operational priorities.  
  
Roadmap Engagement  
 
Engagement both internal and external was included in this process. A larger group of staff from 
Parks, Sustainability, Public Works and Administration was engaged and both the Advisory 
Committee for Environmental Sustainability (ACES) and the Parks and Recreation Advisory 
Committee (PARC) provided input on the draft Roadmap document and map.

Grant Applications and Water Conservation Policy  
This project has resulted in increased organizational preparedness for grants. The roadmap 
provides clear goals and definitions, and the internal project prioritization tool compiles a list of 
high-priority projects complete with the elements of project scope of work often needed to 
assemble a competitive grant application. Details within the tool like project location and size, 
estimated cost, priority level, and intended outcomes like quantified water savings, etc., prepares 
the Town to be able to strategically capitalize on funding opportunities like local, state or federal 
grant programs that often prioritize shovel-ready projects demonstrating community benefit, cost 
reasonableness and technical merit. Grant applications would be further bolstered by Council 
consideration of adoption of this Roadmap at a future Council meeting once staff works with the 
consultant team to incorporate Council feedback on the document and map. 
This work is already getting positive attention from water conservation partners. Northern Water, 
our water provider, heard about this work when staff reached out to them about a grant letter of 
support and has expressed interest in the Town presenting on it to peer communities as part of a 
future regional gathering for Growing Water Smart graduate communities. Alexis Bullen has 
also been tapped to present on this work at the American Water Works Association WaterSmart 
Innovations conference this October in Portland. 
 
ROCK CREEK PARKWAY AND MCCASLIN MEDIAN PROJECT 
Staff have applied for two separate grants to match one another in funding for removal of turf 
and installation of xeriscape the large median at the top of Rock Creek Parkway and McCaslin. 
This project is the number one ranked priority in the Town’s new turf prioritization tool. One 
application was submitted for the federal Small-Scale Water Efficiency Projects (SWEP) grant 
and one application was submitted for the non-federal Colorado Water Conservation Board 
Water Plan grant. If both grants are awarded, the pairing of federal and non-federal funding 
would almost entirely offset the total project cost; the remaining Town match to implement the 
project would be $12,500. We expect to hear back by the end of 2026 or early 2027. The project 
area is a 14,500 sq ft median situated at the top of Rock Creek Parkway and McCaslin 
Boulevard. The entire median will be converted to a xeriscape design which will include the 
removal of 10,833 sq ft of turf that currently exists within the median. The project will replace 
existing landscaping with a mix of rock mulches, shrubs, perennials, and grasses. High water 
input rotor irrigation nozzles will be replaced with high efficiency drip irrigation.  
The project will result in an estimated 819,753 gallons of water savings over six years. At 
baseline, the RCP median uses approx. 169,786 gallons per year. After the landscape conversion 
and irrigation system improvements, this median will use approximately 85,403 gallons in its 
first year, 38,750 gallons in year two and an estimated 18,072 gallons per year from year three 
onward. This represents 89% water savings over six years. These estimates were calculated 
using the WaterNow Alliance Pilot Parcel Water Savings Estimate Tool.  
This water savings represents a significant benefit to the Town and region as well as the Town’s 
water delivery system as it will: 
Page 7 of 77
• Reduce community vulnerability to prolonged drought 
• Lower municipal water demand in general and during drought emergencies 
• Replace high-water-use, non-essential turf with drought-tolerant vegetation 
• Support wildfire resilience by using fire-wise landscaping 
 
DROUGHT-IMPACTED TURF AREAS 
Persistent dry winter conditions combined with the summer drought and efforts to conserve 
water resources through reduced irrigation have negatively impacted Town turfgrass areas. The 
Town reduced irrigation water use by 20% in early summer and retained this reduction in open 
space properties. Irrigation systems were designed to deliver adequate water to turfgrass in 
normal conditions. Many irrigated systems are not designed to deliver 100% of needed water in 
drought conditions resulting in stressed areas of turfgrass. Turfgrass areas experiencing stress 
include south facing slopes, areas with little or no shade, and new turfgrass areas that are less 
than 2-3 years old and still not fully established.  
In addition to the current drought conditions, construction and road projects require dry 
conditions and suspension of watering during extended project timelines resulted in additional 
stress on turfgrass that is already struggling. Many irrigation zones along Rock Creek Parkway 
and Indiana were turned off to ensure that construction could continue without interruption. 
Supplemental watering with water trucks helps but cannot replace the efficiency and 
effectiveness of the irrigation systems.  
Staff will be assessing turfgrass areas that are stressed to determine the best method to revitalize, 
restore, replace, or potentially remove the turfgrass. Some areas are struggling but with improved 
fall weather conditions could rebound and recover. Some areas may be candidates for turf 
removal based on location, use, and size. There will also be areas that are in an advanced state of 
decline but are best suited to have turfgrass, and these areas will need turfgrass to be replaced. 
Staff will evaluate impacted areas throughout the fall to recommend the best path forward in 
each situation.

FACILITIES DECARBONIZATION STUDY 
 
This year, Town staff have been working with our consultants, Blue Strike, on a study assessing 
the feasibility of electrifying and maximizing energy efficiency for five Town-owned buildings. 
Those buildings – Town Hall, the Bungalow Annex, the Water Treatment Plant (WTP), the 
Wastewater Treatment Plant (WWTP) and the Parks Office in the Firehouse, are the remaining 
buildings the Town owns that have not been previously addressed, excluded due to lack of 
equipment ownership, or otherwise analyzed for sustainability enhancements through a previous 
study process.  
This work meets a number of goals, including climate and energy goals outlined in 
the Sustainability Action Plan and the Town's Beneficial Electrification Plan. The final report 
will help with facility planning and allow the Town to prioritize and plan for investments in this 
study that was recommended by the ACES Committee and approved by Council as part of the 
2026 budget. This work also builds on the 2024 roadmap for energy efficiency and 
electrification at the Community Center funded through a Boulder County Sustainability grant. 
Currently, this is a planning study only; no construction or implementation work is included in 
this scope.  
The scope of this facilities work covers establishing a baseline understanding for each facility, 
identifying opportunities for energy efficiency and electrification, evaluating the electrical and 
power needs of each facility, and then outlining priorities. This analysis includes capturing 
energy and cost savings estimates based on equipment specifications and consumption and 
identifying all-electric and hybrid configurations of potential equipment, with comparative 
analysis of each pathway and associated capital cost estimates. The project's scope is limited to 
building systems at each facility (it does not include water or wastewater treatment process 
equipment) and is being delivered within the Town's $50,000 project budget. 
As part of their final report, the consultant will prepare four prioritization scenarios, each 
reflecting a different decision lens: 
• Age and replacement timeline: Prioritizes measures based on equipment at or near end of 
useful life, minimizing stranded asset risk and identifying opportunities to 'electrify on 
replacement' 
• Impact and return on investment: Prioritizes by financial return, utility bill reduction, and 
natural gas use elimination, incorporating utility incentives and available grant programs 
into the analysis 
• Ease and equipment availability: Identifies 'low-hanging fruit', measures that are cost-
effective, readily available, and require minimal infrastructure investment, to help the 
Town build early momentum 
• Greenhouse gas reduction and benchmarking: Benchmarks each facility against efficient 
EUI and natural gas GUI targets, identifies the largest gaps, and prioritizes measures that 
Page 9 of 77
deliver the most meaningful emissions reductions 
Financial analysis is a key component of this scope, with emphasis on exploration of pathways 
that alleviate or reduce cost burdens on the Town's general fund. A full lifecycle cost analysis for 
each major measure category, comparing the business-as-usual cost of retaining existing 
equipment (including maintenance, fuel, and end-of-life replacement costs) against all-electric 
and hybrid alternatives over a 10-to-20-year horizon will be prepared. There will also be 
guidance on grant opportunities and any other potential rebates or cost-saving strategies included 
in the report. 
As of this update, the baseline data collection and opportunity identification work is substantially 
complete. The electrical load evaluation is in progress, and the prioritization and lifecycle cost 
analysis is beginning. A draft report compiling all findings is expected in the next month or two 
and then shared with ACES and Town Council. 
The following reflects preliminary, in-progress findings based on data collected to date. These 
are directional observations, not final recommendations — the Town's final prioritized options 
will be delivered once the prioritization and lifecycle analysis task is complete. 
• Lighting retrofits can produce near-term cost savings. There are significant lighting and 
lighting control upgrade opportunities at the WTP and WWTP. Converting lights to LED 
is a straightforward, comparatively low-cost efficiency measure with a clear payback. 
These upgrades can also enhance safety and reduce maintenance costs.  
• Decarbonizing heating equipment may be most cost-effective as end-of-life replacement, 
rather than early retrofit. However, several facilities have HVAC and water heating 
equipment approaching the end of useful life. Replacing this equipment with efficient 
electric alternatives at that natural replacement point will be a likely recommendation. 
• There may be significant opportunities for additional savings through repowering 
existing solar systems. A preliminary analysis of existing solar arrays indicates that 
increases in power may be available through upgrading (repowering) the Town's existing, 
aging solar PV installations. This finding should be evaluated separately in more depth 
before any recommendation is made. 
Blue Strike will continue to keep the Town updated as the electrical load evaluation and the 
prioritization and lifecycle cost analysis findings are finalized and will bring a full set of 
prioritized recommendations forward in the draft report. 
Additionally, Town staff have partnered with Xcel Energy's Partners in Energy on a plan to 
consolidate all findings from the five-building study with all the previous Town findings into a 
master Facilities Decarbonization Roadmap document to guide decision-making now into the 
future once the study discussed above is complete.

The biggest issue with the Town's solar arrays is they were primarily built between 2009-2011 
and thus are reaching their end of life, as well as being much lower efficiency panels than current 
solar panels on the market. There are also only a few years remaining on our twenty-year Xcel 
Energy renewable energy credit (REC) revenue agreements, which generate income for the 
Town and help offset maintenance costs. These panels have generated significant revenue over 
their life since being paid off and also greatly reduce the costs of electricity on Town energy 
bills, particularly at energy-intensive buildings like the water treatment plants. Additionally, 
federal solar tax credits to cover 30% of projects will only be available for solar projects 
completed by December 31, 2027, giving some additional urgency to exploring this topic now 
for the financial viability of both a Town-owned approach or exploring alternative ownership or 
financing models. The Town is able to access tax credits directly, despite its tax-exempt status, 
though it will require staff hours to go through a finance process to set up Direct Pay to receive 
the funds if the Town purchases the panels directly.

After further discussions with Blue Strike as part of the facilities scope, the Town is moving 
forward with a deeper assessment and financial analysis of our solar. Current draft electrical 
capacity analysis has identified an estimated 75% increase in available capacity across the 
Town's existing facilities from repowering (upgrading) the arrays. Blue Strike is evaluating the 
estimated financial impact of repowering the Town's existing solar PV installations to take 
advantage of that increased capacity. This analysis will also include exploring all possible 
financial models for the solar and a combination of the solar and the other energy efficiency 
work, including a Power Purchase Agreement (PPA), leasing, Energy Service Companies 
(ESCOs) and energy performance contracting. 
Repowering the solar array at both the North and South Pools is currently in the 2027 Town 
budget; those arrays are currently in the worst shape according to our solar contractor, Namaste, 
and some of the panels at both sites are coming free from their frames. There are also currently 
2027 funds allocated to add solar to the new Operations and Maintenance (O&M) building, 
which will be a particularly valuable addition to the mostly electrified building. 
Next steps and potential paths forward on all the Town's solar assets will be recommended as 
part of this analysis. Because this additional level of detailed study resulted from the facilities 
process and the deeper dive was not originally scoped, the current goal and timeline is to be 
finished by the end of the year, with high-level takeaways made available as soon as possible to 
inform Council’s future decision-making.

Draft Definition of Non-Functional Turf for Superior
Non-functional turf (NFT) or turfgrass 
means irrigated turfgrass on Town-owned property that is not designed for, nor regularly used, as civic, 
community, or recreational purposes. These areas may meet one or more of the following criteria 
below: 
1. Adjacent to and within all parking lots (e.g., parking lot islands and perimeters); 
2. Located in roadway medians, roundabouts, and other public rights-of-way;
3. Within 10 feet from a public street;
4. With a slope of 25% or greater (excluding sledding hills or designated play areas);
5. Adjacent to Town buildings (e.g., within 25 feet of the building footprint);
6. Turf problem areas where turf has been unsuccessful at being established or is difficult to maintain

Introduction 
This roadmap provides a framework for advancing non-functional turf (NFT) conversion and irrigation 
system upgrades on Town-owned properties in Superior, Colorado. It brings together the research, 
tools, and project development completed through WaterNow’s Project Accelerator with 
recommendations for implementing Superior's current priority projects and a repeatable process for 
future NFT conversion efforts. 
Project Background 
The Town of Superior recognizes the importance of converting NFT and upgrading aging irrigation 
infrastructure on Town-owned properties to advance its sustainability and conservation goals. The Town 
has successfully completed several turf replacement projects, but these efforts have historically been 
implemented on a project-by-project basis, resulting in varying approaches to site selection, landscape 
design, and project implementation. To support more consistent and strategic decision-making, the 
Town sought to develop a comprehensive roadmap to guide future NFT conversion and irrigation system 
upgrade projects. 
 
Converting non-functional turf to waterwise landscaping can substantially reduce outdoor water 
demand while creating more resilient and functional public landscapes. Colorado-specific research 
estimates that conventionally irrigated Kentucky bluegrass typically requires approximately 15–16 
gallons of supplemental irrigation per square foot annually, compared with approximately 7.5–11 
gallons per square foot for typical waterwise landscapes. At a planning level, this means that converting 
10,000 square feet of high-water-use turf to waterwise landscaping could reduce irrigation demand by 
approximately 40,000–85,000 gallons per year, depending on the replacement landscape, existing 
irrigation practices, and site conditions.1 In addition to water savings, well-designed landscape 
conversions can provide broader community benefits, including reduced maintenance needs, improved 
pollinator and wildlife habitat, healthier soils and improved stormwater infiltration, neighborhood

To achieve their water conservation goals and implement a strategic framework, the Town applied to 
and was selected for WaterNow’s Project Accelerator Program in Spring 2025, receiving 250 hours of 
pro bono technical assistance. Over the past year, WaterNow and Town staff collaborated to research 
best practices from peer communities, review applicable federal, state, and local policies, develop an 
internal definition of non-functional turf, map potential NFT areas on Town-owned properties, establish 
project prioritization criteria, and create a project ranking tool to support future decision-making. This 
work resulted in several key resources and outcomes for the Town, including the Town-owned 
Landscaping and Turf Map, the Project Prioritization Tool, an initial portfolio of ten priority NFT 
conversion projects, and this roadmap for advancing these projects. 
 
Developing an internal definition of functional and non-functional turf for use on Town-owned 
properties was a key component of this work. The project team reviewed definitions used by peer 
communities and state regulations across the western United States before working with Town staff to 
develop definitions that reflect Superior's landscape management goals and operational needs. These 
definitions are intended to guide internal decision-making on NFT mapping, project prioritization, and 
future turf replacement on Town-owned properties. If the Town chooses to expand these definitions for 
use on private property in the future, it may wish to seek additional public input before adoption. 
 
Turf or turfgrass means irrigated, maintained grass species that is regularly mowed and primarily 
intended to provide an aesthetic lawn appearance. Turf does not include native grasses, grasses 
hybridized for arid conditions, ornamental grasses, or other non-irrigated vegetation.   
   
Functional turf or turfgrass means turf that is located in a recreational use area or other space that is 
designed for, and regularly used for, civic, community, or recreational purposes. Functional turf or 
turfgrass includes:   
1. Athletic Fields   
2. Designated play or recreation areas (e.g., small patches of turf within playgrounds or other 
community facilities specifically intended for informal play or recreation)   
3. Amphitheaters   
4. Event spaces   
5. Sledding hills   
6. Dog parks    
7. Utilities or stormwater areas for drainage or erosion needs  
8. Historic or interpretive settings    
9. Cemeteries    
 
Non-functional turf (NFT) or turfgrass means irrigated turfgrass on Town-owned property that is not 
designed for, nor regularly used as, civic, community, or recreational purposes. These areas may meet 
one or more of the following criteria below:    
1. Adjacent to and within all parking lots (e.g., parking lot islands and perimeters);

Throughout the project, the team also engaged Town staff and advisory committees to ensure the 
roadmap reflects local priorities and practical implementation considerations. A cross-departmental 
staff workshop gathered feedback on the proposed NFT definitions, lessons learned from previous turf 
conversion projects, and the benefits and challenges associated with future conversions. Staff also 
identified and prioritized the criteria that should be incorporated into the site ranking tool. In addition, 
the project team presented the draft approach to the Advisory Committee for Environmental 
Sustainability (ACES) and the Parks, Recreation, Arts, and Culture (PARC) Advisory Committee to gather 
input on priority project locations and on the community co-benefits to consider when evaluating future 
NFT conversion projects. This feedback helped refine the NFT definition, project prioritization 
framework, and the recommendations throughout this roadmap.

This roadmap brings together the work completed through the Project Accelerator with a practical

process, including the Town's Irrigated Turf Map, Project Prioritization Tool, peer community case 
studies, and funding research. These resources are incorporated throughout the roadmap and can be 
updated and reused as projects advance and new opportunities emerge.

Recommendations for Future Project Selection 
When Superior is ready to identify a new group of NFT conversion projects, the Town can repeat the 
site-selection process used during the Project Accelerator. The Irrigated Turf Map provides a 
consistent starting point, and can be used alongside field observations, operational knowledge, and 
evolving Town priorities. 
 
To identify potential NFT conversion projects, Superior can use three complementary approaches: 
• Visual inspection to quickly identify obvious opportunities, such as medians or parking lots; 
• GIS analysis to identify sites that meet specific Town priorities or selection criteria; and  
• Staff knowledge and field observations to account for operational considerations that may 
not be reflected in the map.  
 
Using a combination of these approaches allows the Town to tailor site selection to current priorities, 
available funding, and staff experience. The map is intended to support, not replace, staff expertise. 
Candidate sites identified through GIS should therefore be reviewed during field visits to confirm 
existing conditions, irrigation infrastructure, safety considerations, and operational constraints before 
advancing to the prioritization process. 
 
Areas classified as NFT on the map meet at least one of the criteria in the Town's internal NFT 
definition (See Project Background). During development of the NFT map, Superior intentionally did 
not prioritize one NFT criterion over another. However, for preliminary site selection, the Town may 
wish to focus on a few "low-hanging fruit" categories, such as medians or parking lots, to generate an 
initial list of candidate projects. 
 
Superior may also wish to narrow candidate sites based on additional questions, such as: 
• Which areas have low pedestrian activity? 
• Which areas are adjacent to existing water-efficient landscaping or past conversions? 
• Which areas may connect to the region’s pollinator garden network? 
• Which areas are within planned or recently completed capital improvement projects? 
• Which areas border the wildland-urban interface (WUI) or lie within high-risk wildfire zones? 
• Which areas does the Town receive complaints about watering or experience higher than 
usual maintenance issues? 
 
These questions can often be answered by overlaying additional GIS layers with the irrigated turf map 
to identify locations where multiple priorities overlap. For example, staff could identify NFT sites that 
are both within a planned capital improvement project and adjacent to the wildland-urban interface, 
allowing multiple Town objectives to be addressed through a single project. These spatial filters can 
help staff narrow a large inventory of potential sites to a manageable list of candidate projects.

Selecting Landscape Approaches for Future Projects 
As Superior develops landscape approaches for future NFT conversion projects, the peer community 
examples in Appendix A: Peer Community NFT Conversion Projects can provide a reference for 
considering how landscape design can respond to site characteristics and Town priorities. The 
examples demonstrate that communities have adapted their approaches based on factors such as site 
function, visibility, maintenance capacity, irrigation needs, and desired community benefits. Superior 
has already implemented many of these considerations into selecting the initial portfolio of priority 
projects and may wish to consider the following in future project: 
• Match the landscape approach to the site's visibility and public use. Highly visible civic 
spaces often incorporate more diverse waterwise landscapes, including native and drought-
tolerant plantings, trees, gathering spaces, and limited areas of functional turf, to maintain 
aesthetics and community value, while lower-profile locations such as medians, utility sites, 
or low-use park areas are often well suited for simpler native grass or low-water planting 
conversions. Westminster's City Hall project and Louisville's median renovations illustrate 
how landscape intensity can vary depending on site visibility. 
• Select landscape treatments that reflect how the site functions. Peer communities tailored 
landscape approaches to site type. Medians frequently used low-maintenance native grasses 
and drought-tolerant plantings, parks often retaining limited functional turf and native grass 
areas, and demonstration sites incorporated more diverse plantings and educational features.  
• Consider irrigation improvements alongside the replacement landscape. Many communities 
paired turf conversion with irrigation modernization to ensure efficient irrigation of the new 
landscape. Considering irrigation improvements alongside the landscape approach can help 
inform water savings, maintenance requirements, and project costs. 
• Select landscape approaches that align with long-term maintenance capacity.  
• Peer communities considered available maintenance resources when selecting landscape 
treatments, balancing more intensive landscapes with the capacity to maintain them over 
time. 
• Use landscape design to achieve multiple community goals. NFT conversion can be paired 
with other priorities, such as wildfire risk reduction, pollinator habitat, stormwater 
management, increasing shade, or public education. Considering these co-benefits early can 
help identify landscape approaches that support multiple Town objectives while 
strengthening future funding opportunities.  
• Develop repeatable landscape approaches for common site types. Communities such as 
Longmont and Erie have developed standardized landscape templates and plant palettes for 
common municipal landscapes. Similar approaches could help Superior streamline future 
project design, create greater consistency, and reduce design effort over time. (See more 
details about Longmont’s standardized design template in Appendix A: Peer Community NFT 
Conversion Projects).

Step 3: Evaluating & Prioritizing Projects for Implementation  
Project Work Completed 
After identifying candidate project sites and considering preliminary landscape concepts, Superior 
evaluated the ten priority projects to establish an initial implementation sequence. Due to staffing, 
funding, and construction capacity constraints, the Town will likely limit implementation to one or two 
projects per year, and a consistent approach to evaluating projects can help the Town direct resources 
toward projects that best advance its goals. 
 
To support this process, WaterNow developed the Project Prioritization Tool. The tool allows staff to 
evaluate potential projects using a consistent set of criteria derived from Town plans, staff workshops, 
and advisory committee input. In addition to assessing water savings, the tool recognizes that NFT 
conversion projects can provide multiple community benefits and gives Superior a way to consider these 
opportunities alongside water conservation. The resulting scores provide a transparent framework for 
comparing projects and establishing an initial implementation sequence. The tool also allows Superior to 
document why certain projects move forward before others. 
 
The tool evaluates projects across seven criteria that reflect the priorities identified during this project. 
• Irrigation System Efficiency Improvements: How the project will improve irrigation system 
efficiency and reduce water waste, overspray, runoff, and potential damage to nearby 
infrastructure.  
• Water Savings: Estimated water savings compared to cool-season turfgrass?3 
• Replaces Unused and Underutilized Turf Areas: Extent to which the project replaces turf that 
has limited recreational or other functional use. 
• Reduces Wildfire Risk: How the project will reduce wildfire risk through landscape design and 
vegetation management. 
• Implementation Alongside Other Town Projects: Potential to coordinate implementation with 
planned or recently completed roadway, trail, or other Town projects. 
• Provides Environmental Co-benefits: How the project will provide environmental co-benefits 
beyond water savings. 
• Town Priority: Alignment with current Town priorities. 
 
The Town used the prioritization tool to evaluate the priority projects identified through the Project 
Accelerator. Following the initial prioritization, an additional project was added based on its status as a 
Town priority. Projects that ranked highest generally combined substantial water-savings potential with 
opportunities to improve irrigation efficiency, replace clearly nonfunctional turf, and advance other 
Town priorities such as coordination with other Town projects and wildfire risk reduction. Because the 
tool allows Superior to assign different weights to each criterion, the rankings reflect the relative

Applying the Funding Approach to Future Projects 
The same funding framework can be applied as Superior identifies additional projects. As new 
projects are developed and prioritized, the Town can assess project scale, readiness, potential 
benefits, and available funding programs to determine whether projects should be pursued 
individually, phased, or bundled with other improvements. The funding resources identified in this 
roadmap can serve as a starting point, but Superior may wish to reassess available programs and 
requirements as new projects move forward.
