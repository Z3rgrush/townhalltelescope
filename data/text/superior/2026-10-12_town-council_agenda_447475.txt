Background:  
The Town began service with Republic Services for residential waste collection on April 1, 
2025, and currently serves 1,249 homes in Superior. The service includes recycling and trash 
pickup every week, as well as every other week compost pickup, with electric collection (EV) 
trucks, additional educational outreach from the vendor and a pay-as-you-throw (PAYT) or 
volume-based pricing model with cart size choices determined by each resident.  Republic 
Services staff members Steve Gilmore, Municipal Relationship Manager, and Matthew Silva, 
Operations Manager, plan to attend the meeting this evening to discuss and answer questions 
from Council about their firm's services in Superior. 
 
OPERATIONS 
Since operations started, there have been relatively few complaints from residents or issues that 
have limited Republic’s ability to serve the residents. In many communities where Republic 
operates, contamination of both recycling and organics streams, vehicles blocking containers, 
and customers not putting out their containers are significant issues. Over the past 6 months, 
there have been no issues reported for any of these items, and no missed pickups have been 
reported. 
 
The EV trucks are running well and are operating with an uptime of 95%.  Traditional internal 
combustion engine garbage trucks have an uptime of around 80%, so a significant improvement. 
This is a major benefit for both the Town and Republic’s operations. When a truck breaks down 
on route, another truck needs to be diverted to finish with a driver who might not be familiar 
with the community, which impacts operations across Republic’s service area and could 
negatively impact operations in the Town, such as a missed pickup because of the lack of 
familiarity. 
 
Republic in January added Commerce City as another community utilizing EV trucks to provide 
their residential waste collection service, and the City is pleased with their performance. There 
are several other cities that are interested in EV trucks, including a couple that currently do not

have a single-hauler contract but are looking at them in the context of a future request for 
proposal process.  Republic is currently operating two of the first Volterra ZFL trucks for the 
company, which are fully integrated front-loading EV collection vehicles. Republic is projecting 
that by the end of the year that their Commerce City yard will have over 20 EV trucks operating 
out of this facility. 
 
The McNeilus Volterra EV trucks in use in Superior continue to be best-in-class for all types of 
refuse collection trucks for their safety features that include 360-degree cameras, blind-spot 
monitoring, automated braking and lane-departure warnings.

CART DATA 
There has essentially been no change in cart sizes since last year across all categories, with 10% 
large trash cart (95 gallon), 61% medium trash cart (65 gallon), 29% small trash cart (35 gallon). 
For compost, 97% are small carts. For recycling, the breakdown is large 70%, medium 19%, and 
small 11%. While the trends remain consistent with last year’s data, residents can change their 
sizes up to three times per year for no charge. Only eleven homes have opted to pay for extra 
bins beyond the standard set included (two recycle; nine trash), meaning current service levels 
are working well for most people. 
 
The overall percentage of residents with compost carts is unchanged this year at 65%, but we 
have seen residents continuing to opt to keep the compost carts as more have joined the service 
over the last year.  
 
DIVERSION DATA 
Working with Republic has greatly improved the quality and frequency of Town waste data. Not 
only do we get the diversion and cart choice data monthly, but the more frequent reporting also 
allows us to address any issues as they happen with additional reports on customer service and 
pickup issues. The current 2026 Town service collection is currently at 30.3% diversion, which 
represents how much of the waste stream (i.e. compost and recycling) was kept from the landfill 
just from Superior’s Town contract customers. This year’s Republic diversion rate is slightly 
lower than last year’s 35.5%, but neither of those represents a full year of data due to the April 
2025 start date and the fact that the current year is not yet complete. Staff will continue to 
monitor the data and look for ways to improve resident education and diversion. 
 
REPUBLIC’S SUSTAINABILITY INVESTMENTS  
Republic Services highlighted the Town in its recent company-wide  Sustainability Report on 
page 19. The report showcases the Town’s improved data collection and the use of EV trucks, 
which, when charged on Colorado’s electric grid, reduce greenhouse gas emissions 
approximately 75% when compared with diesel trucks. 
 
The company continues to invest in local facilities to increase diversion away from landfills. 
Republic’s Foothills Organics Transfer Station has been completed, and it is being utilized daily, 
both by Republic and third parties.

Republic also doubled the size of its Bennett, Colorado, composting facility and can now process 
over 20,000 tons of organics per year. This facility is proving to be especially beneficial for 
Boulder County communities as the County recently announced that their site is not moving

Renewable Natural Gas (RNG) plants are on schedule to be built at the company’s Foothills

neutral fuel, and it captures organic waste that would otherwise decay and create additional

REIMBURSEMENT FOR RECYCLING THROUGH STATE PROGRAM 
House Bill 22-1355, the Producer Responsibility Program for Statewide Recycling Act, more 
often referred to as Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR), is a key policy for Council to be 
aware of because it changes residential waste collection costs specifically for recycling service 
and also impacts what materials can be recycled as a result of the newly established statewide 
rebate program. 
 
Town staff have submitted our request to Circular Alliance Action (CAA), who is managing this 
state process, for reimbursement of recycling collection costs, to reimburse residents on our 
contract through a reduction reflected on their individual utility bills for the cost of recycling, 
and an application for recycling outreach and education funds.  
 
Once CAA reviews our application and rebate numbers are finalized over the coming weeks, 
staff and the Town Attorney will work on finalizing the related agreements, which are planned 
for Council review after the start of the year. A contract amendment for Republic, which will 
include an explanation of their administrative fee under EPR collection costs and their additional 
services provided with this fee, is planned to come to Council at the same time.  
 
WASTE COLLECTION SERVICE AT APARTMENT COMPLEXES 
State statute C.R.S. § 30-15-401(7.5) prevents the Town from requiring larger apartment 
complexes to be a part of Town’s residential trash contract. However, given the significant 
number of residents residing in apartment complexes in Superior, understanding the current 
status of recycling and composting is important to our Town goals. 
 
To help better understand waste collection at apartment complexes, the Town partnered with the 
state’s Colorado Circular Communities (C3) program this year to provid free consulting support 
to study the current status of recycling and composting services at the Town's apartment 
complexes, as well as identify barriers and opportunities for waste service in those communities. 
Inclusive Design Group (IDG), the selected consultant, provided a report (attached) with their 
research, hauler and property manager interview takeaways, resident survey, and site 
assessments of all three properties in Superior. 
Page 5 of 298
 
The report found that recycling already exists at all three of Superior’s apartment complexes 
communities and is required by Town code, but execution can be improved. Composting does 
not yet exist at any of them, and is feasible, but only with the right structure. Commonalities 
between successful recycling programs include collection points that are as easily accessible as 
those for trash, continuous education and outreach, clear labeling and instructive signage, and a 
diligent supporting cast of people that underpin each step of the process. Left as a purely 
voluntary, opt-in effort, composting for apartment complexes in Superior is unlikely to reach 
meaningful participation. 
 
The report found that the most realistic path forward is incremental: strengthen recycling access 
and education first, support the introduction of composting in the one community that already 
wants it, and require space for organics in every new apartment complex project going forward. 
Resident interest is real. Of the 31 residents who responded to the study survey, 81% of those 
who answered said they would like composting service if it were offered, even though almost 
none of them compost today, mainly because the service does not exist.  
 
While this study examined both recycling and composting, and the findings point to recycling as 
the more urgent of the two. Residents reported confusion about recycling services they have, 
contamination severe enough that some no longer trust the system, and in several cases a belief 
that no recycling exists where they live despite the property providing it. Composting introduced 
on top of an unreliable recycling system is unlikely to succeed. In practice this argues for 
pursuing both together, with recycling improvements sequenced first, since most of the work that 
makes recycling function well (accurate signage, convenient container placement, an on-site 
champion, right-sized service) is the same work that makes composting viable later. 
 
Some recommendations for the Town include adapting signage guidance for complexes, 
adopting proximity standards for containers, supporting complexes in applying for grant funding, 
requiring organics collection for new development, and tracking Denver’s new ordinance rollout 
to learn from their experiences. There are also recommendations in the report that complexes can 
take if they are interested in advancing their offerings.

About 
Colorado Circular Communities (C3) is a program focused on supporting the state’s 
transition to a circular economy. C3 provides funding and support for organizations 
around the state to create a sustainable system for our waste streams. The Strategic 
Technical Expertise for the Public Sector (STEPS) program at C3 provides completely 
free consulting for communities to plan and act on their circularity goals. Local 
governments, tribes, and public K-12 school districts are all eligible for STEPS. 
The STEPS program has different levels of support depending on what a community 
may need. C3 can provide resources, conduct studies, design programs, and support 
any community in the way that they believe makes the most sense for them. The C3 
team has a list of subject matter experts to support the STEPS program, including 
Inclusive Design Group. 
Inclusive Design Group (IDG) is a local consulting partner who works between people 
and systems, bringing together educators, government agencies, nonprofits, funders, 
and community members to move from ideas to programs that operate in real 
communities. IDG combines community-centered design with analytical rigor to help 
partners navigate complexity and build solutions that last.

Executive Summary 
IDG partnered with the Town of Superior to study the current status of recycling and 
composting services at the Town's apartment complexes, as well as identify barriers 
and opportunities for waste service in those communities. This report answers those 
questions for three of the Town's apartment communities, Community #1, Community 
#2, and Community #3 (currently under construction), and offers general lessons that 
will apply to future apartment complex development in Superior. 
The short answer: recycling already exists at all three communities and is required by 
Town code but execution can be improved. Composting does not yet exist at any of 
them, and is feasible, but only with the right structure. Commonalities between 
successful recycling programs include collection points that are as easily accessible as 
those for trash, continuous education and outreach, clear labeling and instructive 
signage, and a diligent supporting cast of people that underpin each step of the 
process. Left as a purely voluntary, opt-in effort, composting for apartment 
complexes in Superior is unlikely to reach meaningful participation. Every successful 
composting program studied, in Colorado and around the country, shares a small set 
of traits: participation is close to mandatory rather than fully optional, someone (a 
"champion") is responsible for keeping the program running day to day, the cost is 
bundled into fees residents already pay rather than added on top, and the physical 
setup makes composting at least as convenient as throwing something away. 
Superior's three communities each present a different starting point. Community #2 
already has trash and recycling service and open interest in composting but faces real 
space and access constraints inside its 14 buildings. Community #1 is Superior's largest 
and most complex property, and residents there report the most confusion and 
dissatisfaction with current service, especially the required trash valet, well before 
composting enters the picture. Community #3, still under construction, is a preview of 
a harder problem: its design is already locked in, with no space reserved for 
composting, which means it will join Community #2 and Community #1 as a retrofit 
case once it opens. Given its location immediately next to Community #2, sharing a 
single downtown solution between the two properties (and possibly others) may be 
more efficient than solving each separately. The real design-stage opportunity is with

future apartment complex projects still in early planning, where reserving space now 
costs far less than retrofitting later. 
Resident interest is real. Of the 31 residents who responded to the study survey, 81% 
of those who answered said they would like composting service if it were offered, 
even though almost none of them compost today, mainly because the service does not 
exist. But interest alone does not build a program. Property managers, haulers, and 
other municipalities that have done this work all point to the same barriers: limited 
physical space, cost, pest and odor concerns, and the ongoing effort of educating a 
resident population that turns over constantly. 
This report walks through what IDG found in the research, the hauler and property 
manager interviews, the resident survey, and site visits to all three properties, and 
closes with a set of practical next steps the Town can act on. Nearby jurisdictions 
(Boulder, Lafayette, Denver) are actively building the playbook the Town can borrow 
from, and multiple funding sources exist for this kind of work. The most realistic path 
forward is incremental: strengthen recycling access and education first, introduce 
composting in the one community that already wants it, and require space for 
organics in every new apartment complex project going forward. 
Methods 
This study combined five activities to build a complete picture of the barriers and 
opportunities for apartment complex recycling and composting in Superior, consistent 
with the project scope. 
Desktop research. IDG reviewed how nearby cities (Denver, Boulder, Lafayette) and 
small municipalities around the country have approached apartment complex 
recycling and organics collection, including what worked, what didn't, and how 
programs were funded. 
Hauler interviews. IDG spoke with three of the haulers active in the Superior area 
(Hauler #1, Hauler #2, and Hauler #3), and with Hauler #4, a regional composting

Existing Conditions: Context in 
Superior 
The Superior Municipal Code describes trash hauler requirements in Chapter 6, Article 
V. Within this code, Section 6-5-70 describes requirements and exceptions for a single 
residential waste service provider area [Ref. 1]. 
State statute C.R.S. § 30-15-401(7.5) provides the Town authority to require single-
service collection for "residential waste services" but "only from sources other than 
industrial or commercial establishments or multifamily residences of eight or more 
units" [Ref. 2]. Therefore, the Town cannot require larger apartment complexes to be 
a part of the trash contract, which is limited to single-family and smaller apartment 
complexes only. Large apartment complexes seek and negotiate services 
independently and separate from the Town. It is important to recognize a balance 
between the levels of service offered within a neighborhood and their cost-
Page 31 of 298
 
www.coloradocircularcommunities.org 
 
8 
 
  
effectiveness; any additional fees passed along to residents increase the cost of 
living. Colorado's disposal economics shape what is realistic in Superior. Landfilling 
here is inexpensive relative to the cost of hauling, sorting, and processing recyclables 
and organics, so unlike regions where high disposal costs make diversion the cheaper 
choice, properties in Superior face no cost pressure pushing them toward recycling or 
composting on their own. This means programs that succeed elsewhere on economics 
alone will require deliberate effort and support to work locally. 
It is important to distinguish between what exists and what is required. All three 
communities in this study currently have recycling service, and Town code already 
requires recycling at new apartment developments. A gap identified in this study is 
not the absence of recycling but its usability: whether residents can find it, 
understand what goes in it, and trust that it works. Composting is the service that 
does not yet exist at any of the three properties. 
 
Property Manager (and Developer) Interviews 
Community #1 
IDG was unable to schedule a formal interview with a property manager at Community 
#1 despite weekly email outreach, phone call, and a personal visit to the residential 
services office, where contact information, messages, and interview question 
materials were provided to a receptionist. As an alternative, IDG was able to gather 
some insight from a leasing agent rather than the property manager directly, which 
limited the depth of some answers. Trash is collected via a valet service Sunday 
through Thursday, 6:00 to 9:00 PM, with recycling separated into blue bags. Hauler #2 
provides trash and recycling service. There is no composting. Residents may also drop 
off trash and recycling themselves at any of three community-edge locations. 
 
Community #2 
Waste collection at Community #2 currently works reasonably well. Hauler #1 handles 
the commercial/retail portion of the property, and Hauler #6 handles the residential 
apartment complex portion, with separate side-by-side indoor collection rooms for 
Page 32 of 298
 
www.coloradocircularcommunities.org

buildings along Main Street. Restricted access prevents residents from dumping in 
retail collection spaces, which may have different requirements, particularly for food 
service businesses. The property's central building uses trash chutes; the rest use 
exterior dumpsters. There is no valet service; residents dispose of their own trash. 
Collection occurs daily, and beyond ongoing concrete cracking at loading areas, no 
major service issues were reported. Trash and recycling are both provided; there is no

The property manager identified the primary barriers to adding composting as 
logistical: enclosed hallways and keycard access make collection more complex, and 
available space is limited, particularly with the property still filling toward its 
eventual capacity of roughly 800 to 900 residents. That said, the property manager 
described real resident interest and said composting could reasonably be positioned 
as a building amenity. If a workable logistical solution were identified, Community #2 
would likely be willing to participate. Community #2 currently employs two 
groundskeepers and three service personnel (two of whom live on-site), a staffing 
base that could plausibly support waste education and oversight, and management 
expressed openness to hosting resident engagement events.

Resident Engagement 
IDG worked with the Town of Superior to develop and distribute an online survey to 
residents of the Community #1 and Community #2 communities. Survey questions 
were prioritized jointly with Town staff and built into an online survey platform. The 
Town provided address lists and mailed information to residents inviting them to 
participate. It is important to note that both communities are large, with hundreds of 
residents each, and these results reflect the feedback of the relatively small (and 
likely less than statistically significant) subset of residents who chose to participate: 
31 total responses, 27 from Community #1 and 4 from Community #2. 
A note on what the combined results show: Looking at both communities together 
adds a layer of insight beyond what either community's results show on its own. Of the 
31 total respondents, 23 (74%) recycle, and among the 27 who answered the 
composting question, 22 (81%) said they would like composting service if it were 
offered, even though only one respondent currently composts (it is unclear where 
they take their compost). That gap, strong stated interest against almost no current 
access or participation, is the clearest single finding in the survey data. It suggests 
the barrier to composting in Superior's apartment complex communities right now is 
availability, not demand. Among the 30 respondents who don't currently compost, the 
Page 34 of 298
 
www.coloradocircularcommunities.org 
 
11 
 
  
top reasons cited were simply that the service isn't offered (27 respondents, or 90%), 
followed by not having anywhere to bring compost themselves and not having room to 
collect it at home (11 respondents, or 37%, for each), well ahead of concerns about 
pests, odor, or cost. Willingness to actively encourage neighbors to participate was 
also encouraging on the recycling side: 42% said yes outright and another 32% said 
they would if the program were well explained, meaning roughly three-quarters of 
respondents are open to helping a program succeed, not just using it themselves. 
 
Community #1 
Twenty-seven of the 31 total responses came from Community #1 households, 
representing about 6% of the community's residences. The results point to a 
significant need for improved communication about waste services generally, 
beginning with recyclable items and contamination. 
Most respondents described trash as collected via valet service, though a meaningful 
minority, 3 of 27, said they use the community dumpster directly instead, by choice. 
Recycling is technically available in the neighborhood, but only 19 of 27 respondents 
(70%) said their household recycles. Of the eight who don't, four (15% of all 
Community #1 respondents) said they lack access to recycling, and all four said they 
would like it if it were available, a direct conflict between what the property offers 
on paper and what residents experience. The remaining non-recyclers cited 
inconvenience (three respondents) or another reason (one respondent), consistent 
with a community layout where the nearest recycling dumpster can be nearly a 
quarter mile from some units. 
Confusion extends to how residents dispose of items correctly. One resident said 
flatly, "I take my recycling all the way to Boulder" rather than trust the on-site 
system, and another wrote plainly, "The recycling bin at Community #1 is not usable. 
There is trash mixed in and always full." Several respondents described trash 
contaminating the recycling dumpsters and asked for better guidelines, labels, and 
signage. A number of residents said they don't use the valet service, despite paying 
for it, because of lost confidence in proper sorting, animal interference with torn 
bags, or confusion about what the valet will and won't take. 
Page 35 of 298
 
www.coloradocircularcommunities.org 
 
12 
 
  
One resident requested a local drop-off site for hazardous or special items like 
batteries, mattresses, and chemicals, a need worth noting for future Town planning 
even outside the scope of this study. Another respondent raised a technical point 
worth flagging directly to the Town and any future compost partner: compostable 
bags, even plant-based ones, can introduce contamination that jeopardizes the 
compost's eligibility for organic-farming certification, meaning "clean" collection 
matters as much for the finished product as for the collection process itself. A 
separate resident suggested proactive education tools the Town could adopt directly, 
including specific guidance on rinsing containers and handling items like milk cartons 
and aluminum foil, and even suggested organizing resident tours of the Boulder 
recycling center. 
Several respondents also raised a related concern: because Community #1 is large and 
open to public access, non-resident dumping is suspected as a contributor to 
overflowing bins, and one resident noted plainly that locking the bins down isn't a 
realistic fix given how residents also need convenient access. This reinforces that the 
community's size and layout add a genuine enforcement challenge that a signage or 
education fix alone won't fully solve. 
 
Community #2 
Only four Community #2 households responded, representing less than 1% of the 
community's residents, so these results should be read as directional rather than 
representative. All four respondents said they participate in trash and recycling. None 
currently compost, citing lack of available service. However, two of the four (50%) 
said they would like it. Those who did not want composting cited cost, odor, and pest 
concerns. Both interested residents said they would encourage their neighbors to 
participate, and a third said they might, if the program were well understood. 
A consistent theme across Community #2 responses was a need for better resident 
education: incorrectly placed items around dumpsters, and general uncertainty about 
what belongs where. One resident wrote, "I think people don't know how to recycle, 
there are often unrecyclable things in the bin just for recycling. Posters would help, 
or mailers to remind people." Another added a useful nuance: residents who moved 
from communities with different (sometimes more demanding) sorting requirements

www.coloradocircularcommunities.org 
 
13 
 
  
may not realize the rules have changed, since what happens to recycling downstream 
varies by community, hauler, and processing facility. Any education effort should 
make clear not just what Superior's specific rules are, but that they can differ from 
what a new resident may already assume. 
One respondent also flagged an "ick factor" that exists even with current trash and 
recycling, noting dumpster lids are "dirty and unpleasant" and requesting more

would make the value of composting more tangible: "It would be even better if we 
had community gardens somewhere in Superior to repurpose some of the compost." 
Where a community garden isn't feasible, education on compost's other benefits, 
particularly drought and wildfire resilience given Superior's local risk profile, may 
serve a similar motivating purpose. 
 
Site Assessments 
Community #1 Site 
Community #1 spans approximately 150 acres and includes about 163 residential 
buildings totaling 438 units, along with amenity centers, maintenance structures, and 
offices. Despite the valet service, residents may also self-haul trash and recycling to 
any of three community-edge drop-off locations, at least two of which are monitored 
by video. 
1. East Site (near the primary eastern entrance) has one trash compactor and one 
to two recycling dumpsters. This location sits along a major entry point, is 
convenient but high-traffic and on a hill. The existing concrete pad appears 
able to accommodate at least one additional dumpster. 
2. North Site (northern loop dead ends) has one trash compactor and one 
recycling dumpster, in a lower-traffic area near a maintenance building, with 
modest additional space nearby. The split location may add to resident 
confusion; the recycling dumpsters are not indicated on the map provided by 
the property. 
Page 37 of 298
 
www.coloradocircularcommunities.org 
 
14 
 
  
3. South Site (southern loop, along the arterial) has two large open trash roll-offs 
and two recycling dumpsters, monitored by video, in the largest and most

sidewalk makes it more exposed to dumping by non-residents. 
Across all three sites, IDG observed that trash and recycling containers are visually 
similar (same blue color, distinguished only by a small recycling logo sticker), which 
likely contributes to the sorting confusion residents described in the survey. Any

building-level champion model, similar to the resident-championed approach 
described by Hauler #3 (see hauler interviews below), is likely more practical here 
than a single centralized solution. The property could also benefit from additional 
sorting and educational support from maintenance staff, whose buildings are located 
next to two of the three waste collection areas. 
 
Community #2 Site

three mixed-use buildings along Main Street have paired, enclosed, external-access 
waste rooms; and the remaining 10 buildings to the north use exterior parking-lot 
dumpsters at five locations. 
The enclosed rooms along Main Street have double doors and an L-shaped layout that 
gives residents plenty of room to load either dumpster, though it is unclear whether 
these rooms meet ventilation requirements that would matter for composting, and 
the concrete pads outside them are already showing wear, perhaps from dumpster

Ongoing Efforts Nearby (Denver, 
Boulder, Lafayette) 
Superior's neighbors are already working through many of the same questions the 
Town is asking. 
Boulder requires property owners (including apartment complexes) to provide on-site 
recycling and compost collection that is at least as convenient as trash service [Ref. 
3, 4]. Another stipulation of Boulder's municipal code is that property owners and 
managers must conduct training and distribute information about how to use the on-
site system established for collection of recyclables and compostables. This is 
required at least annually and within 30 days of move-in, or within 30 days of any 
substantive changes in the recycling or composting location or service offered at the 
property [Ref. 3]. This educational loop is essential for keeping residents updated, 
especially with a fluctuating university population. 
In practice, Boulder has run into real problems: many compostable bags are no longer 
accepted because they "contain and conceal" contamination, and standard 
compostable bags can cost up to four times as much as regular trash bags. Boulder has 
since paused enforcement of its compost requirement for commercial and apartment 
properties and special events, meaning no penalties currently apply for discontinuing 
compost service [Ref. 5]. 
Boulder provides a detailed Compost Rules FAQ page on the city's website, explaining 
that the region's only commercial compost manufacturer has changed what materials 
they accept [Ref. 5]. The latest guidance recommends skipping bags altogether, 
which is understandably difficult in some apartment complex scenarios where they 
can be functionally necessary for the collection system, hence the city's relaxed 
enforcement. A 2023 statement from the compost processor noted that while 90% of 
the compost material received was high-quality, about 10% was considered 
unacceptable because of contamination [Ref. 6]. The final compost product must 
meet strict regulatory, testing, and certification requirements. Although the 
prevalence and adoption of composting practices in the area has grown, so too has 
the amount of contamination. It is unclear if current contamination levels have since 
improved, but a general best practice is to avoid any plastic-replacement 
Page 40 of 298
 
www.coloradocircularcommunities.org 
 
17 
 
  
"compostable" items (such as bags, forks, and containers) whenever possible. Even 
though these items may be designed to break down, the time and temperatures 
required to do so are prohibitive, and their likenesses to their traditional counterparts 
make them hard to distinguish. One non-profit-based hauler in the Boulder area 
routinely engages the community, provides outreach services, and maintains an 
extensive public resource library for support materials and signage [Ref. 8]. 
The City of Boulder also provides printable signs for recycling, composting, and 
landfill items along with free services for laminated signs and labels or even free 
custom sign creation. Boulder also offers free educational materials and videos for 
training, and free metal signs with permanent installation for qualifying apartment 
complexes [Ref. 4, 7]. 
Lafayette recently put into effect (beginning in 2025) a solid waste ordinance 
requiring the adoption of recycling by commercial and apartment properties [Ref. 9]. 
This ordinance places responsibility on property owners and managers to provide 
collection locations and containers for recyclable materials that are at least as 
convenient as those for trash. Notably, the regulations include verbiage stating 
recycling containers "shall be of adequate size and number to prevent recyclables 
from being mixed with trash" and collected at least every other week. Further 
requirements are that apartment "customers must provide conspicuous signage at or 
on each trash and recycling container, clearly indicating the appropriate materials to 
be placed inside the container in accordance with the collector's requirements, to 
ensure proper sorting and minimize contamination." 
The ordinance identifies the city's intent to distribute an annual survey to commercial 
and apartment customers, gathering information on arrangements made for recycling 
collection services, partnerships with other customers, container sizes and collection 
frequencies, and whether organics collection services are provided. 
The city provides free (at its discretion): downloadable signage (bilingual), video and 
written educational materials, and other campaign materials [Ref. 10]. Regulated 
customer responsibilities include displaying city-provided or city-approved signage, 
routinely distributing educational materials (similar to Boulder), and distributing site-
specific information. 
Page 41 of 298
 
www.coloradocircularcommunities.org 
 
18 
 
  
Lafayette does not yet require composting in apartment communities. Its single-unit 
collection services utilize a three-cart program similar to Superior's own, with a 
voluntary opt-out that adds a $17.32 monthly fee to the resident's water bill, phasing 
to mandatory after three years [Ref. 11]. Lafayette has faced the same processing-
requirement issues as Boulder with its compost partner. 
Denver's new universal recycling and composting ordinance takes effect for all 
residents, including apartments, on September 1, 2026 [Ref. 12]. Denver modeled its 
ordinance on Austin's Universal Recycling Ordinance. This fall's rollout in Denver is 
worth watching closely, since it will be a significant real-world test case for 
apartment complex organics mandates in the region. Denver's ordinance requires 
apartment buildings to provide recycling and composting services to tenants, 
residents, employees, and contractors. Building owners and managers must also post 
clear signage and distribute education materials for proper sorting practices for each 
waste stream. Also stipulated are requirements for collection point access, capacity 
and collection frequency minimums, regular container cleaning, and signage and 
education standards [Ref. 13]. Denver property owners and managers must complete 
and submit a Waste Diversion Plan to the city in conjunction with residential building 
license renewals or any changes to service. The city is supporting compliance by 
providing free downloadable signage and has future plans to provide a Waste 
Diversion Plan Template and submission platform, step-by-step instructions for 
compliance, supporting materials, tips, best practices, and resources for residents 
and tenants to learn how to report compliance issues [Ref. 12, 14, 15]. These 
materials will be worth future investigation for potential adaptability for the Town of 
Superior. 
Hauler Interviews 
Colorado faces structural barriers that make participation in recycling and composting 
programs more challenging than other locations across the country. The local costs for 
transportation services carrying waste to landfills are relatively cheap compared to 
the costs of hauling (and sorting and processing) recyclable and compostable 
materials. The economics that incentivize the other streams elsewhere have not been 
Page 42 of 298
 
www.coloradocircularcommunities.org 
 
19 
 
  
integrated into landfill systems in Colorado. The absence of cost-driven pressure 
explains why apartment complex recycling and organics services have been rare in the 
region and why building these programs takes deliberate effort. Although several 
national examples of successful programs exist (see Appendix), it is important to 
recognize that some of their crucial cost-savings tactics and strategies may not be 
applicable locally. 
IDG interviewed three haulers active in the Superior area (Hauler #1, Hauler #2, and 
Hauler #3) and one regional composting provider (Hauler #4). A fifth conversation, 
with Hauler #5, the concierge (“valet”) trash provider used by some Community #1 
residents, could not be scheduled within the study period. 
Across all four conversations, the same barriers surfaced independent of who was 
answering: space, cost, and pests. Older Superior properties were rarely built with 
room for recycling, let alone composting, and even newer buildings typically planned 
only for recycling. For high-volume sites, haulers generally consider a paired trash and 
recycling compactor the strongest long-term solution: higher upfront cost, but it saves 
space, cuts noise, reduces pickup frequency, and keeps the recycling stream cleaner. 
Composting adds its own complications on top of these: it requires sealed, specialty 
trucks, frequent pickup to avoid odor and pest issues, and access to a limited number 
of processing sites, which drives up routing cost. For Superior, organics processors are 
primarily limited to just two viable options: a primary regional compost processor 
and, to some extent, a Denver-based provider (Hauler #4) [Ref. 6]. Smaller operators 
and sites are developing and are expected to grow along with the rollout of Denver's 
coming ordinance. See Appendix for more hauler interview details. 
Recommendations for Next Steps 
Based on the research, interviews, survey results, and site visits gathered for this 
study, IDG organizes its recommendations into two tracks: actions the Town can take 
directly, and actions the communities themselves (property owners, managers, and 
residents) are best positioned to take. 
Page 43 of 298
 
www.coloradocircularcommunities.org 
 
20 
 
  
Many of the recommendations include a working model from another jurisdiction or 
property. These are offered as starting templates the Town can adapt rather than 
build from scratch. 
One framing point applies across both tracks. This study examined both recycling and 
composting, and the findings point to recycling as the more urgent of the two. 
Residents reported confusion about recycling services they have, contamination 
severe enough that some no longer trust the system, and in several cases a belief that 
no recycling exists where they live despite the property providing it. Composting 
introduced on top of an unreliable recycling system is unlikely to succeed. In practice 
this argues for pursuing both together, with recycling improvements sequenced first, 
since most of the work that makes recycling function well (accurate signage, 
convenient container placement, an on-site champion, right-sized service) is the same 
work that makes composting viable later. 
 
Actions for the Town of Superior 
1. Adapt the Town's existing waste guidance and signage for apartment 
complexes, matched to each community's actual service chain. 
Superior already has guidelines and signage developed for the residential Three Cart 
Program, and a behavior change campaign [Ref. 16]. That material is the right 
starting point, but it may need adjustment prior to copying over to apartment 
complexes. Single-unit residential households in Superior are served under the Town 
contract; apartment complexes are not. Community #1, Community #2, and any 
future property may each use a different hauler, and each hauler potentially sends 
material to a different processing facility with its own accepted-materials list. 
This matters because what is accepted genuinely differs. As one hauler put it during 
the interviews, an item that is recyclable under one company's program is considered 
contamination under another's, even when the resident believes they are doing the 
right thing. Signage that reflects the Town's current residential program requires 
review by a property's actual hauler prior to use. 
The recommendation is therefore to produce guidance for apartment complexes that 
is accurate end to end, from the resident's bin through the specific hauler serving that 
Page 44 of 298
 
www.coloradocircularcommunities.org 
 
21 
 
  
property to the specific facility that processes the material, covering both recycling 
and organics where organics service exists. The Town should commit to updating it 
when a property changes haulers or a processor changes its rules. Superior's own 
Three Cart materials provide the visual template and plain-language approach; only 
the accepted-materials content needs to vary by property. 
Model to copy: Denver publishes separate resident-facing fact sheets for apartment 
and condo dwellers, distinct from its single-unit materials [Ref. 15]. Austin requires 
containers to be labeled with the stream, list the materials accepted at that location, 
and provide information in at least two languages [Ref. 17]. 
  
2. Map each apartment complex's full waste chain and keep it current. 
Before signage can be accurate, the Town needs to know what it is describing. A 
simple flow chart per community, showing what services exist (trash, recycling, 
compost), which hauler provides each, and which facility processes each stream, 
would take modest effort and would resolve several problems at once. It gives the 
Town the basis for accurate signage, a factual answer when residents ask what 
happens to their material, and an early warning when a property changes service 
without notice. 
The survey showed why this matters. Residents at one community reported having no 
recycling access at a property that does provide it, which means either the service is 
not reaching them or they have never been told it exists. It is essential to keep lists 
current for acceptable items, especially considering the frequency at which residents 
move and the previous practices they bring along. 
  
3. Support capacity-building work and open a funding conversation. 
Given that this study was itself funded through C3, the Town is well positioned to 
open a direct conversation about what comes next [Ref. 18]. C3 often supports small-
scale capacity-building work, which should shape how the Town frames any request. 
Several of the recommendations in this report fit that description naturally. 
Developing accurate apartment complex signage and guidance, mapping each 
Page 45 of 298
 
www.coloradocircularcommunities.org 
 
22 
 
  
community's waste chain, building out education materials, and establishing a 
champion training or support structure are all capacity-building activities rather than 
pilots. Framing a request around building the Town's and the communities' ongoing 
capability to run recycling and composting services well is likely to align best with 
what C3 supports. 
Grant programs generally require the entity that will own and operate the 
improvement to be the applicant. For improvements at a specific property, that 
means the property is the applicant, not the Town. The Town's role in those cases is 
support rather than funding: helping identify opportunities, providing application 
templates or background material, and acting as a point of contact. Where the Town 
would own and operate the asset itself, such as a Town-managed drop-off site, the 
Town can apply directly. Direct Town funding of property improvements, whether 
through a rebate or a locally administered grant program, remains an option worth 
considering, but would likely need to extend beyond apartments to businesses and 
other properties facing the same space constraints. 
 
 4. Adopt a proximity standard for collection containers at apartment complexes. 
Several residents may have to walk a substantial distance (or drive) to reach a 
recycling dumpster within their community, or skip recycling entirely because trash is 
closer. This is one of the most consistent findings across the research: when recycling 
or composting is less convenient than trash, most residents choose trash. A distance 
requirement addresses this without prescribing what containers a property must use. 
Model to copy: Austin requires outdoor recycling and composting containers to be 
within 25 feet of trash containers, and indoor bins to be grouped together [Ref. 17]. 
Boulder's ordinance uses a similar principle, requiring collection areas to be at least 
as convenient to residents as trash [Ref. 3]. 
  
5. Require space for organics collection in new apartment complex construction 
through a code update. 
Town code requires recycling at new apartment developments. Extending that 
requirement to organics would close the remaining gap, and a mechanism to consider 
Page 46 of 298
 
www.coloradocircularcommunities.org 
 
23 
 
  
is code amendment. Once the requirement is in code, compliance is verified through 
the site plan review process. 
At least one additional apartment project is in early planning in Superior, and more 
are likely. Reserving space and basic infrastructure at the design stage costs a 
fraction of retrofitting later. Community #3 illustrates the cost of missing this 
window: because organics were not required when its design was set, it has 
compactors for trash and recycling but no clearly defined organics provision, and will 
open as a retrofit case. 
The update should also address recycling capacity, not just presence. Existing 
properties may have recycling that is undersized relative to demand, which is itself a 
contamination driver when bins overflow. 
Model to copy: Hauler #3 described an "equal space" approach used regionally, where 
new construction must include space for all streams. Bellingham, Washington offers 
the cautionary version: newer dense developments ended up with more bins than 
space, prompting the city to work on right-sizing solutions after the fact [Ref. 19, 20]. 
  
6. Support a shared downtown drop-off serving Community #2 and Community #3 
(and potentially the downtown public). 
These two properties sit immediately adjacent on Main Street. Rather than solving 
access twice, one well-placed community drop-off could serve both, and would avoid 
the hardest problem at each, which is moving material into or out of individual 
buildings.  
This would need a champion among the properties themselves. The site would most 
likely sit on one of their properties rather than on Town land, and that property 
would carry responsibility for hosting, access, and day-to-day management. The 
Town's role is best understood as support: convening the conversation, helping think 
through siting and logistics, and providing backbone for a funding application. If the 
Town were to own and operate a drop-off site directly, it could apply for funding 
itself, but that is a different and larger commitment than facilitating a shared 
arrangement between two willing properties. 
Page 47 of 298
 
www.coloradocircularcommunities.org 
 
24 
 
  
Model to copy: Manchester, Connecticut built five neighborhood drop-off sites at 
locations residents already visit, for $20,000 to $25,000 total startup [Ref. 21]. 
Washington, DC installed 30 access-controlled smart bins targeted at apartment 
complex areas [Ref. 22]. Hauler #4 already operates similar models in Louisville and 
Broomfield, each roughly ten minutes from Superior. 
  
7. Promote household collection containers and connect finished compost to a 
visible local use. 
The barrier residents cited most often for not composting, after the absence of 
service itself, was having nowhere to collect scraps inside the home. A simple 
countertop or under-sink pail removes that barrier at minimal cost and gives residents 
a tangible signal that the service is real. Note that compostable bags are often not 
accepted at processing facilities: a primary regional processor no longer accepts 
them, and Boulder's experience shows that bags residents believe are acceptable 
become a significant contamination problem [Ref. 5, 6]. Containers should be easy to 
rinse and reuse instead. 
The second half of the incentive is harder in an apartment setting. Programs 
elsewhere sustain participation by returning finished compost to residents, but 
apartment residents typically have no yard or garden to use it in, so the standard 
"close the loop" reward does not translate directly. Two approaches can substitute. 
First, the Town could support development of a community garden accessible to 
apartment residents, which gives finished compost a destination and creates a visible 
reason the program exists. One Community #2 resident raised exactly this idea 
unprompted, suggesting community gardens in Superior to repurpose the compost. 
Second, where a garden is not available, the Town should connect compost to 
benefits residents already care about locally. Compost improves soil moisture 
retention, supports regrowth in burn areas, and contributes to landscape resilience 
against fire, all of which carry particular weight in Superior given recent local history. 
Compost applied in Town parks, medians, open space, or fire recovery areas gives 
residents a concrete answer to what their participation produces, even when they 
never handle the finished product themselves. 
Page 48 of 298
 
www.coloradocircularcommunities.org 
 
25 
 
  
Model to copy: Takoma Park provides a free 2.5-gallon kitchen bin and lets 
participants collect finished compost annually [Ref. 23, 24]. Prince George's County 
sells its finished product commercially and reinvests the revenue in the program [Ref. 
25]. On the messaging side, EPA and LA Compost both document compost's role in soil 
moisture retention and post-fire recovery [Ref. 26, 27]. 
  
8. Consider a rebate program for enclosure modifications and containers. 
Cost and space were the barriers property managers raised most often. A targeted 
rebate addresses the one-time capital cost, which properties are least willing to 
absorb, while leaving ongoing service costs with the property. Extending eligibility to 
recycling capacity upgrades, not just organics, would address the overflow and under-
sizing problems residents reported. This suggestion would likely need broader 
eligibility considerations than just apartment communities. 
Model to copy: Austin's Zero Waste Business Rebate offers eligible apartment 
properties up to $3,000, explicitly covering new enclosures, concrete slab work for 
larger carts, resident bins, and certified compostable bags [Ref. 17, 28]. 
 
9. Pursue outside funding, recognizing that the applicant depends on who owns 
the improvement. 
The Recycling Partnership offers grants for recycling infrastructure and education and 
is generally accessible to smaller applicants. Traditionally, federal programs including 
USDA's Composting and Food Waste Reduction cooperative agreements and EPA's 
Recycling Education and Outreach and SWIFR grants have supported comparable work, 
though federal grants carry heavier reporting and management burdens and their 
availability is currently uncertain.  
Model to copy: Prince George's County funded its initial program partly through an 
EPA grant before it became self-sustaining [Ref. 29]. In both Mamaroneck and 
Harrisonburg, state grant funding arrived after the community demonstrated 
voluntary traction, which argues for starting small and using early results to 
strengthen later applications [Ref. 30].

10. Use hauler licensing as a lever for signage and education consistency 
Haulers operating in Superior are required to be licensed by the Town and are 
required to report waste data through the Re-TRAC system. Licensing is an existing, 
established mechanism the Town controls, and one that reaches every hauler serving 
every property without the Town becoming involved in any private service contract. 
A licensing condition requiring haulers to maintain current, accurate signage and 
customer education materials for the properties they serve would provide support to 
residents by clarifying precisely what their specific hauler accepts. It places the 
responsibility with the party that directly knows the answer, and it benefits the 
hauler as well by reducing contaminated and rejected loads. 
  
11. Track Denver's rollout and apply the lessons. 
Denver's universal recycling and composting ordinance takes effect September 1, 2026 
and includes apartment properties. It will be a significant large-scale regional test of 
the exact problem Superior is studying, covering both streams. The first year of 
Denver's experience with contamination, space, and enforcement will be directly 
transferable [Ref. 12, 14]. 
  
12. Anticipate developer and operator pushback on any future requirement. 
If the Town moves toward requiring recycling or composting at apartment complexes, 
expect resistance from large operators and developers on space and housing-cost 
grounds. This is not a reason to avoid the conversation, but it should shape how any 
requirement is designed, phased, and paired with support. Lafayette's three-year 
phase-in before mandatory compliance is one approach [Ref. 11]. 
 
Actions for Apartment Communities 
These recommendations apply to any apartment property in Superior, existing or 
future, regardless of size, hauler, or configuration.

www.coloradocircularcommunities.org

Some of these actions require the property owner or manager specifically, such as 
right-sizing trash service or modifying an enclosure. Others are open to residents 
directly, or are things residents can effectively advocate for with their property 
management. Residents are particularly well positioned to serve as the waste 
champion described below, to organize or help run sorting events, to raise signage 
and container problems when they see them, and to ask their property to look into a 
waste audit or funding opportunity. Several survey respondents in this study already 
volunteered ideas along these lines, which suggests the interest is there where the 
opportunity is made clear. 
  
13. Identify and support a waste champion at the property. 
This is the highest-value, lowest-cost action available and it does not require the 
Town's involvement. Research across nearly every successful program points to the 
same finding: a motivated person inside the building predicts success better than the 
property manager's own enthusiasm. That person can be a resident, a leasing agent, 
or maintenance staff. Their role is to answer questions, keep signage current, and 
notice problems before they become patterns. Properties with on-site staff are 
particularly well positioned, but a resident volunteer works equally well. 
Model to copy: Hauler #3's leadership program recruits a resident or on-site staff 
member, provides training and tools, and maintains an open line to address 
participation and contamination as they emerge. NYCHA, serving over 500,000 
residents, built its engagement strategy around resident champions [Ref. 33]. 
  
14. Contact C3 for assistance with bins and waste enclosure expansion. 
Property owners and managers do not need to wait for the Town to act as 
intermediary. C3 connects communities with grants, funding, and technical assistance 
specifically to promote waste diversion, and physical capacity improvements such as 
purchasing recycling or organics containers and expanding or modifying waste 
enclosures fall squarely within that purpose [Ref. 18]. 
This directly addresses a barrier property managers raised consistently in interviews, 
which is the upfront capital cost of adding capacity rather than the ongoing service 
Page 51 of 298
 
www.coloradocircularcommunities.org 
 
28 
 
  
cost. A property that has identified what it needs, ideally informed by a waste audit 
as described below, is in a strong position to make a specific request. Properties 
should approach C3 with a clear description of the capacity gap and the physical 
change that would close it, whether that is additional containers, an enclosure 
extension, or a concrete pad to accommodate carts. C3 also provides funding for 
education and outreach materials. 
  
15. Conduct a waste audit before changing anything physical. 
Before a property concludes it has no space, it should establish what it is throwing 
away. Generally, properties are often over-serviced on trash relative to real volume, 
and the audit is what makes right-sizing possible. It also reveals whether recycling 
capacity is genuinely undersized or simply poorly located, and produces the specific 
numbers that strengthen any funding request. 
  
Model to copy: Thurston County, Washington promotes exactly this, and haulers may 
perform a service level analysis at no charge [Ref. 34]. Guidance compiled by the 
National Apartment Association, drawing on University of Louisville research, suggests 
roughly one-quarter cubic yard of container capacity per three residents weekly as a 
benchmark [Ref. 35]. 
  
16. Right-size trash service and redirect the space and savings to recycling and 
organics. 
This is the most common way properties expand service without new construction or 
net new cost. Working with haulers can optimize the strategy for a community's waste 
collection. Once material is diverted, trash fills more slowly, so a property can 
downsize a dumpster or reduce pickup frequency and place additional recycling or 
compost capacity in the space freed up. 
Model to copy: Over half of Compost Crusader's commercial customers reduced 
landfill pickups or receptacle size after starting composting [Ref. 36]. Thurston 
County's right-sizing program formalizes the process [Ref. 34].

17. Make containers unmistakably distinguishable, using signage accurate to the 
property's own hauler. 
Where trash and recycling containers look similar, residents cannot reliably tell them 
apart, and contamination follows. Large, high-contrast signage and clear color 
differentiation is often a same-week fix. Critically, the accepted-materials list on that 
signage must reflect what the property's specific hauler and processing facility 
accept, not a generic list or one borrowed from a different program. Residents who 
moved from other communities frequently apply their previous community's rules, 
which is a common and avoidable source of contamination. 
Model to copy: The Town's existing materials are a good starting point but need to be 
verified by the haulers (and potentially updated) for each community [Ref. 16]. Best 
practices research emphasizes that clear, consistent, multilingual signage matters 
most where resident turnover is high [Ref. 37]. 
  
18. Ensure recycling is at least as convenient as trash. 
Where trash is collected at the door but recycling requires a walk across the property, 
residents recycle less. Any doorstep or valet collection service should cover recycling 
on the same terms as trash. Where that is not possible, recycling containers should be 
sited to minimize the distance gap. This asymmetry was a practical barrier cited in 
the resident survey. 
  
19. Where a valet or concierge service is used, keep sorting responsibility with 
residents and communicate its rules clearly. 
Valet service can help or hurt depending on design. It helps when it accepts recycling 
on the same terms as trash and residents trust that streams stay separate. It hurts 
when residents believe material is being combined, when bag requirements create an 
additional cost or step, or when service is inconsistent enough that material sits out 
and attracts wildlife. Properties using these services should confirm what the service 
does and does not accept, communicate it plainly and repeatedly, and structure the 
Page 53 of 298
 
www.coloradocircularcommunities.org 
 
30 
 
  
service so residents still separate materials themselves, with the valet handling 
transport only. Separating pickup times and schedules according to waste stream 
could be explored with valet services as a means of simplifying services while 
reducing contamination. 
  
20. Treat education as a permanent program, not a one-time launch. 
Every hauler interview, every case study, and the survey responses themselves point 
to the same conclusion: residents move in and out constantly, and even long-time 
residents may carry incorrect assumptions from a previous community. What is 
accepted, and how it should be sorted, varies by hauler and processor and can change 
over time. Ongoing communication (signage, mailers, reminders at lease renewal) 
needs to be built into any program from day one, not treated as a launch task to 
complete and move past. 
  
21. Start new services with movable equipment rather than construction. 
For properties that cannot build anything permanent, the lowest-risk entry point is a 
locked, wheeled bin placed near existing containers. It requires no permitting or 
construction, just a flat surface with truck access, and it can be relocated or removed 
if it does not work as intended. Locked containers also help to address some pest 
concerns and contamination risks. Properties should involve their haulers before 
making any changes to ensure aspects like access, safety, and truck maneuverability 
are considered first. 
Model to copy: Compost Crusader delivers a locked wheeled bin, gives participating 
residents access keys, and conducts a site assessment to determine placement before 
launch [Ref. 36]. Locally, Hauler #4 provides similar services. Collective Resource 
Compost notes pickup frequency scales to participation, so a low-participation 
building can have the tote swapped less often [Ref. 38]. 
  
22. Use competitions and visible recognition to build participation.

Building-level or floor-level competition is a low-cost way to convert a passive service 
into an active one. Roughly three-quarters of survey respondents said they would or 
might encourage neighbors to participate, which means a social base for this already 
exists in Superior's communities. 
Model to copy: A Seattle apartment complex runs a floor-versus-floor diversion 
competition with a lobby leaderboard, the winning floor receiving a monthly 
communal dinner. San Francisco building managers send monthly newsletters 
recognizing top recyclers [Ref. 37]. 
  
23. Host hands-on sorting events in partnership with the hauler. 
Signage tells residents what to do; a live event lets them practice it. Partnering with 
the property's hauler to host periodic community events, built around food and drink 
that generate real waste, turns education into immediate action. Residents sort 
actual materials on the spot, with the hauler present to answer questions and correct 
mistakes in the moment. The Town also has a trained Green Team volunteer corps 
that could support events like these. 
The value comes from deliberately including the items residents get wrong. A pizza 
and wing night, for example, produces clean and grease-stained boxes (which often 
belong in different streams), crusts and bones, paper plates, aluminum cans, plastic 
bottles, and "compostable" utensils that may or may not be accepted by the property's 
specific processor. These edge cases are exactly where contamination originates, and 
they are far easier to explain with the item in hand than on a sign. Small incentives 
for correct sorting, such as gift cards or a credit on a utility or amenity bill, add 
friendly competition and give residents a reason to show up. 
This approach also serves a second purpose: it puts the hauler in direct contact with 
residents, which surfaces service problems the property manager may not hear about, 
and gives the hauler a chance to explain what actually happens to material 
downstream, addressing the trust gap several survey respondents described. 
Model to copy: Mamaroneck found face-to-face contact at the farmers' market to be 
its single most effective outreach tool, describing it as the ideal way to demonstrate 
how the program works and connect with neighbors who then spread the word

themselves [Ref. 30]. Hauler #3's approach pairs regular education with direct, on-
the-ground support for the specific questions that come up at each property. 
 
24. Consider waste data sharing when negotiating with a hauler. 
Contracting is entirely the property's decision, but properties that want visibility into 
how their program is performing may find it useful to discuss data sharing when 
entering or renewing a hauler agreement. Contamination levels are a practical way to 
gauge whether education efforts are working over time, and to target future outreach 
at the specific items showing up in the wrong stream. Rejected or contaminated loads 
typically carry additional cost that the property absorbs, so there is a direct financial 
interest in tracking this. This data could even feed back into hands-on sorting events, 
described above.

Hauler Interview Details 
IDG interviewed three haulers active in the Superior area (Hauler #1, Hauler #2, and 
Hauler #3) and one regional composting provider (Hauler #4). A fifth conversation, 
with Hauler #5, the concierge trash provider used by some Community #1 residents, 
could not be scheduled within the study period. Details from the individual interviews 
and experiences are shared below. 
 
Hauler #1 
Hauler #1 offers trash, recycling, and organics collection, and was candid about the 
operational realities of servicing apartment complexes. A few points stood out: 
Apartment complex recycling and organics is an open market in Colorado, meaning a 
property can choose not to participate, and most management companies don't, 
because it costs more. Space is the single biggest barrier: older properties often have 
no room for recycling, let alone organics, and even newer properties usually only 
planned for recycling. 
Organics attract pests (rodents, raccoons, and even bears), a concern echoed 
independently by Community #2's property manager and by Community #1 residents in 
the survey. Standard plastic poly-carts are the common low-cost solution, but animals 
chew through them, especially the lids, which cost $20 to replace on a $55 cart, an 
ongoing expense for large properties. 
For high-volume properties, Hauler #1 considers a paired trash and recycling 
compactor the "ideal solution": higher upfront cost, but it saves space, reduces noise, 
keeps the recycling stream cleaner (since a compactor doesn't care whether boxes are 
flattened correctly), and lowers long-term hauling costs by cutting pickup frequency. 
Hauler #1 strongly discourages split-body trucks and split bins for handling multiple 
waste streams at once, citing both worker safety and resident confusion (residents 
often assume, incorrectly, that split loads end up in the same place anyway).

www.coloradocircularcommunities.org

One Hauler #1 representative summarized the tension local governments face plainly: 
requiring recycling or composting infrastructure is the Town's best lever, but 
developers will push back, arguing that space dedicated to waste infrastructure is 
space taken from parking or housing, which raises costs. Balancing sustainability goals 
against affordability is an ongoing challenge with no easy answer. 
Hauler #1's bottom line: education is the constant need. Residents dump based on 
available space, not according to the correct stream, and since apartment populations 
turn over constantly, that education effort never really ends. 
 
Hauler #2 
Hauler #2 currently offers trash and recycling directly and works with third-party 
partners for composting rather than running it themselves. Their key concerns mirror 
Hauler #1's: composting means an entirely separate truck route and driver, which is 
expensive to add, and the limited number of processing sites in the region 
complicates routing and drives up cost further. 
Hauler #2 noted that apartment recycling contamination is a persistent, specific 
problem: a dumpster located even 10 feet farther away than the trash dumpster 
reliably sees more trash mixed in. Their experience is that communities often start a 
recycling program and later discontinue it once the contamination problem becomes 
unmanageable without more investment in education. 
On the positive side, Hauler #2 is opening a new, state-of-the-art materials recovery 
facility (MRF) in the region within the next year, built for residential volume and 
designed to better handle contamination, which should make recycling economics 
somewhat more favorable in the region going forward. 
Their read on resident behavior: roughly 80% of residents are willing to participate in 
some kind of waste diversion, but of that group, only about 30% understand what 
belongs in which bin, and only 20% want both recycling and composting. This 
underscores that willingness and understanding are two different things, and a 
program needs to address both.

Hauler #3 
Hauler #3 is a mission-driven hauler serving commercial and apartment properties 
with dedicated recycling and composting service, though not standard trash service or 
single-family residential. 
Hauler #3's signature contribution to this conversation is its leadership program, a

ramping up over a period of months rather than launching all at once, and noted 
they've previously offered composting incentives, including gift cards through a 
Boulder County incentive program, to residents at low-income housing sites.
