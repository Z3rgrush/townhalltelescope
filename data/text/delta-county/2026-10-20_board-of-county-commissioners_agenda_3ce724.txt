County Assessor Jolene George – Presentation of the 2026 Delta County Property Assessment 28 
Study Findings: 29 
Jolene explained that she wanted to present 2026 Property Assessment Findings.  This is annual 30 
property assessment audit that is done for all county assessor’s across the state.  It was initiated back 31 
1982 as part of the Gallagher Amendment.  This is a means of achieving statewide equity and uniformity 32 
in valuation for all county offices.   33 
 34 
Jolene referred the Board to page six of the report.  The property assessment study looks at two primary 35 
areas.  One is the procedural review which identifies if they are following state law and constitution on 36 
their methodology used to value property.  The second part of the study is a statistical analysis to make 37 
sure that their values are uniform and applied in a consistent manner across all property.  Jolene said on 38 
page six is the residential study findings and at the top of the page is the measures they are measuring 39 
such as median sale ratio; coefficient of dispersion and the far column shows the result of pass or 40 
sufficient.  She reported that the median sales ratio needs to be between .95 and 1.05 for the residential 41 
class of property and it shows that Delta County is at .994 so they are within that range.   42 
 43 
Jolene stated that this report goes through and identifies all the different measures and what the results 44 
were for each property class.  She referred the Board to page seven that talks about the coefficient of 45 
dispersion.  Coefficient of dispersion means how much dispersion is there about the mean of the median 46 
sales ratio for the sample size.  The median sale for residential is .994, how much of that goes outside of 47 
that with other sales.  There can only be so much dispersion in the data group.  Are your numbers tight or 48 
are they not?  They are well within the range with the coefficient of dispersion.   49 
 50 
Jolene reported that Market Trending, price related differential and price related bias are a few other 51 
measures that they use.  Those will identify if you are valuing properties uniformly based on value. Lower 52 
valued properties are being valued correctly or higher valued properties is being valued correctly and 53 
there is not bias one way or the other. 54 
 55 
Jolene said ultimately, they don’t value just the properties that have sold, they value everything in the 56 
county.  There is the sold and unsold analysis that is performed.  This looks at the unsold which is the 57 
larger number of properties in the county and ensures that they are valuing them the same way they are 58 
valuing the sold properties.  That is the equity across the entire class.  Commissioner Fuller clarified that 59 
unsold does not necessarily mean they were on the market and didn’t sell, they are just not for sale.  60 
Jolene said it didn’t sell during that time frame.  Jolene stated that for each class these performance 61 
measures are done and that is what this report conveys.  They did come back in full compliance with the 62 
states requirements.  She is always happy to report on that.  This is a team effort and the appraisers put a 63 
lot of time and effort training and making sure they are doing the best they can for the people of Delta 64 
County.  Jolene added that they actually enjoy this work and it is important work.   65

Speed Limit Review and Recommendation Report: Fire Mountain Road 
Recommendation 
Based on a comprehensive review of roadway characteristics, access density, stopping sight distances, winter 
climate patterns, and verified traffic speed data, it is recommended to reduce the speed limit on the entire 
length of Fire Mountain Road—from Highway 133 through and extending 0.25 miles past Cedar Lane—from 35 
mph (downhill direction) to a uniform 25 mph for both directions of travel. 
Current Roadway Characteristics Fire Mountain Road is classified as a Local Access Road. The overall roadway 
topography presents factors that influence optimal travel speeds across its entire length. 
 Right-of-Way: The road has a total 60-foot right-of-way requirement, with the existing right-of-way 
varying between 30 and 60 feet. 
 Grade and Elevation: The road maintains an average slope of 7%, with a total elevation change of 
approximately +294 feet from Highway 133 to Cedar Lane before continuing north. 
 Curvature: The roadway features multiple curves, including a 103-foot radius centerline curve located 
1,118 feet west of Cedar Lane, alongside others ranging from 311 to 638 feet. 
Access Point Density 
The frequency of access points requires sufficient driver reaction time and appropriate speed management. 
 There are 19 distinct access points along the section between Highway 133 and Cedar Lane alone. 
 The average distance between driveways is 274 feet. 
 The shortest distance between driveways is 5.28 feet, which falls below standard optimal spacing 
guidelines. 
 While 18 of the 19 driveways meet the 10-foot offset distance requirement, the high density of access 
points along a 7% slope is a key factor in establishing uniform corridor-wide operating speeds. 
Sight Distance, Climate, and Maintenance Considerations 
Stopping sight distance compliance varies based on vehicle speed, roadway slope, and environmental 
conditions. 
 Stopping Sight Distance Definition: Stopping sight distance is the total distance a driver needs to see 
ahead in order to recognize a potential hazard—such as a vehicle pulling out of a driveway—and bring 
their car to a complete stop safely. It accounts for both the distance traveled while the driver reacts and 
the actual distance required to brake. 
 Impact of Roadway Slope (Grade): Roadway grade plays a direct role in braking performance. When 
traveling downhill on a steep 7% grade, gravity resists the vehicle's braking force, significantly increasing

 Climate & Winter Temperatures: Paonia experiences approximately 60 days of measurable precipitation 
annually, receiving an average of 42 inches of snowfall over a six-month winter season. Winter daytime 
highs typically reach slightly above freezing, while overnight lows drop well below freezing. This 
introduces daily freeze-thaw cycles that can contribute to ice formation on the roadway surface. 
 Maintenance Operations: The road and bridge district actively maintains local roads using plowing and 
sand applications to enhance traction. Because sand improves traction but does not chemically melt ice, 
speed limits must account for standard winter driving conditions where snow, ice, or packed road 
surfaces are present between scheduled maintenance passes. 
 Sight Distance Evaluation: Under dry conditions at a 35-mph limit, 9 out of 19 driveways (47%) meet 
optimal stopping sight distance requirements. In wet conditions at 35 mph, this margin narrows, with 5 
out of 19 driveways (26%) meeting stopping sight distance parameters. Reducing the speed limit to 25 
mph improves the safety margin, bringing 9 out of 19 driveways (47%) into compliance under wet 
conditions and 12 out of 19 (63%) under dry conditions. 
Traffic Volume and Speed Data Analysis Traffic data highlights a significant behavioral conflict caused by the 
current split speed limits (25 mph uphill vs. 35 mph downhill): 
 Uphill Traffic (25 mph Limit): Uphill compliance is exceptionally low. Between 85% and 90% of all uphill 
drivers exceed the 25-mph limit, carrying momentum from Highway 133 up the 7% grade. 85th 
percentile speeds reach 36 to 39 mph, with maximum speeds recorded up to 52 mph. 
 Downhill Traffic (35 mph Limit): Downhill traffic naturally accelerates as it descends the grade. At the 
upper portion of the hill, only 6.5% of drivers exceed 35 mph. However, near the bottom of the 
corridor, gravity pushes 37.5% of downhill drivers over 35 mph, with 85th percentile speeds reaching 
38 mph and maximum speeds hitting 53 mph. 
Conclusion The current 35 mph downhill speed limit does not provide optimal stopping sight distances for the 
roadway's 7% slope, curve radii, and access density. Furthermore, applying split limits or inconsistent limits 
across different segments of Fire Mountain Road creates confusing expectations for motorists, encouraging non-
compliance in the uphill direction and accelerating speeds downhill. Paonia's winter weather conditions and 
freeze-thaw cycles further reduce the available margin for driver reaction times. Implementing a uniform 25 
mph speed limit across the entire length of Fire Mountain Road will improve overall sight distance compliance, 
establish consistent expectations for motorists, and better align operating speeds with the corridor's geometric 
and environmental constraints.

A RESOLUTION IN SUPPORT OF AMENDMENT 83, THE CONSTITUTIONAL 
RIGHT TO HUNT AND FISH  
 
WHEREAS, Amendment 83 recognizes hunting and fishing as the preferred means of managing 
fish and wildlife populations while preserving the authority of the State of Colorado to regulate 
hunting, fishing, and wildlife management when necessary for sound scientific wildlife 
conservation and management, public safety, and the preservation of future hunting and fishing 
opportunities; and  
 
WHEREAS, the Delta County Board of County Commissioners recognizes hunting and fishing as 
important components of Colorado’s wildlife conservation system, rural heritage, outdoor 
traditions, and local economy, and believes decisions regarding wildlife management should 
continue to be informed by sound science and professional wildlife management; and 
 
WHEREAS, the Board believes protecting hunting and fishing for future generations strengthens 
Colorado’s long-standing commitment to wildlife conservation and helps ensure that these 
traditions remain an important part of the state’s heritage.  
 
NOW, THEREFORE, BE IT RESOLVED, that the Delta County Board of County Commissioners 
herby expresses its support for Amendment 83, the Constitutional Right to Hunt and Fish, and 
encourages the citizens of Delta County and the State of Colorado to Support its passage at the 
November 3, 2026 General Election.
