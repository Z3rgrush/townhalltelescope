OFFICE OF THE MAYOR 
PROCLAMATION 
National Law Enforcement Records and Support Personnel Week 
November 9-13, 2026 
WHEREAS, dedicated members of the Canon City Police Department Records 
Management Unit and Support Personnel serve the citizens of Canon City and provide 
them with vital services; 
WHEREAS, law enforcement records personnel are crucial to assisting the Canon City 
Police Department with identifying, pursuing, and processing suspects; and, 
WHEREAS, these professionals continually use their expertise and experience in 
maintaining Criminal Justice records, statistics, and improving strategies; and, 
WHEREAS, the efficiency of the qualified and dedicated personnel who staff the Canon 
City Police Department Records Management Unit is materially influenced by the 
people's attitude and understanding of the importance of the work they perform, and, 
WHEREAS, Law Enforcement Records and Support Personnel serving the City of Canon 
City have exhibited professionalism, efficiency, and compassion during the performance 
of their essential duties this past year; and 
NOW, THEREFORE, I, Phil Lund, Mayor the City of Canon City , on behalf of the City 
Council and the residents of this community do hereby recognize November 9-13, 2026, 
as National Law Enforcement Records and Support Personnel Week to thank the 
personnel in our community, as well as communities across the nation, for their service. 
Dated this 2nd day of November, 2026. 
Phil Lund, Mayor

II. 1 Housing and Growth Context
Each weekday morning, nearly two of every three people who work in Cañon City drive in from
somewhere else. 3 The City is the county seat of Fremont County and the region’ s center for employment, 
retail, services, and tourism, yet much of its workforce lives beyond its limits. That commute is the
clearest sign of a housing market that has not kept pace with the economy it serves. 
The City held 17, 421 residents in 2022, 35% of the County’ s population. 4 Cañon City’ s household
population grew ~ 0. 65% per year from 2011 to 2022, just over half Colorado’ s 1. 15% pace. 5 Fremont
County’ s natural increase has been negative every year since 2010. 6 In other words, deaths outnumber
births, and net migration is the County’ s only source of growth. 
The City’ s housing stock is the oldest in the region. Cañon City’ s median home was built in 1973, 13 years
older than Colorado’ s 1986 median. 7 In fact, 24% of its units predate 1939, against 7% statewide. 
Production has not caught up. The City holds ~ 38% of the County’ s household population but issued ~ 21% 
of its residential permits from 2010 to 2022, averaging ~ 20 new single - family homes per year. 8 An
outdated housing stock is an uncompetitive housing stock. 
The rental market is thinner still. Cañon City has ~ 665 multifamily units across 15 properties, just 30% of
its occupied rental units, and 10 of those properties are subsidized or income restricted. 9 The City has no
Class A apartments, the newest and best- amenitized tier of rental housing. The share of Cañon City jobs
held by nonresidents is nearly double the 34% countywide rate. Workers are choosing to live elsewhere, 
and the housing stock gives them little reason to move closer.

II. 2 Local Conditions That Require Care
Two conditions set Cañon City apart from a typical assessment community. First, Fremont County houses
15 correctional facilities, including the nation’ s only federal supermax prison. Group quarters, the Census
term for congregate living such as prisons, ac count for 8. 1% of the City’ s population and 24. 5% of the
unincorporated County’ s. 10 Census household, income, tenure, and vacancy measures mislead unless
those congregate populations are removed. The 2024 Study already built the household- population
method this assessment will update. 
Second, the City’ s growth capacity sits in a few large sites. Four Mile Ranch, the Holy Cross Abbey campus, 
the Riverfront Mixed Use District, and five Priority Annexation Areas inside the Urban Growth Boundary
each raise different infrastructure and fiscal questions. Housing need and growth strategy cannot be
analyzed separately in Cañon City. This proposal therefore integrates the Base Bid and Bid Alternate A
around one data foundation. 
II. 3 SB24- 174, DOLA Acceptance, and Housing Funding
Senate Bill 24- 174 requires the City to prepare a local Housing Needs Assessment ( HNA) under DOLA’ s
methodology, consider it at a public hearing, and submit it to DOLA by December 31, 2026, with updates
every six years. DOLA’ s HNA Guide defines six require d outputs: existing stock and households, jobs by
wage, existing need, ten- year need, combined need including accessible and supportive units, and areas
at elevated risk of displacement. Its baseline requirements add measures of homelessness and housing
instability and of local resources dedicated to affordable housing. DOLA accepts an assessment only when
each output is documented against its checklist, so a compliance matrix governs this work from kickoff
to submission. 
Acceptance matters beyond compliance. An accepted HNA gives the City a defensible, state- recognized
record of need, the foundation for Proposition 123 commitments, CHFA and DOLA housing programs, 
federal grant applications, and conversations with developers. An assessment that DOLA accepts but that
no funder or developer can use leaves that value on the table. 
II. 4 The 2024 Study and DOLA’ s Review
DOLA’ s July 2026 review found three required outputs missing from the 2024 Study: accessible and
visitable unit needs, supportive unit needs, and displacement risk. 11 The finding is fair. The City
commissioned the 2024 Study to identify latent demand and attract residential development, before
SB24- 174 defined its outputs, and its data now runs two vintages behind. The City has stated its
preference for a new 2026 assessment, and that is what this proposal delivers. Section III. 5 explains how
PDC’ s prior work shortens the schedule without carrying forward dated figures. 
WHY THIS TEAM, WHY NOW
PDC studied this market in 2024 and knows where its data misleads. The City needs a DOLA- compliant
assessment and an adoptable element on a grant timeline, and it needs both to lead to housing on
the ground. PDC can begin analysis at kickoff rather than after months of data collection, and it can
carry the findings into the financing conversations that follow. That head start is the margin
between compliance- in-progress and compliance.

III. 1   “ We Find the Way” Methodology
Most housing needs assessments answer one question: how many homes, at what prices, does the
community lack? This proposal answers that question to DOLA’ s standard and then asks a second one: 
what would it take to build them? The Team reads every finding through two lenses, compliance and
delivery. The compliance lens ensures each SB24- 174 and DOLA output is documented, sourced, and
verifiable. The delivery lens tests each need against what Cañon City’s market, construction costs, and
financing tools can produce. 
FROM COUNT TO CONSTRUCTION
When PDC structured North Main Gateway, Durango Crossing, and Nazarene, it tested the same
rents, sale prices, land costs, and funding sources this assessment will measure. The Team will carry
that discipline into Cañon City’ s HNA, so its recommendations name the products the market can
deliver, the price points that need public participation, and the tools that could close each gap. The
City receives an assessment that DOLA accepts, funders credit, and developers can act on. 
Three principles shape each task. First, the HNA and the Strategic Growth Element share one data
foundation, one GIS base, and one engagement program. Second, every figure traces to a workbook with
live formulas, a named source, and a data vintage. Third, quality control is built in rather than added at
the end ( Section III. 4). One compliance matrix governs the work, so every statutory and DOLA
requirement has an owner, a method, and a place in the report. 
Tasks follow the numbering of the City’ s Appendix B cost form, which lists the existing work review as a
separate Task 2. Scope Tasks 2 through 9 in RFP Section 4. 1 therefore appear here as Tasks 3 through 10.

Identifying Hidden Demand for Housing ( July 2024) 
City of Cañon City, Economic Development Department  |  PDC
The City commissioned PDC to identify latent housing demand and the products that could attract
residential development. The study analyzed population, migration, wages, industry, commuting, housing
stock, affordability, and development pace, and PDC interviewed nine major employers and service
providers. It found that out- migration of residents aged 40 to 54, heavy in- commuting, and an outdated
housing stock were holding back growth. The study is the foundation of PDC’ s knowledge of this market. 
The Animas Airpark and La Posta Strategic Plan ( August 2026) 
La Plata Economic Development Alliance  |  PDC  |  Contract value $ 50, 000
PDC prepared an economic feasibility and strategic plan for a 533- acre employment district south of
Durango, with funding partners that included La Plata County, the City of Durango, the Southern Ute
Indian Tribe, and CHFA. The plan sized market demand in Low, Base, and High scenarios and placed a 1. 1
million SF program across four Development Opportunity Zones, then tested trunk infrastructure against
tax increment bonding capacity. Its method of land capacity, scenarios, infrastructure dependencies, 
and fiscal responsibility is the method of Bid Alternate A. 
Durango Crossing and North Main Gateway Urban Renewal Plans ( 2023 and 2026) 
City of Durango  |  PDC  |  Contract values $ 40, 000 and $ 24, 900

169 for- sale and rental units, 139 of them priced below 120% AMI. For each, PDC prepared the conditions
survey, feasibility and fiscal analysis, and plan, and supported the revenue - sharing negotiations. Both
plans show PDC carrying a documented housing need through to a financed project. 
Nazarene Urban Renewal Plan and Gap Funding Analysis ( 2026) 
City of Golden and Golden Urban Renewal Authority  |  PDC  |  Contract value $ 29, 975
PDC evaluated a 47- unit for- sale townhome development on former church property, priced for
households at 80% AMI, ~$ 350, 000 per unit and less than half Golden’ s median home price. The analysis
verified costs against market and Colorado Division of Housing benchmarks and tested a capital stack of
HOME, Division of Housing, CHFA, City, land trust, and tax increment funds. It found tax increment
financing essential to closing the project’ s $ 7. 9 million funding gap.
