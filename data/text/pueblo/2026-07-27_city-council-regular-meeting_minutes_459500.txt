Summary Paragraph 
 
Plaza Verde Park, located in Pueblo’s Lower East Side neighborhood, remains within its original 
property boundary. It measures approximately 852 ft by 744 ft.1 It is surrounded by the flood 
plain of Fountain Creek to the southwest and by residential houses to the north and east. Also 
bordering the park to the north and east are East Ash Street and South Hudson Avenue. The park 
includes a large recreational field comprised of grass and trees, a horseshoe court, a park trail, a 
restroom facility, playset equipment, a gazebo, a basketball court, infographic marker about the 
history of the park, and handball courts. Murals are found on the handball courts and restroom 
facility. The oldest of the murals, painted on the outer wall of the east elevation of the handball 
courts, was completed in 1978 by Leo Lucero.2 The handball courts structure and the restroom 
facility building are the only contributing resources. The site itself is a non-contributing resource. 
The horseshoe court, playset equipment, gazebo, basketball court, and marker are non-
contributing features. The park’s boundaries and parcel allotments remain the same from its 
initial construction. All parcels were granted to the City of Pueblo in 1973 following the 1965 
flood, except for the northeast corner of parcel 0431412001, which was granted in 1976.3

Narrative Description  
 
Surrounding Landscape of Plaza Verde Park (Non-contributing) 
 
To the east, there is a recreational field comprised of grass and trees. It is bordered in part by 
East Ash Street to the north and South Hudson Avenue to the east. To the south, a levy runs 
diagonally along the recreational field, trending southeast-northwest. The space is open aside 
from a horseshoe court. A concrete sidewalk leads to the horseshoe court from the west. The 
horseshoe court was added sometime after 2012.4 To the south, the concrete park trail leads up 
the levy to Fountain Creek Park Trail, which follows along the Fountain Creek floodplain and 
Fountain Creek itself. The levy is covered in grass and small bushes. Running along the bottom 
of the levy are cottonwood trees. Located to the northwest of the restroom structure is playset 
equipment. The playset equipment was added sometime before 2007.5 The remaining playset 
equipment is situated right off of the main park trail, and consists of a concrete path that circles 
the equipment, as well as two benches. The equipment has slides and swings. The area for the 
playset equipment is covered in mulch and has two nearby cottonwood trees. Located at the very 
northern edge of the park is a gazebo. Under the gazebo are four picnic tables. To the southeast 
of the gazebo are nine rose bushes planted in commemoration of Las Madres de la Casa Verde. 
A basketball court is located to the west of the gazebo and directly to the east of the handball 
courts. Both basketball hoops are painted with three vertical stripes of green, white, and red, 
representing Mexico’s flag.  
 
Recreational Field and Horseshoe Court (Non-contributing) (Photos 1-4) 
 
To the east, there is a recreational field comprised of grass and trees. It is bordered in part by 
East Ash Street to the north and South Hudson Avenue to the east. To the south, a levy runs 
diagonally along the recreational field, trending southeast-northwest. The space is open aside 
from a horseshoe court. A concrete sidewalk leads to the horseshoe court from the west. The 
horseshoe court was added sometime after 2012 and is not associated with the historic function 
of the property.6 It is therefore considered a non-contributing feature. The base of the court is 
made from concrete. Two parallel narrow divots are inlayed into the concrete. These are both 
long rectangular strips that each lead to metal stakes in the ground meant to catch a horseshoe 
during the game. Behind each metal stake is a backboard, placed there to prevent the horseshoe 
from being tossed outside of the court. Each backboard is made from wood and is held in place 
by two metal bars that are set into the ground.

Restroom Building, Murals (Contributing) and Historical Marker (Non-contributing) 
(Photos 8-15)  
 
The restroom building is located towards the southeast portion of the park, near the playset 
equipment and gazebo located to the north and the horseshoe court to the northeast. It was 
installed sometime before 2007.7 Approximately 10 feet to the north of the restroom building is 
an infographic marker that tells the history of the park and the contributions of Las Madres de la 
Casa Verde. The historical marker was contributed by a local organization, Comadres, in 2016.8 
The historical marker is a non-contributing feature.  
 
The restroom facilities building has a small rectangular semicircle plan with rounded corners, 
measuring approximately 27 ft by 12 ft. The building trends northwest-southeast. It sits at the 
convergence of the park trail trending north-south and the concrete path to the horseshoe court in 
the recreational field trending east-west. The restroom building is approximately 65 feet to the 
southeast of the playset equipment, and approximately 145 ft to the south of the Gazebo. The 
building sits on a concrete pad. It is made from bricks, all of which have been painted over. 
Murals and artwork are painted on the façade as well as each elevation of the structure. They 
were painted in 2013.9 A parapet extends over the roof on the southeast and northwest elevations 
of the structure. It has a red metal roof. In the center of the roof is a vent pipe.  
 
Façade (Photos 11-12): The building rests on a concrete pad. It is comprised of bricks, all of 
which have been painted over. Towards the base of the building is a light blue painted motif 
consisting of a circle with four teardrop shapes extending from it. Each motif is set on a grey 
border, and enclosed by black lines in the shape of rectangles. Each motif is separated by two 
black lines. The majority of bricks on the façade are painted beige. The bricks of the parapet 
extending past the side of the southeast corner of the façade are painted red. A metal door is 
located to the southeast corner of the elevation. The frame of the door is painted light blue with 
light blue painted lines used to emulate stones. Black painted lines are used to emulate vertical 
bricks. Towards the bottom portion of the door are painted the words “Una Dama Maya.” Above 
these words is an abstractly painted motif with colors of purple, black, orange, yellow, and blue.

Playset Equipment (Non-contributing) (Photos 16-17) 
 
Located 60 ft to the northwest of the restroom building, 35 ft to the southwest of the gazebo, and 
50 ft to the southeast of the basketball court, is playset equipment. The playset equipment was 
added sometime before 2007. Between 2012 and 2022, another set of park equipment at the 
southwestern portion of the park, near the handball courts, was removed.11 The remaining 
playset equipment is situated right off of the main park trail, and consists of its own concrete 
path that circles the equipment, as well as two benches. The equipment consists of slides and 
swings. The area for the playset equipment is covered in mulch and has two nearby cottonwood 
trees. The playset equipment is non-contributing.   
 
Gazebo (Non-contributing) (Photos 19-22) 
 
Located at the very northern edge of the park is a gazebo. It is 25 ft to the northeast of the playset 
equipment and 130 ft to the east of the basketball court. The gazebo is an open air structure, with 
eight support beams, on a concrete pad. Under the gazebo are four picnic tables. The gazebo has 
an octagonal metal pro-panel roof. The roof is two tiered with a cupola picket at the very top. 
Although a gazebo was requested by community members in 1972, it was not installed until 
2015.12 Due to its recent construction, it is a non-contributing feature.

Handball Courts (Contributing) (Photos 28-41) 
   
The handball courts are located directly to the west of the basketball court. The handball courts 
are an open concrete structure measuring approximately 50 ft by 25 ft in its entirety. There are 
two courts connected by the front wall, as it is known in the game. Atop the front wall, which 
connects the two courts, is a chain link fence, approximately 5 ft high, meant to prevent the ball 
from going over the court during the game. Each individual court measures 25 ft by 20 ft. Each 
court is encircled by a chain-link fence. The fence measures approximately 10 ft. high. The entire 
structure is made from 3 tilt up concrete walls. When observing the structure from a sky view, it 
forms an “H” shape. The floor or deck of the court is made from poured concrete. The poured 
concrete floor or deck of the court encompasses both the inside and outside of the walls. It 
contains several significant murals painted between 1978 and 2012. 
 
East elevation (Photos 28-29): The tilt up concrete wall of the east elevation measures 
approximately 50 ft. across. It is made of smooth concrete. The wall is not a perfect rectangle, 
but instead extends outward from the top and tapers inward towards the bottom. A chain link 
fence connects to northern and southern sides of the east elevation. The first mural to be painted 
in the park encompasses the entire east elevation of the handball courts. It was painted by local 
Chicano artist Leo Lucero in 1978.14 The mural has several representations and perspectives. 
The first perspective depicts a buffalo in what represents North America to the south of the 
elevation, and a llama standing in what represents South America on the north elevation. In 
between these two depictions, towards the center of the elevation, is a giant eagle holding a 
serpent in its mouth. When standing closely to the mural, the eagle feathers make up an army of 
soldados, soldiers, with gun belts. From another perspective, the painting reveals other figures. 
One figure in the mural’s upper register emulates revolutionary figure Emiliano Zapata as noted 
by artist Leo Lucero. This figure and another male figure depicted below hold a gun belt together 
with what appears to be a representation of the xiuhpōhualli calendar between them. Next to the 
figure representing Emiliano Zapata, is a woman holding a torch and below this painting, in the 
lower register, is another depiction of a woman with her hands on either side of a basket filled 
with white calla lilies and a child sitting on her shoulder.15

The handball court and restroom facility are located in Plaza Verde Park on 800 East Ash Street. 
The park is bordered by East Ash Street and South Hudson Avenue, and the levy to the 
southeast. The location of the structure and building remain original to their construction. The 
landscape and setting surrounding the handball courts and restroom facility in Plaza Verde Park 
remain relatively unchanged from their period of significance. Plaza Verde Park is surrounded by 
residential homes to the north and east and the levy to the southwest.  
 
The handball courts and restroom facility retain good integrity of design. Both resources retain 
their functionality and spatial organization in the park. They are situated within an area of the 
neighborhood that is accessible to the East Side community in Pueblo. The design features and 
spatial organization of the handball courts and restroom facility remain the same and serve the 
same purposes for recreation and functionality within the larger park. The retention of the 
original spatial organization of the park, as well as its designed functionality, also convey good 
integrity of workmanship.

do the handball courts. Additionally, the many design elements of the murals are unaltered, aside

of Cesar Chavez was painted over and replaced by a depiction of Dolores Huerta in 2026.25

painting in Plaza Verde Park.

community still associates this structure and building, that each adorn murals, as a places of 
remembrance. The protection of the park’s murals and continued mural painting depicting 
cultural heritage and pride portrays how this association remains strong with East Side 
neighborhood residents. They also retain integrity of feeling. During community gatherings, 
protests, and events, this recreational structure and building were used. The retention of the 
murals also portray the historic feeling of the property. The continuation of this practice to paint 
murals depicting cultural heritage also showcase their continued importance to the community.

Statement of Significance Summary Paragraph (Provide a summary paragraph that includes 
level of significance, applicable criteria, justification for the period of significance, and any 
applicable criteria considerations.)  
 
Plaza Verde Park is eligible for the National Register of Historic Places at the local level under 
Criterion A for Art, Ethnic Heritage: Hispanic/Latino/Chicano and Social History, and 
Social History: Women’s History. It is associated with the history of the Chicano Civil Rights 
Movement in Pueblo, Colorado. The park historically acted as a central space for Chicano 
activism and community pride amongst the city’s Lower East Side residents.

In 1968, women from the Lower East Side of Pueblo created Las Madres de la Casa Verde in 
response to the lack of recreational facilities for the youth of their community. Las Madres de la 
Casa Verde organized and established a community center, Casa, to facilitate activities for 
Chicano youth such as after school programs, cultural education, tutoring, and lobbied for safe 
recreational spaces to be built in Pueblo’s Lower East Side neighborhood. Their activism was in 
response to a number of youth deaths that had occurred in the community due to the lack of safe 
recreational spaces for them to use. Las Madres de la Casa Verde lobbied for a park to be built in 
their community using land devastated by the Pueblo Flood of 1965. Many of the homes 
destroyed on the parcels that would become Plaza Verde Park belonged to Las Madres de la Casa 
Verde members and their families.26  
 
The park was commissioned on May 5, 1972. Las Madres de la Casa Verde worked with Martín 
Serna, a Chicano Activist, member of the Brown Berets, and co-director of La Raza Unida, to 
complete the park and its planning. Since its construction, Plaza Verde Park has been a place of 
civil rights activism and community gathering. The park is significant for its murals depicting 
Chicano history and culture. Plaza Verde Park meets the requirements for Criterion 
Consideration G as a significant site representing the history of the Chicano Movement in 
Pueblo, Colorado through its murals. The park was created out of a need for infrastructure and 
safe recreational spaces in the face of redlining and discrimination experienced by Pueblo’s 
Lower East Side neighborhood. It became a significant site of activism during the Chicano 
Movement and continues to act as a community gathering place. It is a reminder of how the 
movement in Pueblo had impacts not only within the city but within the larger Civil Rights 
Movement. This history is reflected in the murals that adorn walls on structures in the park. The 
murals, painted between 1978 and 2013, are significant representations of Chicano muralism that 
hold incredible significance. Therefore, the period of significance is 1972 when the park was 
commissioned, to 2013, the year when the most recent murals were painted in the park.27

In 1973, a statement was issued to the Pueblo City Government by community members 
demanding to know why construction for Plaza Verde Park was not complete. Although the land 
at Hudson and Ash was acquired to build Plaza Verde Park on May 5, 1972, work was not yet 
done. The statement addressed how land had been acquired to build Vinewood Park in the same 
year, and while that park was now complete, Plaza Verde Park remained unfinished. Community 
members expressed the urgent need for the park to be completed due to multiple deaths of 
Chicano youth that had occurred in the area who had been forced to play “in hazardous streets, 
cattlecars, lakes, and rivers in Pueblo.” A park was desperately needed in the neighborhood to 
provide a safe recreational space for local youth, as none existed. The work of Pueblo’s East Side 
community and Las Madres de la Casa Verde ultimately resulted in construction moving 
forward; yet, the exact date of the parks completion is unknown.41 It took the advocacy of Las 
Madres de la Casa Verde and the community to begin a campaign to see advancement. The 
group worked with Martín Serna of La Raza Unida gathering over 3,000 signatures to present to 
the city, as well as holding bake sales to raise money for the park.42  
 
Serna reached out to members of City Council, the Pueblo Urban Renewal Authority, and the 
U.S. Department of Health and Urban Development in an effort to move the park’s construction 
along. Because the park’s progress continued to be stalled, Serna worked with La Raza Unida, 
the Brown Berets, Las Madres de la Casa Verde, and several other community groups to 
orchestrate a protest to their lack of action to provide the Lower East Side Community with safe 
recreational spaces. The rally was for youth who had lost their lives or been injured playing near 
streets and rivers. The face of the protest was Rudy Maestas, who was 14 years old when he was 
struck by a car while riding his bicycle in the neighborhood. The rally was held at Zapata Park. 
City officials maintained that funding had been allocated for the park but was held up at the 
federal level. Serna and protesters did not accept this reasoning, instead, questioning why the city 
would not use city funding to provide a park for the neighborhood. It was at this juncture, and 
due to the activism of these community groups that ultimately resulted in construction moving 
forward; however, several amenities at the park have taken years to be acquired. In 1980, the 
Chicano community made a request to the City of Pueblo to have permanent restrooms installed 
to replace an outhouse that neighborhood residents had constructed at the park.43 The

Since its construction, Plaza Verde Park has been used as a place of protest, celebration, and 
remembrance by the Chicano community. In 1976, La Cucaracha called upon the Chicano 
community to attend a rally to be held at Plaza Verde Park. It was planned for September 19, 
following El Diez y Seis de Septiembre, in honor of Mexico’s Independence. The Brown Berets 
and El Partido La Raza Unida planned celebrations for Mexican Independence Day in Pueblo to 
unify Chicano communities. Usually a day for celebration, the organizers of this rally said the 
event in 1976 was to be in protest of United States encroachment into Mexico. The protestors in 
Plaza Verde Park were unified against the exploitation of Mexican workers by American 
capitalist corporations who paid workers extremely low wages and who took resources away 
from Mexico’s infrastructure. The protest illuminated Mexico’s resistance against United States 
exploitation.46 At the rally of over 200 people, several activist speakers such as Juan Haro, 
Martín Serna, Francisco Coca, and Fred Archer were in attendance and gave speeches for the 
cause.47 Plaza Verde Park was a significant place for the Chicano Civil Rights movement and 
was often used historically by organizations such as the Brown Berets and El Partido La Raza 
Unida as a site of protest.  
 
Another important protest that occurred at Plaza Verde Park included The Longest Walk which 
was supported by the Chicano community in Pueblo’s East Side neighborhood. The Longest 
Walk was a demonstration held by Indigenous groups across the country in which protestors 
walked from California to Washington D.C. The protest was to prevent eleven pieces of 
legislation brought forth in Congress that threatened to violate treaty rights of First Nations.48 
Along their route to the capitol, protestors stopped at Plaza Verde Park in Pueblo where they 
held a rally. As part of their protests, they provided cultural education in the form of speakers 
and music. The Chicano community supported their protest and provided donations, such as 
clothing, while they were demonstrating in Pueblo.49 Plaza Verde Park continues to be a place of 
protest and remembrance for the Chicano community and groups who have worked in close

Murals 
 
Contributing to the significance of Plaza Verde Park are historic murals that depict Chicano 
culture and heritage. The murals also serve as a source of remembrance and pride for the 
Chicano community. The first mural in the park was painted by local Chicano artist Leo Lucero 
on one of the outer walls of the handball courts in 1978. The mural has two perspectives. The 
first perspective depicts a buffalo standing in North America to one side, and a llama standing in 
South America on the other. In between these two depictions is a giant eagle holding a serpent in 
its mouth representing “the people of Atzlán, founders of Tenochtitlan, the Aztec empire” as 
described by Lucero in a 1978 interview with La Cucaracha. When standing closely to the 
mural, the eagle feathers make up an army of soldados, soldiers, with gun belts. From another 
perspective, the painting reveals other figures. Lucero described how one figure in the mural’s 
upper register is meant to emulate revolutionary figure Emiliano Zapata. This figure and another 
male figure depicted below hold a gun belt together with the xiuhpōhualli calendar between 
them. Next to the figure representing Emiliano Zapata is a woman holding a torch and below this 
painting is another depiction of a woman with her hands on either side of a basket filled with 
white calla lilies and a child sitting on her shoulder. When further describing the meaning of his 
work, Lucero said “the Zapata-like figure represents the soldier fighting for his land…they’re 
carrying a gun belt because only through force will the Chicano get his land back. Only through 
force will you get your freedom.” Lucero also described how the woman figure holding the torch 
was inspired by a painting by Alfaro Siquieros. The symbol of the torch represents new 
democracy. The community helped Lucero with the mural, offering scaffolds, paint, and rollers. 
Other local painters also came to support Lucero in his efforts. An inauguration for the mural 
was held on July 2, 1978 and included music and speakers to celebrate the artwork.50  
 
The mural was originally contested by city officials, but Lucero, with the support of La Raza 
Unida and La Gente, continued to paint it despite threats. As he completed the mural on the wall 
of the handball courts, the city threatened to paint it over as it had not been officially sanctioned 
by city officials. The Director of Pueblo Parks and Recreation at the time said he would give 
prior warning to the community before it was painted over. Community support for Chicano art 
in Pueblo’s Lower East Side neighborhood, as well as the support from La Raza Unida and La 
Gente, prevented this from happening. Community members stayed with the mural while Lucero 
completed it.51 In the 1990s, the city contacted Lucero to repaint the mural as people had started

Early Development of Pueblo’s East Side Neighborhood 
 
East Pueblo was first developed in the late nineteenth century by Lewis/Louis Conley. He and 
three other builders were responsible for much of the city’s development; however, Conley spent 
much of his focus on the East Side neighborhood. Conley is responsible for the Conley Addition, 
which platted and recorded the land of the East Side Pueblo neighborhood. Much of the land 
Conley purchased had been homestead claims, which included large portions of what would 
become Pueblo’s East Side.69  
 
Many of the adobe residences constructed during this initial development still stand and 
represent some of the oldest buildings in the city. After incurring losses to his wealth in 1873 due 
to an economic recession, Conley left Pueblo and development stalled for nearly a decade. Over 
time, the East Side neighborhood was predominantly settled by Pueblo’s working-class 
communities.70  
 
In 1875, East Side Pueblo had become Pueblo’s Fifth Ward and gained a representative on the 
city council; however, the physical boundary of Fountain Creek also became a political and 
social boundary, where the needs of the community were often ignored by the city government. 
Beginning at this shift, East Pueblo was differentiated from what many described as “Pueblo 
Proper” encompassing all communities located west of Fountain Creek. After the economic 
recession of 1873, developers working in East Pueblo, such as the Pueblo Building & 
Improvement Company led by Colonel M.H. Fitch and Ferd Barndollar, continued to appeal to 
working classes. At the same time, these developers were planning subdivisions in Pueblo’s 
North Side neighborhood that catered towards the city’s wealthy. While the East Side 
neighborhood was given less infrastructure, consisting of smaller building construction and less 
emphasis on landscaping, the North Side neighborhood was planned with graded streets, planted

Redlining in Pueblo’s East Side Neighborhood 
 
Settlement in the City of Pueblo was defined by a discriminatory practice referred to as redlining. 
Redlining was developed from the Great Depression and used by local bankers, realtors, and real 
estate experts to establish ethnically and racially segregated neighborhoods across the United 
States. Maps were made in this practice to assess risk in home buyers to acquire opportunities 
such as home loans, mortgages, insurance, and many other financial services. Despite this 
assertion, people’s financial qualifications and credit were never considered. Instead, redlining 
was an ethnic and race based system that sought to keep neighborhoods segregated in cities 
around the country.75 As described by Dawn DiPrince, President and Chief Executive Officer 
and State Historic Preservation Officer: 
 
Realtors deliberately maintained the ethnic and class purity of neighborhoods deemed 
‘best’ and ‘desirable’ and filled with a ‘good class of citizens.’ The system worked so 
well because residents also enforced this segregation by establishing homeowners’ 
covenants to prohibit demographic diversity, excluding racial and ethnic minorities, 
immigrants and working class people from their neighborhoods.76

The term “redlining” comes from the maps made that highlighted “hazardous neighborhoods” in 
a red/pink shade to indicate addresses, and therefore families, who were denied home ownership 
and/or property in specific neighborhoods. In this system, neighborhood’s deemed “hazardous” 
experienced divestment from city officials which created a cycle of these neighborhoods being 
underfunded. Redlined neighborhoods received no investment for infrastructure, being left 
without paved streets, sidewalks, safe recreational spaces, and basic safety needs such as storm 
water drainage and plumbing.77 Redlining was encouraged at the highest levels of government in 
the United States. The Federal Reserve and Federal Housing Administration (FHA) were 
charged during the Great Depression to ensure loans as part of the residential mortgage system 
were stable.78 As part of this, Federal Reserve History describes: 
 
The FHA began redlining at the very beginning of its operations in 1934, as FHA staff 
concluded that no loan could be economically sound if the property was located in a 
neighborhood that was or could become populated by Black people, as property values 
might decline over the life of 15 to 20 year loans they were attempting to standardize. For 
example, 1938 Underwriting Manuals emphasized the negative impact of ‘infiltration of 
inharmonious racial groups’ on credit risk. To limit that risk, it recommended restrictive 
covenants that prohibit ‘the occupancy of properties except by the race for which they are 
intended.’79   
 
Several of Pueblo’s neighborhoods were subject to relining. This includes Pueblo’s Lower East 
Side community, marked as D6 and shaded in red/pink on a 1930 reality map of the City of 
Pueblo. The key delineates all class D neighborhoods as “hazardous” (see Figure 1 for 1930 
Redline Map of Pueblo). All class A, class B, and class E were labeled as “best,” “still 
desirable,” and “business and commercial.” Class C properties were labeled as “definitely 
declining.”80 The historical descriptions that accompany the map use offensive and racist 
language to describe populations of class C and class D neighborhoods comprised of primarily 
Latino, Hispano, African American, Italian, and Slavic groups.81 Historically redlined 
neighborhoods in the City of Pueblo still experience the effects of this system, even after the Fair 
Housing Act outlawed redlining in 1968. Relining prevented many families from garnering 
generational wealth from home equity, as well as caused severe delays in infrastructure in 
several neighborhoods. The history of Plaza Verde Park is deeply connected to the history of 
redlining in Pueblo’s Lower East Side neighborhood.82 Because the city refused to invest in 
recreational spaces in the neighborhood, organizations such as Las Madres de la Casa Verde took 
it upon themselves to advocate for their community. It is the activism of several community 
groups in Pueblo who instigated change in the mid-to-late twentieth century.

History of Park Development in East Side Pueblo     
 
The desire for public parks was revitalized in 1896, not only in Pueblo, but nationally, after the 
unveiling of the Beaux Arts inspired White City at the 1893 Chicago World Columbian 
Exposition. It inspired the City Beautiful Movement, which in turn influenced cities across the 
country to create their own parks and boulevards. Local civic leaders and members of the Ladies 
Parks and Improvement Association such as Charles Gast, W.D. Latshaw, and H.W. Mitchell 
met with the Pueblo Chamber of Commerce to discuss the establishment of a city parks system. 
The Ladies Parks and Improvement Association presented a plan to create four park districts 
within the city; one was to be located at the highest point of Pueblo’s East Side neighborhood. 
Their plan was accepted by the city government and Mitchell Park was established in Pueblo’s 
East Side.83  
 
Although some amenities were added in the 1930s by the Works Project Administration, such as 
a wading pool, tennis court, and basketball court, the East Side neighborhood was often last to 
receive new amenities, updates, or repairs. As described, East Side Pueblo was not only 
separated physically from the rest of the city, but there was also a political and social boundary 
that has led to ethnic, racial, and social discrimination and segregation. For example, City Park 
received a swimming pool in 1957, followed by Mineral Palace Park in 1963, and Minnequa 
Park in 1965. The East Side’s Mitchell Park did not receive a public pool until 1976. Although 
Mitchell Park existed in East Side Pueblo, it was difficult for many residents to use, as it was set 
far apart from most of the community.84 Pueblo’s Lower East Side neighborhood did not have a 
park until Plaza Verde Park was established in the 1970s.  
 
Chicano Movement in Pueblo, Colorado                 
 
The Chicano Civil Rights Movement began in the 1960s and was a multifaceted social and 
political movement that sought to protect the labor rights of farm workers, reclaim stolen land, 
expand economic opportunity, create educational reform, and to bolster the rights of women in 
their communities. Early strategies of mobilization and protest included utilizing regional 
community networks to gain political power. Chicano Movement leaders sought the formation of 
an independent political party that addressed the needs of their communities. The 1960s created 
significant shifts in the social and political fabric of the United States. In part, these shifts were 
inspired by the war in Vietnam. Groups across the country began to question the United States’ 
role in world affairs and the country’s exploitation of people abroad. These sentiments quickly 
sparked shifts on the domestic front as well, where underrepresented groups across the nation 
began to organize in mass for social change. The Chicano Movement was a struggle for civil 
rights in the United States where Latino communities had historically and continuously 
experienced land theft, economic inequality, segregation, deprivation of cultural education,

The Chicano Movement in Pueblo was influenced by the momentum of the Civil Rights 
Movements happening throughout the United States. In the 1960s, the population of Pueblo was 
approximately 125,000. Of the population, 31,800 were recorded as being of Latino heritage. 
Racial discrimination and systemic inequality was reflected by city records, which indicate a 
majority of school dropouts, welfare recipients, and incarcerations were those of Latino heritage 
when compared to the entire population of the city during the decade of the 1960s. In his work, 
“The Chicano Wars,” Instructor David Marquez of the University of Southern Colorado 
recounts: 
 
A general overview of the discrimination existing in Pueblo can be demonstrated 
by analyzing employment statistics for Pueblo’s School District 60. In 1968, the 
District employed 1,847. Only 125, or 6.7%, were Spanish surnamed. Of that 
total, 23.2% were maintenance workers, clerical help, and teacher aides. Most 
Chicano children completed a full 12-year course of study never having been 
exposed to a Chicano teacher.86 
 
The initial leaders of the Chicano Movement in Pueblo were students influenced by the call for 
social change developing in the 1960s. Alberto Gurule was one of these early Chicano leaders 
that helped spark the movement.87 Gurule worked as a caseworker for the Pueblo County 
Welfare Department. During his time there, he received a full salary education leave to study at 
the University of Denver. In 1968, Gurule received his Master’s Degree in social work. It was at 
this time that he became heavily involved in the Chicano Movement in Pueblo and began 
organizing the community to spark change within the city. Gurule’s strategy for change was 
dependent on student participation. He based mobilization of students on the walkouts occurring 
across the Southwest. At Southern Colorado State College, Gurule sponsored a Chicano student 
organization called Movimiento Estudiantil Chicano de Aztlan (MECHA). This organization 
linked with the Pueblo G.I. Forum, Pueblo Welfare Rights Organization, and Legal Services of 
Pueblo and planned their first demonstrations in Pueblo stores, schools, universities.88   
 
Gurule, with the support of several Pueblo based Chicano organizations, incited the community 
to picket local Safeway stores beginning on December 12, 1969. This was in support of Caesar 
Chavez’s “La Causa.” The national movement observed a grape boycott in protest of the 
treatment of farmworkers. The demonstrations carried out by Gurule against Safeway were the 
beginnings of the Chicano Movement in Pueblo. From these initial protests, students became

organizing their own demonstrations. Focus shifted towards education. Inspired by the walkout 
in East Los Angeles, students in Pueblo organized a walkout of their own in District 60.91 
 
Demonstrations by Chicano students in District 60 schools in Pueblo were motivated to act due 
to the lack of representation they received in schools. Educators, such as Carmen Serna, wife of 
Chicano leader Martín Serna, worked to promote bilingual-bicultural curriculum in the district; 
however, these attempts were often met with retaliation and anonymous threats. The lack of 
education representing Chicano history and heritage motivated students to organize. During the 
1972-1973 school year, dozens of Chicano students were arrested or suspended from Centennial 
and East High Schools for demonstrations and walk outs. Parents, fearful that their children 
would be hurt by armed police at the schools, began patrolling the halls. The community, with 
the support of the Brown Berets, formed the Chicano Barrio Education Committee to challenge 
the school board to increase representation of Chicano students. In 1973, the committee 
submitted a list of recommendations to the school board that included hiring more Chicano 
educators and administrations as well as offering Chicano studies, and bilingual-bicultural
