6. PUBLIC HEARINGS AND ACTION ITEMS 
Public hearings are held to obtain input from the public on various items.  Public hearings are either 
Legislative in nature or Quasi-Judicial in nature.  Public Input is limited to three minutes per person.  
People speaking should step up to the microphon e and state their name and address.  Each is 
described as follows: 
LEGISLATIVE – Legislative public hearings are held when the City Council is considering an item 
that establishes legislation such as an ordinance amending or establishing laws of the city.  
 
QUASI-JUDICIAL – Quasi-judicial public hearings are held when the City Council is acting in a 
judicial or judge like manner and a person with a legitimate interest is entitled to an impartial 
decision made on the basis of  information presented and laws in effect. Quasi -judicial hearings are 
commonly held for land use hearings and liquor license hearings. The City Council must limit its 
consideration to matters which are placed into evidence and are part of the public recor d.  Quasi-
judicial hearings are held in the following manner: 
 
1. Staff presentation - Staff will present the comments and reports received from review agencies and offer a recommendation. 
2. Applicant Presentation - The petitioner  is asked to present the proposal.  Presentations should be  
brief and to the point and cover all of the main points of the project.   
3. Public Comment - (limit of 3 minutes per person) Speakers must step up to the microphone and state their name and address. Speakers 
should be to the point and try not to repeat the points others have made. 
4. Staff and/or applicant(s) respond to public comment. 
5. Council will ask questions of staff and applicant. 
6. The public hearing is closed to public comments. 
7. Make a motion.  A member of the City Council will make a motion on the issue. 
8. Following a second to the motion, the City Council may discuss the motion. 
9. Vote.  The City Council will then vote on the motion. 
ORDINANCES - After introduction of an Ordinance (First Reading), a public hearing date is set and 
notice of the hearing is published in the newspaper.  Staff presents the ordinance on Second Reading 
and the hearing is opened to the public for public input.  After comments from the public, the Mayor 
will close the hearing and bring the Ordinance back to the City Council for discussion and potential 
action.  The Council will make a motion to approve the Ordinance or take no action.  In the event the 
ordinance is approved, it will become effective 30 days after adoption. 
ACTION ITEMS – Action items are items that require Council action and have public comment.

City Planner Joey Morris presented the 1925 J Road Annexation’s Zoning Ordinance 2026-12. He stated 
that the Ordinance would zone the property that was just annexed (in two steps) to a Community 
Residential (CR) Zone District. It is currently zoned under Mesa County’s rules as Urban Residential 
Reserve (URR). 
Morris explained that the City’s Future Land Use Map contained in the Comprehensive Plan identifies the 
subject property as appropriate for residential density at 4-8 dwelling units per acre, which reflects what 
the City’s plan is for the area. 
Morris noted that the property is bounded to the south by the Copper Creek West Subdivision, which is a 
CR zoned piece of property and because the applicant is the same entity as the developer, it is very likely 
that it will develop in a manner consistent with Copper Creek West.  He referred to an aerial photo and 
noted that the subject property is largely agricultural and residential land. 
Morris stated that staff does not anticipate any compatibility issues that would be disproportionate or 
severe, which is the stated criteria for compatibility contained in the Fruita Land Use Code.  
Morris spoke about the Comprehensive Plan and what is meant by the residential 4-8 dwelling units per 
acre designation. He said it is a land use category that is specifically intended for undeveloped areas where 
public infrastructure and services are available and proximal. The  land use is also recommended for 
developed or semi-developed areas that are built out at a minimum of two units per acre . Morris stated 
that in areas that are currently built out at below th at minimum density, it is expected that the minimum 
density of four units per acre is achieved  when redevelopment occurs and the applicant’s plan does 
recognize that many already developed areas will remain in their current form for decades to come. 
Fruita City Council Minutes 10                             August 18, 2026 
 
Morris continued by saying that this is contrasted with the purpose of the CR zone, which is a legal 
designation whereas the land designation contained in the Comprehensive Plan is a guiding designation. 
He stated that the purpose of the CR Zone is to allow for moderate density, detached single -family 
residential neighborhoods with the inclusion of other housing types such as attached dwelling units  like 
apartments and townhomes (for example). In addition, innovative neighborhood design is encouraged in 
the CR Zone to provide opportunities for housing diversity. 
Morris stated that the area is served by public utility infrastructure and is appropriate for density of 4-8 
dwelling units per acre, so the CR Zone being requested directly corresponds to the land use designation 
contained in the City’s Comprehensive Plan. 
Morris noted that there are five  Review Criteria contained in Section 17.09.070 that the Council must 
consider when approving a rezone and that all of them will never apply to a single application; only three 
of them must be met. These include: 
• Zoning compatibility with surrounding land uses 
• Consistent with the Master Plan 
• Is incidental to an annexation application
