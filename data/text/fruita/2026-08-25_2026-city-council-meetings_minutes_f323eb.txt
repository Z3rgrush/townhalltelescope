1. BEE CITY PRESENTATION (6:30 – 7:00 P.M.) 
 
Management Analyst Kaydee Lucero introduced CMU interns Jordan Jennings and Kayla Bauer, who 
researched the benefits of Fruita becoming a Bee City. Bauer explained that Bee City USA promotes 
pollinator-friendly practices that support healthier ecosystems, agriculture and community 
engagement. Jennings highlighted the benefits of replacing invasive plants with pollinator -friendly 
species, including improved water conservation, soil and air quality, crop yields and reduced lawn 
maintenance costs. 
 
Jennings outlined Bee City requirements, including establishing a pollinator committee, reducing 
pesticide use, incorporating pollinator -friendly practices and hosting public awareness events. Bauer 
noted that Fruita already supports many of these practice s and encouraged the City to become Mesa 
County’s first Bee City. The Parks Department could serve as the committee and is already meeting 
many technical requirements. 
 
Becoming a Bee City would require a $200 annual fee and City Council adoption of a resolution 
committing to pollinator habitat, pesticide reduction and public outreach. 
 
Jennings proposed the following: 
 
• Systematically replacing turf grass spaces with native grasses and flowers in highly visible and 
well-trafficked downtown spaces in small sections  (Buffalo grass and Blue Gamma Grass are 
two excellent options for starter grasses).  
• Utilize signage to educate the public about how to effectively xeriscape.  
• Replace the south turf grass lawn of the old Fruita Middle School with native grasses  to make 
it an escape from the urban environment. 
 
Councilor Kumro asked how the City could prevent invasive bee species from becoming established. 
Bauer explained that strengthening native bee populations can help them defend their territory, while 
Jennings recommended contacting the Palisade Insectary if invasive species are suspected. H e and 
Bauer also suggested pollinator awareness events to educate the public and local beekeepers.

4. COMMUNITY DEVELOPMENT FEES OVERVIEW (8:45 – 9:15 P.M.) 
 
City Manager Shannon Vassen noted that the City Council had received two letters from local 
developers regarding concerns with the City’s use taxes on building materials and how they are 
collected. Staff felt that it would be a good time for the Community Development Department to share 
an overview of all Community Development fees that are charged to developers. Vassen noted that 
Chief Building Official Jen Wooten was also in attendance at the meeting. 
 
Planning and Development Director Henry Hemphill described the fees and charges associated with 
certain steps in processing  applications by his department . He noted that the City’s Budget season 
would begin soon and that the City Council has a few new members. 
 
Hemphill stated that the City charges fees for basically every land use application that is processed 
including annexations, zonings  and subdivisions. He said  those fees have not changed over the last 
twelve years but there was one text clarification that was added last year for the City Attorney’s legal 
fees reimbursement. The clarification stipulates that the first three hours of attorney time is  paid into 
the overall application fee and anything after that is back charged to the developer or applicant. 
 
Hemphill explained that the City Council adopts a Resolution establishing the City’s fees and charges 
as part of its annual Budget  that includes Building Permit fees  and a use tax table that is associated 
with those fees.

Hemphill noted that the 2026 Fees and Charges determine Building Permit fees, while Building 
Valuation Data (BVD), based on national averages and development square footage, determines use 
tax fees. Fruita’s use tax, adopted in the 1980s, was primarily intended to capture sales tax on materials 
purchased outside the City where another jurisdiction collected the tax at the point of sale rather than 
delivery. 
 
Hemphill explained that under the Supreme Court’s Wayfair decision, jurisdictions can require sellers 
to collect sales tax regardless of physical presence. Developers who purchase materials outside Fruita 
pay that jurisdiction’s sales tax, then are also re quired to pay Fruita’s 3% sales tax when obtaining a 
building permit. By providing the purchase receipt, developers can request reimbursement of the 
original sales tax from the jurisdiction where the materials were purchased. 
 
Hemphill referred to the chart in the Council packet for the national average of assumed BVD costs 
per square foot by construction type/uses and the associated costs  which are arrived at by utilizing a 
formula.  He explained that at the beginning of each year, the Community Development Department 
reevaluates the costs and that is what is used for the remainder of that year. 
 
Hemphill stated that he thinks there is more of a discrepancy in the operations of how materials are 
purchased and then used in the City versus a straightforward material purchase that may require a 
Building Permit. He said that state statute allows a vendor to waive sales tax if they can show that they 
paid a use tax, which in Colorado is very common. How it is collected and at what point does vary 
from jurisdiction to jurisdiction.  
 
Hemphill pointed out that the Town of Palisade does not charge a use tax at all, but they are currently 
discussing doing so with their Board of Trustees.  The City of Grand Junction does have a use tax 
requirement in their Municipal Code and Hemphill said they essentially have taken more of a different 
approach of charging it at the back end after a full audit is done and a Certificate of Occupancy (CO) 
is issued. 
 
Hemphill said Fruita’s use -tax collection process is simplified, fair, consistent and transparent, but 
acknowledged the letter writers’ concerns are about the methodology and collection process, not the 
amount. The City collects a 3% use tax on estimated building -material costs for remodels, additions, 
sheds and commercial buildings, using a formula that has remained consistent for years. This year’s 
use tax is about $0.04 more per square foot than last year. 
 
Hemphill added that when staff brought the Building Department inhouse, there were many things that 
they wanted to accomplish at that time such as taking care of the citizens of Fruita by being local and 
making sure that the services were top-notch with same day permits, options for inspections and being 
very available.  
 
Councilor Miller stated that she’s heard claims that Fruita is “double -dipping.” Building Official Jen 
Wooten said the issue is likely a misunderstanding of tax timing. Developers can provide suppliers 
with their Building Permit showing Fruita taxes were already paid so those taxes can be removed from 
material purchases. However, if developers do not provide the paperwork upfront, they may pay taxes 
twice. Staff continues to educate developers and suppliers about the process. 
 
Mayor Breman noted that some of the other confusion was related to buying in bulk for both Grand 
Junction projects and Fruita projects and now it’s becoming more and more of an administrative burden 
for developers.
