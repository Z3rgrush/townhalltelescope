7. PUBLIC HEARINGS AND ACTION ITEMS 
Public hearings are held to obtain input from the public on various items.  Public hearings are either 
Legislative in nature or Quasi-Judicial in nature.  Public Input is limited to three minutes per person.  
People speaking should step up to the microphon e and state their name and address.  Each is 
described as follows: 
LEGISLATIVE – Legislative public hearings are held when the City Council is considering an item 
that establishes legislation such as an ordinance amending or establishing laws of the city.  
QUASI-JUDICIAL – Quasi-judicial public hearings are held when the City Council is acting in a 
judicial or judge like manner and a person with a legitimate interest is entitled to an impartial 
decision made on the basis of information presented and laws in effect. Quasi-judicial hearings are 
commonly held for land use hearings and liquor license hearings. The City Council must limit its 
consideration to matters which are placed into evidence and are part of the public record.  Quasi -
judicial hearings are held in the following manner: 
 
1. Staff presentation - Staff will present the comments and reports received from review agencies and offer a recommendation. 
2. Applicant Presentation - The petitioner  is asked to present the proposal.  Presentations should be  
brief and to the point and cover all of the main points of the project.   
3. Public Comment - (limit of 3 minutes per person) Speakers must step up to the microphone and state their name and address. Speakers 
should be to the point and try not to repeat the points others have made. 
4. Staff and/or applicant(s) respond to public comment. 
5. Council will ask questions of staff and applicant. 
6. The public hearing is closed to public comments. 
7. Make a motion.  A member of the City Council will make a motion on the issue. 
8. Following a second to the motion, the City Council may discuss the motion. 
Fruita City Council Minutes 4                       September 15, 2026 
 
9. Vote.  The City Council will then vote on the motion. 
ORDINANCES - After introduction of an Ordinance (First Reading), a public hearing date is set and 
notice of the hearing is published in the newspaper.  Staff present the ordinances on Second Reading 
and the hearing is opened to the public for public input.  After comments from the public, the Mayor 
will close the hearing and bring the Ordinance back to the City Council for discussion and potential 
action.  The Council will make a motion to approve the Ordinance or take no action.  In the event the 
ordinance is approved, it will become effective 30 days after adoption. 
ACTION ITEMS – Action items are items that require Council action and have public comment. 
A. ORDINANCE 2026 -15 – SECOND READING – AMENDING SECTION 2.10.030 OF 
THE FRUITA MUNICIPAL CODE REGARDING CITY COUNCIL MEETINGS TO 
ADD PROVISIONS FOR EMERGENCY MEETINGS  – MANAGEMENT ANALYST 
KAYDEE LUCERO 
Management Analyst Kaydee Lucero provided staff’s presentation on the Ordinance  that would amend 
Section 2.10.030 of the Fruita Municipal Code.  
It was first introduced to City Council on August 18, 2026 and a public notice of the hearing was published. 
Lucero explained that the Ordinance will give the City a lawful way to convene Council quickly in the 
event of a true emergency  that can’t wait for the next scheduled meeting.  The Ordinance adds a new 
subsection to Section 2.10.030 of the Fruita Municipal Code and only covers Regular meetings. It 
authorizes emergency meetings for immediate Council action, but the action taken is only effective until 
the next scheduled, publicly noticed meeting. 
Lucero also explained that the current Municipal Code has no mechanism for Council to act quickly when 
a Regular or Special meeting can’t be practically noticed to the public in advance. 
 
Mayor Breman opened the agenda item for public comment.  Hearing none, he referred the Ordinance to 
the City Council.  
 
Discussion ensued after Councilor Hancey asked if text could be added to the Ordinance to define “police 
power” and whether the City has a definition of what constitutes an emergency.   It was pointed out that 
an emergency calls for  the immediate protection of public health, safety and welfare  and that “police 
power” is the authority for being able to carry out the necessary protection of the public health, safety and 
welfare right away without having to go through other steps that may normally have to be taken. 
 
Councilor Hancey argued that adding more verbiage would be more transparent and may avoid unintended 
consequences. 
 
Lucero stated that staff looked at other municipal codes who have adopted a n Ordinance for emergency 
meetings, and generally, things are left  broader because when it comes to a crisis, nobody  really knows 
what could potentially happen.  
 
City Attorney Mary Elizabeth Geiger  (in attendance virtually) explained that the term “police power” is 
used even throughout the Fruita City Charter, “police power” is a general power that municipalities have, 
is a commonly used term and is not necessarily defined per se. She offered, however, to look for a statutory 
definition to see if there is one. 
Fruita City Council Minutes 5                       September 15, 2026 
 
She added that “police powers” are powers that Council has per case law  regardless of whether or not it 
is added into the Municipal Code, but by adding it into the Fruita Municipal Code , the City is giving the 
public some transparency. She stated that police powers are an exception to the rule and are not to be used 
lightly. As a legal opinion, she did not think it was wise to try to define “emergency” because it is unknown 
what that could be until it happens. She added that the words, “public health, welfare and safety”  are the 
guardrails and any situation that doesn’t describe one or more of those things does not apply.  
 
It was eventually decided that the text should be rewritten to read  “At any such emergency meeting,  
Council can take any action that is necessary for the immediate protection of the public health, safety and 
welfare, meaning within the City’s general police powers, and all of those actions are only valid until the 
next Regular meeting of the City Council.” 
 
Councilor Kumro suggested citing C.R.S. §31-15-401, which outlines municipal police powers. Attorney 
Geiger explained that because Fruita is a Home Rule municipality, the City Charter controls local 
governance. She preferred referencing Charter Section 2.1 4, which already authorizes emergency 
ordinances to address threats to life, health, property or public peace without limiting the City’s public 
health, safety and welfare authority. 
 
Geiger requested that the Council wait to make a motion so she could email the amended language in the 
Ordinance to the Council members and City Manager to put on the screen for the public to see. 
 
Vassen pointed out that the case law that allows emergency meeting s is from a case called Lewis v.  
Nederland and the Municipal Code amendment before the City Council was copied straight from the 
Nederland Municipal Code.

Hemphill pointed out that the City is the applicant because the City is under contract to purchase the 
property at 853 Raptor Road. It is an opportunity for the City to make modifications given the fact that in 
2022, the application was approved by Council for a Class A Motorcoach project containing 39 lots. There 
has been a change in the area and a change for the site itself. 
The amendment to the PUD Guide will allow for a variety of different uses, establish regulations  for 
fences, signs and architectural standards and will remove or modify regulations that no longer apply. 
Prior to application submittal and legal notices, City of Fruita staff hosted a neighborhood meeting, which 
is required for all other applicants for a PUD Guide amendment or other zoning matter. 
Hemphill pointed out that the changes in land uses include  removing the luxury Class A Motorcoach 
concept and replacing it with single -family detached dwelling units over the same amount of lots (not 
changing density) including some flexibility by adding in some soft commercial uses (such as businesses 
located within residential buildings) and creating more architectural character.  Because of the proximity 
of the subject property to The Launch property, there can be some opportunity for cohesiveness between 
the two. 
Hemphill said residential flexibility is likely necessary for the City to close on 853 Raptor Road, with 
housing supported by the City’s Housing Needs Assessment and Housing Action Plan. The proposed C -
2 zoning would limit residential and commercial lots to 5,000 square feet, consistent with the Downtown

returning to Council for future approvals.

sign and fencing regulations. If the City cannot close on the property, the PUD Guide amendment would 
automatically be revoked and zoning would revert to its prior status; a future buyer would need to complete 
the same amendment process for other uses.

D. RESOLUTION 2026 -26 – AUTHORIZING DESIGNATED AGENTS TO ESTABISH 
BANK ACCOUNTS WITH ALPINE BANK – FINANCE DIRECTOR CHRISTA 
YAMASHITA 
 
Finance Director Christa Yamashita presented Resolution 2026 -26, w hich authorizes designated city 
officials to establish and maintain banking accounts with Alpine Bank on behalf of the City. Currently, 
the City uses BMO as the City’s bank , but over the past couple of years, bank fees associated with the 
City’s bank accounts have continually increased. Yamashita explained that staff would like to partner with 
a local bank which would provide accessible, responsive service while also offering opportunities to help 
reduce the City’s bank fees and overall costs. 
 
Yamashita stated that Resolution 2026 -26 establishes the appropriate authority to open the accounts and 
internal controls for managing the accounts. 
 
Yamashita concluded her presentation by recommending the approval of Resolution 2026-26. 
 
Mayor opened the agenda item to public comment. Hearing none, he closed the public hearing and referred 
the matter to City Council. 
 
Councilor Prochnow asked if the intent was to eventually transition all monies with BMO to Alpine Bank 
or hold accounts open with both banks. Yamashita responded that staff’s intent is to transition all of the 
accounts to Alpine Bank.
