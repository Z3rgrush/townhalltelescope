C. Presentation - Update from Ilana Moir, Conservation Director of the Colorado West Land 
Trust 
 
Ilana Moir, Director of Conservation at Colorado West Land Trust provided a PowerPoint presentation  
about the Community Separator (Buffer) Program . Colorado West Land Trust is a private, non -profit 
organization founded by a group of farmers in Palisade in 1980  who saw the shale boom come in and 
wanted to protect their fruit growing operations.  They did some of the first conservation work in the 
Palisade area and Colorado West Land Trust has since grown to cover a six -county region in western 
Colorado. Their mission remains the same: they want to protect agricultural lands and community access 
to them for future generations. 
 
Ms. Moir’s presentation also included the following information:  
 
• Map of the six-county region consisting of approximately 150,000 acres that are protected 
through private conservation agreements with the Land Trust 
• All Land Trust work is voluntary; they do not force any landowners to conserve their 
properties. 
• The Land Trust personalizes its conservation easements and agreements to what families’ 
wishes are for their property. 
• Of the 150,000 acres, about 3,300 acres are on the Valley floor between Palisade and 
Loma. 
• The Land Trust has a three-pronged approach to its conservation work:  
✓ Land protection through Conservation Agreements 
✓ Restoration work on properties /making sure that the terms set forth in 
Conservation Agreements are followed forever so that future generations have the 
same experience with the property 
✓ Focus on community engagement to connect people to open spaces or to the value 
of the agricultural lands that have been conserved 
• Community Separator Program was established in 2000 when the City of Grand Junction, 
City of Fruita and Town of Palisade all signed a Memorandum of Understanding (MOU)  
where they agreed not to annex designated buffer areas.  
• The Land Trust was brought in as a partner to actively pursue conservation work that 
allows landowners to make the choice to conserve their properties instead of  forcing 
landowners to diminish their private property rights through regulations. 
Fruita City Council Minutes 3                       October 20, 2026 
 
 
• The Land Trust is very transaction-oriented; they work hand-in-hand with landowners to 
help them conserve their properties. 
• The Land Trust does active community outreach in the two Community Separator areas  
including writing letters and holding Workshops for landowners, but its best outreach has 
always been when neighbors talk to each other about their experience with the Land Trust. 
• The Land Trust seeks funds to match either local partner dollars or just to bring funds in 
on projects. Funds come from entities such as the Great Outdoors Colorado (GOCO) and 
Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS), which has a farm bill program. 
• Information about Dixie Williams, who was the first person in the Fruita area to conserve 
her property followed by many of her neighbors 
• Photos of conserved land adjacent to the Colorado Riverfront Trail, across the river and 
across the bank on the south side of the river 
• Photos of conserved property at Horsethief Ranch 
• Photos of Green Valley Ranch Conservation Easement 
• Photos of Snooks Bottom, where property is also conserved 
 
Plans for 2027 include pursuing conservation easements on farms, doing outreach in Fruita in a smaller 
Workshop-style setting with targeted landowners and partnering with municipalities on public access as 
needed. 
 
Lastly, Ms. Moir noted that the Colorado West Land Trust’s ask of the City of Fruita for 2027 is $9,000, 
which is an amount that has not increased over the last fifteen (15) years.

6. Public Hearings and Action Items 
Public hearings are held to obtain input from the public on various items.  Public hearings are either 
Legislative in nature or Quasi-Judicial in nature.  Public Input is limited to three minutes per person.  
People speaking should step up to the microphon e and state their name and address.  Each is 
described as follows: 
Fruita City Council Minutes 6                       October 20, 2026 
 
 
LEGISLATIVE – Legislative public hearings are held when the City Council is considering an item 
that establishes legislation such as an ordinance amending or establishing laws of the city.  
QUASI-JUDICIAL – Quasi-judicial public hearings are held when the City Council is acting in a 
judicial or judge like manner and a person with a legitimate interest is entitled to an impartial 
decision made on the basis of information presented and laws in effect. Quasi-judicial hearings are 
commonly held for land use hearings and liquor license hearings. The City Council must limit its 
consideration to matters which are placed into evidence and are part of the public record.  Quasi -
judicial hearings are held in the following manner: 
 
1. Staff presentation - Staff will present the comments and reports received from review agencies and offer a recommendation. 
2. Applicant Presentation - The petitioner  is asked to present the proposal.  Presentations should be  
brief and to the point and cover all of the main points of the project.   
3. Public Comment - (limit of 3 minutes per person) Speakers must step up to the microphone and state their name and address. Speakers 
should be to the point and try not to repeat the points others have made. 
4. Staff and/or applicant(s) respond to public comment. 
5. Council will ask questions of staff and applicant. 
6. The public hearing is closed to public comments. 
7. Make a motion.  A member of the City Council will make a motion on the issue. 
8. Following a second to the motion, the City Council may discuss the motion. 
9. Vote.  The City Council will then vote on the motion. 
ORDINANCES - After introduction of an Ordinance (First Reading), a public hearing date is set and 
notice of the hearing is published in the newspaper.  Staff present the ordinances on Second Reading 
and the hearing is opened to the public for public input.  After comments from the public, the Mayor 
will close the hearing and bring the Ordinance back to the City Council for discussion and potential 
action.  The Council will make a motion to approve the Ordinance or take no action.  In the event the 
ordinance is approved, it will become effective 30 days after adoption. 
ACTION ITEMS – Action items are items that require Council action and have public comment.
