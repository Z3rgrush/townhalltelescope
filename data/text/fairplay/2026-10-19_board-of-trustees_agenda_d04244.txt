 B. THIRD READING: Should the Board of Trustees for the Town of Fairplay, Colorado approve adoption of 
Ordinance No. 3, Series of 2026, entitled "AN ORDINANCE OF THE TOWN OF FAIRPLAY, COLORADO 
AMENDING SECTIONS 1-4-20 AND 1-4-30 OF THE FAIRPLAY MUNICIPAL CODE CONCERNING THE TOWN’S 
GENERAL PENALTY PROVISION TO COMPLY WITH RECENT COLORADO SUPREME COURT CASE LAW AND 
TO DECRIMINALIZE TOWN CODE VIOLATIONS."? (L) 
Mayor Douglas introduced  Ordinance No. 3  and the Board conducted the third reading . Town Attorney Rivera 
explained that the ordinance addressed  two legal developments. First, the Colorado Supreme Court decision in 
People v. Camp requires municipal court sanctions for the same conduct to be consistent with applicable state 
penalties. Second, recent legislation requires municipalities to provide access to a public defender or similar 
legal representation when municipal code violati ons carry the possibility of incarceration. Removing 
incarceration as a potential municipal court sanction would allow the Town to avoid the associated obligation 
and costs. Chief Worley explained that, in practice, serious or significant offenses are generally referred to county 
or district court, while officers exercise discretion based on the nature of the offense and the offender’s history. 
He stated that municipal court cases involving incarceration have been extremely uncommon and that the Police 
Department would generally refer serious or repeat offenses to the appropriate higher court. 
 
The Board discussed concerns about removing incarceration from the municipal court’s available sanctions, 
particularly the possibility that repeat or organized theft could be handled in municipal court without the 
possibility of jail. The Board also discussed the distinction between decriminalization and removing incarceration 
as a municipal court penalty. Rivera  clarified that the underlying conduct would remain a crime; the proposed 
change would remove incarceration as a potential municipal court sanction while leaving other penalties, such 
as fines or community service, available. Serious offenses could still b e prosecuted in county or district court, 
where incarceration remains an available sanction. The Board also discussed the alternative of retaining 
incarceration in the municipal code and providing access to a public defender or similar counsel. The Town 
Attorney advised that this would create additional administrative responsibilities, staff time, and potential costs 
to ensure appropriate legal representation was available when required.  
 
A typographical error in the ordinance title/body language was identified  and acknowledged by Town Attorney  
Rivera.

violation, municipal court summonses, or immediate abatement when health, safety, or welfare concerns exist. 
 
The Board discussed the importance of distinguishing between routine code enforcement and matters requiring 
Board involvement. Staff will continue providing updates on significant enforcement matters and will review 
applicable code provisions concerning vehicles, property maintenance, and use of the public right -of-way. 
The Board also discussed whether the community wants the Town to take a more proactive approach to 
longstanding code violations. Members expressed interest in obtaining public input through the 
UDC/Comprehensive Plan process before establishing new enforce ment priorities. It was suggested that 
residents be given advance notice and a reasonable compliance period if the Town adopts new or more 
consistently enforced standards. 
 
The Board discussed using community outreach, neighborhood cleanup efforts, and available Town resources to 
assist residents with property cleanup rather than relying solely on citations or penalties. Staff will explore 
opportunities for community cleanup events, business and organization participation, and better communication 
regarding existing beautification resources.  
 
 B. 2027 Budget Timeline and Process. 
Staff and the Board discussed development of the upcoming Town budget and a proposed process for prioritizing 
projects and expenditures. Five preliminary priorities were identified:  
1. Public safety and emergency preparedness;  
2. Infrastructure and utilities;  
3. Financial stability and fiscal responsibility;  
4. Community character and quality of life; and  
5. Economic vitality.  
The proposed weighting of the priorities was discussed, with public safety and infrastructure receiving the highest 
emphasis, followed by financial stability, community character, and economic vitality. Board members generally 
expressed support for the proposed priorities . The proposed process would allow staff, Board members, and 
consultants/contractors to submit potential projects or initiatives for consideration. Proposals would be 
evaluated using a rubric and then reviewed by the Board in light of available revenues, required expenditures, and 
overall Town priorities.  
 
The Board requested a simplified financial summary showing major revenue and expenditure categories, 
including totals for areas such as water, wastewater, debt service, and other significant revenues and expenses. 
Staff indicated that additional financial information would be available following completion of the six -month 
reconciliation and would be incorporated into the proposed budget. 
 
The Board discussed employee compensation, benefits, retention, and staffing levels as part of the budget 
process. Staff reported that the Town currently pays 100% of employee health, vision, dental, and long -term 
disability insurance costs, including fami ly coverage, and that the Town is awaiting information from its benefits 
broker regarding potential increases.

The state task force convened by Gov. Jared Polis to address the outbreak of a
destructive beetle released a draft list of recommendations Tuesday. 
The Ponderosa Mountain Pine Beetle Task Force was launched in December 2025 to
come up with solutions to slow the spread of the mountain pine beetle, which is
decimating ponderosa forests across the Front Range.
The group, which includes dozens of people from different state agencies, universities
and conservation groups, has been meeting since February, and is facing a fast-
approaching deadline to make recommendations in time for next year’s legislative
session.

Background/Analysis: 
The Town can charge reasonable fees incurred in responding to requests for records made under 
the Colorado Open Records Act (CORA) and the Colorado Criminal Justice Records Act (CCJRA). 
 
Under CORA, in response to a records request, the first hour of staff research and retrieval time is 
provided to the requestor at no charge. However, after the first hour, the Town may charge the 
requestor $41.37 per hour for search and retrieval and production costs incurred responding to the 
CORA request. 
 
Under the Colorado Criminal Justice Records Act (CCJRA), the Town may be able to seek 
reimbursement for some search and retrieval and disclosure costs incurred by the Town in 
response to a CCJRA request for records. 
 
Town staff seek to modify the Town’s Fee Schedule to collect reimbursable costs for CCJRA 
requests. Soon, Town staff will also seek to Town’s Fee Schedule for CORA requests.
