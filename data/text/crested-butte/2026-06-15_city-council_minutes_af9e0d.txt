and Subdivision regulations. The session will: 
1. Provide a lay of the land of the structure, as well as what is being proposed for adoption
now in Phase 1 (zoning) vs. Phase 2 later this year (incorporating updated Design

2. Review how the draft reflects the guiding strategies identified by Council and BOZAR
3. Revisit three unresolved items from earlier work sessions:

updates, see the: 
• February 2: Zoning Code Update (Residential Districts) packet
• March 2: Zoning Code Update (Commercial Districts) packet

1: Lay of the Land and Clarifying Phase One (Zoning) vs. Phase Two (Design 
Standards) 
The attached draft Land Use Code consolidates Chapter 16 (Zoning) and Chapter 17 (Subdivision) 
into a single, updated chapter. The structure follows the organization, standards, and best practices 
of the Colorado Division of Local Affairs Model Land Use Code. It modernizes and streamlines 
the Town’s regulations by improving clarity of definitions, introducing tables and graphics, and 
simplifying zoning districts. Each district includes clear baseline standards as well as defined 
incentive pathways where applicable.  
Please note that this draft includes yellow highlights to indicate sections where cross-references 
will be finalized once the document is fully complete and all feedback has been incorporated. 
Definitions will also undergo a final cleanup at that stage to remove any redundant or unnecessary 
common term language. In addition, three items are highlighted in turquoise to identify unresolved 
policy questions, which are addressed later in this memo. 
This draft focuses on Phase One (Zoning) updates: establishing the foundational zoning and 
subdivision framework: what is allowed, where it is allowed, and how development is structured. 
Phase Two (Design Standards), anticipated for adoption in December, will build on this foundation 
by incorporating updated Design Standards, introducing the Early Recreation-Era Period of 
Significance, relocating landscaping and fencing standards into the Design Standards section, and 
updating BOZAR architectural appropriateness review criteria, along with refinements to 
subdivision design standards. These Phase 2 components are identified in green text (Article 4, 
Sections 60–120; Article 7; Article 8; and Article 14, Section 30). 
Council Discussion: Staff seeks any feedback or questions on the overall format/flow of Draft 
Chapter 16. 
 
2: Reflecting on the Guiding Strategies 
The overall zoning code update follows the recommendations identified in the Town Council-
adopted Community Plan, as well as the guiding principles established by Town Council and 
BOZAR at the start of this process. The following is a reminder of the guiding principles and how 
the draft code responds to them: 
1. Simplify the Code: Make the zoning code and development review process easier to understand 
and navigate. 
Success could look like: A clearer, more user-friendly code for property owners, developers, staff, 
and decision-makers. 
 The Draft Code includes: 
• Modernized language and structure aligned more closely with other municipalities, while 
retaining Crested Butte’s unique character through district intents, contextual standards, 
and clear code purpose statements  
• Introduction of tables and graphics to improve usability and help users quickly understand 
key requirements  
4
3 
 
• Includes clear visual representation of baseline zoning standards and incentive pathways 
for applicable zone districts  
• Consolidation of the Town’s existing 22 zone districts into 11 districts, plus three overlay 
districts to better clarify requirements related to: the Historic Core/Mining-Era Period of 
Significance, floodplain areas, and steep slope conditions  
2. Retain Small-Town Feel: Celebrate the character of Crested Butte from the look and feel of our 
buildings to the people and moments that make this place feel like home. Keep zoning tools like 
Floor Area Ratio (FAR) limit that help neighborhoods stay authentic and approachable while 
allowing flexibility that supports connected, neighborly lifestyle.  
Success could look like: New buildings and updates feel like they belong and reinforce the small-
town character that defines Crested Butte. 
 The draft code includes: 
• FAR limits are largely maintained to preserve neighborhood scale, with the exception of 
adjustments in the Sixth Street and High Density Residential zones, where baseline FAR is 
reduced to the current minimum of the range and incentive FAR is increased to the current 
maximum of the range to strengthen the effectiveness of the incentive pathway 
• Height limits remain unchanged, except in the High Density Residential zone where a 35-
foot height allowance (increased from 30 feet) is available as part of the incentive structure 
• The FAR definition is refined for clarity and alignment with the International Building 
Code, and to remove ambiguity that previously allowed a “free” third story in certain 
single-family scenarios 
o A limited exemption is added to allow a 32-square-foot front porch, supporting 
improved pedestrian orientation and building scale 
o FAR incentives are introduced in the residential zones of not counting the garage or 
deed restricted ADU if a deed restricted unit is provided, which provides an 
incentive without significantly altering the scale of residential homes.   
3. Strengthen Historic Preservation: Implement the Historic Preservation Plan recommendations 
to create a new Early Recreation Era Period of Significance, and continue to maintain historic 
preservation for the Historic Core/Mining Era Period of Significance. 
Success could look like: Strong, enforceable protections for both the established historic core and 
a new overlay for Early Recreation Era properties currently being surveyed. 
 The draft code includes: 
• The Historic Core/Mining Era Period of Significance as a clearly defined overlay district, 
rather than defining the full Town as the historic district and relying on dispersed references 
across Zoning and Design Standards, to strengthen consistency and enforceability 
• Additional refinements to historic rehabilitation standards and architectural review criteria 
to improve clarity will be addressed in Phase Two 
• Establishment of the Early Recreation Era Period of Significance will also be addressed in 
Phase Two 
4. Integrate Land Use with Transportation and Climate Action: Update land use regulations to 
support community-serving housing and commercial spaces, and climate and mobility goals, to 
5
4 
 
provide more opportunities for people to live closer to where they work. 
Success could look like: Allowing ADUs and multi-family homes as permitted uses instead of 
conditional uses, which would reduce process barriers but maintain Design Standards. 
 The draft code includes: 
• Increased flexibility in allowable uses within each district while maintaining established 
FAR, height, and scale controls (for example, enabling configurations such as multiple 
smaller attached units in place of a larger single-family home and ADU) 
• Minor regulatory adjustments to better align with building code requirements, including 
allowing egress coverings and heat pump covers within FAR calculations, and permitting 
mechanical equipment within setback areas (Additional refinements related to design 
integration will be addressed in Phase Two through updated Design Standards) 
• Introduction of incentive-based zoning tools across districts tied to the provision of deed-
restricted housing or commercial space (see below) to provide more opportunities for 
people to live closer to where they work 
5. Link Zoning to Community Benefits: Implement the zoning incentives identified in the 
Community Plan in exchange for public benefits like community-serving housing and spaces, and 
improved mobility. Recommendations from the Community Plan include: 
Success could look like: Zoning incentives that reflects the character of each area while providing 
clear community benefits. 
• Residential Zones: Revamp incentives for ADUs, enable micro-lot subdivisions, and 
modernize the home occupation definition. 
• FAR bonuses and a basement allowance are provided as incentives in exchange for 
a deed restricted unit or ADU 
• ROAH requirements have been clarified to require 100% mitigation for 
residential projects that do not utilize the incentive/provide a deed-restricted 
unit 
• Micro-lot subdivisions or condominiumization for deed-restricted units are allowed 
as subdivision exemptions under the subdivision regulations 
• The home occupation definition has been updated with a focus on minimizing 
neighborhood impacts 
• High Density Residential (R4) Zone: Preserve and support compatible multi-family 
housing 
• FAR bonus from 0.6 to 1.0 and height bonus from 30 feet to 35 feet is included as 
an incentive for providing deed-restricted housing 
• High-density standards prioritize four or more attached units as permitted uses, 
allow two- and three-unit configurations as conditional uses (subject to site 
constraints), and remove single-family dwellings as an allowed use to preserve 
multi-family housing  
• Commercial Zone (Belleview): Provide flexibility on height and setbacks in exchange for 
a higher requirement of deed restricted commercial space and/or housing. Set the corridor 
up for improved pedestrian connectivity. 
6
5 
 
• Flexibility is provided for inclusion of residential and market-rate residential units, 
along with parking reductions, in exchange for deed-restricted commercial space 
and one deed-restricted residential unit 
• ROAH continues at 20% mitigation but is refined to emphasize on-site 
provision of units when the incentive is not utilized (and matches baseline 
residential unit allowances to fulfill ROAH) 
• Parking going forward required to be located at the rear of sites, improving 
pedestrian-oriented design outcomes 
• Business 2 Zone (Sixth Street): Improve the PUD process with clearer expectations and 
community benefits. Instead of incentivizing underground parking, tie incentives to 
community-serving housing and spaces. 
• FAR increases from 0.5 to 1.5, with flexibility for residential and market-rate 
residential uses, and reduced parking requirements in exchange for deed-restricted 
commercial space and one deed-restricted residential unit 
• ROAH continues at 20% mitigation, with an emphasis on delivering units 
on-site when incentives are not utilized 
• The PUD process has been streamlined to clarify expectations, strengthen 
community benefit requirements (including the definitions of community housing 
and community spaces), and improve review procedures for BOZAR and Council 
• Mobile Home Zone: Allow modular or stick-built units in exchange for permanent deed 
restrictions. 
• This proposed incentive has been removed at this time based on neighborhood 
feedback (see next section) 
• Town-Wide: Expand trails and sidewalk connections identified in the Transportation 
Mobility Plan, reduce parking requirements where appropriate, and maintain space for the 
realities of snow storage. 
• Parking requirements have been simplified into a clearer table, with administrative 
waivers available for residential projects providing deed-restricted units when 
located within 500 feet of a transit stop or within a parking management zone 
• Snow storage requirements have been clarified and more clearly organized within 
district standards tables 
• Trail and sidewalk connections are further being advanced through implementation 
of the Parks, Recreation, Open Space, and Trails Plan

3: Unresolved Items for Discussion  
The following three unresolved items are highlighted in the attached draft in turquoise, and 
Council direction is sought: 
1. Mobile Home Zone 
7
6 
 
Name, Incentive, Setbacks: The previous draft proposed renaming the Mobile Home District to 
Residential Small Lot, and included a potential incentive allowing replacement of existing mobile 
homes with stick-built or modular homes (up to 24 feet in height), in exchange for a permanent 
deed restriction on the property. The draft also proposed reducing the side setback from 5.5 feet to 
5 feet to provide additional flexibility for replacement while maintaining compliance with fire code 
and minimizing impacts to snow storage. 
This concept was intended to respond to a long-term practical issue: sourcing replacement mobile 
homes. Over time, replacement of mobile homes is becoming more difficult as 14-foot-wide units 
become less available. In the Town’s own efforts to identify a replacement unit, only two 14-foot-
wide options were found, reducing cost and layout competitiveness. In contrast, many 
manufacturers now primarily produce 15-foot-wide or wider models. 
Following additional neighborhood feedback (including the May 18 Council work session, 
subsequent individual conversations, and a mobile home neighborhood meeting), staff is 
recommending that the Town not proceed with renaming the district, introducing the proposed 
incentive, or adjusting the side setback at this time. 
This item could be revisited in the future if there are continued challenges related to mobile home 
replacement availability, or if Council wishes to further explore a deed-restricted housing incentive 
within the district. 
Land Use Clarifications: Since the last Council review, several land use adjustments have been 
made. Bed and breakfast uses and large childcare facilities have been removed from consideration 
the mobile home zone. Home occupation and small childcare (five children or fewer) remain 
permitted, consistent with other residential zone districts. 
All childcare uses remain subject to applicable State of Colorado licensing and insurance 
requirements, in addition to local zoning standards, including parking requirements. If a proposed 
childcare operation cannot meet state licensing standards or applicable parking requirements, the 
use would not be permitted. 
Council Discussion: Final direction is sought on the Mobile Home Zone approach moving 
forward. 
2. Temporary Ornamental (also known as Holiday) Lighting 
At the May 18 work session, Council expressed interest in simplifying temporary 
ornamental/holiday lighting regulations by establishing a single end date aligned with the end of 
ski season, along with introducing time-of-day restrictions. At the May BOZAR meeting, however, 
BOZAR expressed a preference to retain the existing staggered end dates of January 15 for 
residential properties and April 15 for commercial properties. 
The attached draft retains the current end dates (January 15 and April 15), while adding new time 
restrictions: residential holiday lighting would be limited to 10:00 p.m., and commercial lighting 
(including both holiday and architectural lighting) would be limited to 10:00 p.m. or close of 
business, whichever is later. 
Council Discussion: Final direction is sought on whether to maintain the existing seasonal end 
dates or move to a consolidated end date. 
8
7 
 
 
3. Digital Marquee Sign 
The primary sign code update under consideration is the potential allowance of a digital marquee 
sign for governmental uses, specifically to support improved community communication at the 
school. 
Council previously expressed general support for this concept; however, BOZAR did not support 
the allowance, citing concerns about precedent, loss of character, potential expansion beyond a 
single installation, and visual impacts at the entrance to Town. 
The attached draft refines the allowance to apply specifically to public school uses (rather than 
broader governmental uses) to address these concerns and narrow its scope. 
Council Discussion: Final direction is sought on whether to include or remove the digital marquee 
sign allowance.

related to zoning.

Previous Council Action: The regulatory framework for the Town’s affordable housing units has 
evolved over the past 30 years.  Council actions in this space include the following: 
 In 1995, the Council adopted its first housing guideline in the form of a master deed 
restrictions for Kapushion and Red Lady Estates, called the Town of Crested Butte 1995 
Affordable Housing Guidelines.  
 Through various amendments between 1995 and 2000, the Council expanded the 1995 
Guidelines to include the master deed restrictions for Poverty Gulch and Verzuh, make minor 
amendments as well as add policies and procedures for new affordable housing subdivisions.  
 In 2016, the Council adopted a generalized Affordable Housing Guideline that provided 
policies and procedures applicable to new development as well as owners that utilized the 
capital improvement policy to add value to their appreciation capped home.  From this time 
forward, new deed restrictions reference the Guidelines as amended rather than largely 
function as standalone documents. 
 In 2024, the Council removed the 2016 Guidelines from appendices of the Municipal Code 
and by resolution, adopted a new version updated for the best practices, changes in law and 
to include qualification and compliance for new housing programs.   
 The 2025 amendment clarified eligibility rules, administrative processes, and compliance 
procedures; removed references to GVRHA; aligned qualification requirements with current 
policy and law; refined the lottery and violation processes; and restructured the capital 
improvement policy. 
 
 
30
Background: The Town’s affordable housing units and programs are administered, operated and 
managed under various policy and regulatory documents.   
 
A deed restriction or restrictive covenant is a legal document recorded on a property that keeps 
housing affordable and locally focused over the long term. It defines who can occupy a unit, how it 
can be used, and, in some cases, how it can be sold or rented. Early deed restrictions included 
detailed administrative requirements within the document itself, while modern practice favors 
simpler deed restrictions that rely on the Affordable Housing Guidelines for administration. 
 
The Affordable Housing Guidelines set the Town’s policies for managing restricted units, like 
establishing application and qualification rules, monitoring compliance, and guiding program 
administration. Maintaining policies and procedures outside of the deed restriction allow for 
periodic updates to align policy with best practices and changes in the legal landscape. Together, 
deed restrictions and Guidelines ensure public and private housing investment serves the 
community now and into the future. The Town is incrementally updating older deed restrictions as 
units transfer through normal turnover or when owners participate in capital improvements that 
add to home value, to help keep administration consistent and easier to manage

ubject:     A discussion examining Save-As-You-Throw 2026 – 2027 bin fees to fund 
sanitation-related Town programs 
S
ummary: Staff are conducting an annual assessment of SAYT bin fees ahead of the bin selection 
period in August 2026. Council is asked to provide direction on whether future SAYT fees should 
simply recover program costs or also help fund related sanitation programs such as Town Cleanup, 
Yard Waste Pickup, and residential drop-off composting. 
P
revious Council Action: Town Council adopted the Save-As-You-Throw (SAYT) trash program on 
August 18, 2025. Council adopted the 2026 fee schedule, which sets the SAYT fees, on November 3, 
2025. Council directed staff to set pricing so that the Town might collect enough revenue to offset the 
subsidization of the residential drop-off compost program (approximately $21,000). On October 6, 
2026, following the enrollment period, staff discussed with Council that the SAYT fees would bring in 
a sanitation revenue of approximately $120,000 due to the high distribution of 96-gallon bins. Council 
directed staff to adjust the bin fees to reduce sanitation revenue. 
B
ackground: 
In 2025, the Town launched Save as You Throw (SAYT), giving residents a choice of 32-, 64-, or 96-
gallon trash bins, with the 96-gallon recycling bin included in the Waste Management (WM) rate. 
Residents with trash services under the Town contract may elect their bin size annually in August. 
During the initial enrollment period, 590 of 1,141 households selected a bin size:  
• 36% (410) downsized from 96 gallons to 64 or 32 gallons,
• 4% (43) upsized to 96 gallons,
• 12% (137) remained at 96 gallons.
T
he remaining 551 households did not respond and retained their existing bin size. Households may 
select a new bin size upon new ownership or occupancy or within the annual enrollment period, 
scheduled for August 2026.

Council to adjust these rates appropriately. 2026 bin rates were codified in the fee schedule as follows:

The Town expects to net approximately $3,790.17 in sanitation revenue through the SAYT program 
for December 2025 – August 2026. Revenues were significantly lower than anticipated for a few 
reasons: 
• Waste Management’s contracted annual Consumer Price Index (CPI) cost increases for 2026 
were inadvertently not factored in. 
• Waste Management experienced significant delays delivering bins to households due to 
difficulties providing accurate billing data for several months. Bins were not delivered to 
households until late November 2025 (originally scheduled for September 2025), so Town did 
not bill for SAYT bins until December 2025. 
 
As the program enters its first full year of operation, Council has an opportunity to revisit fee levels to 
ensure revenues keep pace with service costs and desired level of investment in related sanitation 
programs. 
 
Discussion: 
Waste Management and Increasing  Service  Costs  
At a minimum, SAYT fees will need to increase to account for WM's annual contracted CPI 
adjustment. Over the past two years, CPI increases for trash services have averaged 9%. Due to the 
2026 Iranian War and its effect on fuel pricing, staff are anticipating that WM will include additional 
fuel surcharges in late 2026 or 2027. Staff are working to confirm this with WM. To be conservative, 
staff included a 4% fuel surcharge in the proposed 2026–2027 bin fees based on historical surcharges 
and will continue contacting WM to confirm whether a surcharge is anticipated. 
 
Leveraging SAYT fees to fund Town Programs  
Council could consider leveraging SAYT bin fees to fund a few of the sanitation-related programs that 
Town has historically paid for. Those programs are: 
 
• Town Cleanup (budgeted at $7,000/year) 
• Yard Waste Pickup ($4,800/year) 
• Residential Compost Drop Off Program (Subsidy of approximately $17,000/yr). 
 
Because these programs directly support the Town's waste reduction and diversion goals, Council may 
wish to consider whether some or all their costs should be funded through the SAYT program. Doing 
so would create a more direct connection between sanitation services and sanitation-related program 
funding while reducing reliance on other Town revenue sources.

Council is being asked about pricing now so staff can advertise preliminary 2026 - 2027 bin fees 
during the August 2026 enrollment period. Final rates would be adopted in December with the annual 
fee schedule, once the Town has confirmed CPI adjustments and any fuel surcharges from Waste 
Management. This approach ensures residents receive clear pricing information during enrollment 
while allowing the Town to finalize fees once WM cost data is available. 
How Account Consolidation affects SAYT Bin Pricing  
The Town is currently updating Chapter 13, Article 5, to allow two residential units under common 
ownership on the same parcel to share trash and recycling service. Because Waste Management costs 
are pass-through expenses, this change is not expected to affect the Town financially under the current 
fee structure. However, if Council elects to use SAYT fees to fund Town programs and a significant 
number of households consolidate service, staff may need to return with additional analysis and fee 
recommendations. 
SAYT Bin Change Fees  
WM currently charges households a $25 fee for selecting a new bin during enrollment to cover bin 
removal and delivery costs. Throughout the year, staff have received community concerns requesting 
this fee be removed for this first re-enrollment period. WM indicated the fee could only be eliminated 
if the Town provided a central drop-off site and assisted with distributing bins. Instead, staff propose 
that the Town absorb the fee. Assuming a 10% bin change rate, this would cost the Town 
approximately $3,000 in 2026.  
E
nvironmental Impact:  
The SAYT program was implemented to help residents save money by throwing away less. However, 
staff does not yet have sufficient residential-specific data to evaluate program effectiveness, and 
current data does not include the summer tourism season, which typically generates the highest waste 
volumes. Staff recommends returning to Council in December 2026, or alternatively Spring 2027, with 
a more complete and seasonally representative dataset. 
F
inancial Impact:   
To maintain the financial sustainability of the SAYT program, staff recommend increasing fees at least 
enough to cover the anticipated 9% CPI adjustment and anticipated fuel surcharges from Waste 
Management. Council may also consider whether SAYT revenues should support additional 
sanitation-related programs and include a modest contingency for potential fuel surcharges.
