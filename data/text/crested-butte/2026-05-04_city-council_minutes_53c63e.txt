Executive Summary 
Crested Butte has strengthened its financial structure and transparency over the past three budget 
cycles, yet long-term operating and capital pressures persist across General, Capital, Parks, 
Recreation & Trails (PR&T), Transit & Mobility, and Affordable Housing funds.  These pressures 
reflect fixed service costs and the rising price of living in high demand markets, including increased 
personnel, health insurance, construction, and operating expenses.  At the same time, our financing 
model relies heavily on sales tax, which is sensitive to occupancy and spending patterns.  When 
homes sit vacant or are used only part-time, everyday local spending drops, and the cost of 
maintaining year-round services shifts more heavily to full-time residents.  
 
Council requested that staff examine new revenue tools, most notably an excise tax on vacant or 
underused homes, while considering targeted resident relief such as a General Fund property tax 
rebate.  This report presents the current financial context, the implications of underutilized housing 
for the Town’s budget, and practical options to sustain core services and align with community 
priorities.  Council direction is sought on whether to advance an excise tax concept to the 
November 2026 ballot and whether to pursue a resident property tax rebate from the General Fund 
to keep affordability front and center.  
 
 
Background (2024–2026) 
Over the past two years, the Town refined its budget framework to provide clearer fund distinctions, 
more disciplined reserves, and improved transparency.  These steps revealed structural gaps in 
specific program areas and highlighted the need for dependable, equitable revenue to sustain 
Council priorities over time. 
 
1) Multi-Year Budget & Financial Structure Improvements 
• New budgetary funds separate program areas and clarify the use of dedicated revenues. 
• A comprehensive capital plan now anticipates facility maintenance and replacement needs. 
• Across the board budget cuts of 8% in preparation of the 2025 budget.

• Limitations on new staff positions, acknowledging personnel costs as a major driver of 
long-term operating expenses, until revenues become more sustainable. 
• The Town also adopted a reserves policy that defines targets for fund balances, capital 
reserves, and contingencies to maintain stability through economic cycles. 
 
2) Adjustments to Existing Revenue Sources 
• Increased mill levy for the Street & Alley Fund from 8.0 to 10 mills to address infrastructure 
maintenance needs.  
• Reallocated Specific Ownership Tax revenues from the General fund to Street & Alley fund 
(~$240,000 annually), reducing pressure for further mill increases. 
 
These incremental moves helped, but projections show they are not sufficient to meet broader 
capital and operating obligations across funds. 
 
3) Strategic Community-Guided Planning 
• The Community Compass established shared long-term priorities for Crested Butte.  
• Subsequent plans (housing, mobility, climate, infrastructure, community spaces) consistently 
point to the need for stable, predictable funding to keep residents, especially local workers 
and families, thriving. 
 
4) Emerging Conclusion: Explore New/Expanded Revenues 
Despite prudent budgeting, rising service expectations and costs outpace current revenues. 
Exploring revenue options complements efficiency work and supports transparent delivery of 
services residents expect. 
 
 
Discussion 
A. Where Crested Butte Stands Today 
Five funds drive long-term sustainability concerns: 
 
• General Fund — Supports the broadest range of services and subsidizes structural gaps 
elsewhere.  Even with strong reserves today, projections show a declining net spendable 
balance under conservative assumptions (≈1% revenue growth; ≈3% expenditure growth), 
driven by rising demands from Capital, PR&T, and Transit.  
• Capital Fund — Underwrites facilities, vehicles, and major projects, including upkeep of 
existing facilities, renovation of the Marshal’s Office, rehabilitation of Jerry’s Gym, and 
planning for a Community Hub.  Without additional revenue, severe additional prioritization 
and external funding will be necessary as obligations exceed available resources. 
• Parks, Recreation & Trails (PR&T) Fund — Created in 2024 for transparency around 
restricted 0.5% sales-tax revenues; clearly shows shortfalls in recreation and parks/trails 
operations.  Absent improved cost recovery or added revenue, transfers from the General 
and Capital funds would have to grow to sustain current access and service levels. 
• Transit & Mobility Fund — Dedicated transit revenues (¼ of the 4% sales tax) do not fully 
cover ongoing program costs or potential enhancements in the Transportation Mobility 
Plan.  The operating reserve (seeded from the General Fund when created) is projected to be 
exhausted by 2028, after which General Fund support resumes.

C.  What It Means to Live in an Amenities-Based Community: Who Pays and Who Benefits 
Residents carry rising costs in housing, insurance, groceries, and utilities and absorb year-round 
impacts of tourism, including traffic and parks demand.  Part-time residents contribute to 
community life and vibrancy, but prolonged underuse of housing reduces everyday sales-tax 
revenue and shifts the cost of year-round services onto those who live here full time.  Visitors, 
meanwhile, contribute substantially through sales and lodging taxes and related fees, which helps 
keep local property-tax burdens lower than they would otherwise be for residents.

D. Property Values & Property Taxes Statewide — Key Context 
Rapid increases in home values, especially in mountain towns, alongside the repeal of the 
Gallagher Amendment have changed property-tax dynamics statewide.  Temporary relief 
measures enacted between 2021 and 2024 have sunset, and 2025 begins a new long-term 
structure.  In resort counties such as Gunnison, taxable-value jumps produced notable bill 
increases even during the relief period.  Crested Butte property owners have experienced overall 
mill increases in recent years.  Note that mill levies for various entities vary annually based on 
ballot measures, TABOR, and decisions by local jurisdictions. 
 
 
 
E. The Homestead Exemption for Property Tax 
Thirty-eight states and the District of Columbia provide homestead exemptions or credits to 
reduce property taxes on primary residences, often adding targeted benefits for seniors, disabled 
residents, or veterans.  Colorado does not provide a universal homestead exemption, and 
uniformity requirements set at the state level prevent local exemptions.  Notable states with 
generous or broad programs include Florida, Texas, Kansas, Iowa, and South Dakota.   
 
Staff looked at a couple of examples from Texas to illustrate the differences: Texas has no state 
personal income tax and relies heavily on property and sales taxes, but its homestead exemption 
substantially reduces primary-residence tax bills (often by thousands of dollars per year) with no 
benefits for second homes.  A detailed chart showing comparable property tax burdens on 
primary residences in Colorado and two Texas locations can be found in Appendix B of this 
document. 
 
Colorado’s approach to residential property taxes treats primary and non-primary homes largely 
the same, which can burden year-round residents in high-cost resort markets. 
 
F. Understanding Regressivity & Fairness (Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy Tax 
Inequality Index; Minneapolis Fed Research) 
Colorado’s state and local tax mix leans regressive: sales and property taxes weigh more heavily 
on lower-income residents unless targeted relief is applied.  National research shows federal 
taxation is progressive while many state and local systems are less equal; strong, targeted relief 
programs improve affordability for resident households.

G. Sales Tax vs. Property Tax: Tradeoffs
In Crested Butte, sales tax offers administrative simplicity and a funding model in which visitors
contribute significantly. Yet it is more volatile and can weigh more heavily on lower-income
households. Property tax provides stable, predictable revenue that supports long-term
operations and infrastructure, but it directly impacts homeowners and may affect rents, with
rising values in resort markets magnifying burdens unless paired with resident protections.
Considering an Excise Tax on Vacant and Part-Time Residences 
Across Colorado’s resort communities in places like Steamboat Springs, local governments have 
started taking a hard look at the budget impact of homes that remain vacant or under-used for most 
of the year.  Steamboat’s analysis last year showed that vacant homes create a net general fund cost 
of roughly $3,000 per home per year, after allocating sales tax, department revenues, and service 
costs across full-time residents, part-time residents, vacant units, and visitors.  These analyses 
consistently show that prolonged under-occupancy reduces everyday local spending and increases 
the degree to which full-time residents carry the fixed costs of year-round services.  Crested Butte’s 
own evaluation reflects this same pattern. 
This is not about criticizing second-home ownership.  Our part-time residents are valued members 
of the Crested Butte community who contribute in many meaningful ways, from volunteering and 
philanthropy to cultural and economic vibrancy.  This discussion is focused specifically on 
prolonged vacancy and the measurable gap it creates between year-round service demands and the 
revenue available to support them.

To understand the local implications, staff developed a financial model grounded in the 2024 
housing census, current revenue sources, and household 
spending behavior.  The census shows that 36% of 
homes in Crested Butte are not used as primary 
residences, about 465 homes in total, including 283 that 
are not short-term rentals and therefore remain unused 
for long stretches of the year.  These “darkest” homes 
generate substantially less sales-tax revenue than homes 
occupied full-time. 
 
1) What the local data tells us 
We began by analyzing 2025 sales tax data in key categories (grocery, retail, hardware) and focused 
on “shoulder season” months (April, May, October, November) to approximate spending patterns 
of full-time residents.  That monthly activity was then annualized to estimate total yearly spending 
per household. 
 
Use of part-time residences varies greatly.  An article from the Federal Reserve Bank of Richmond 
in 2008 included findings that half of second homeowners spend two weeks or less per year in these 
homes, and two-thirds of them spend four weeks or less.  To estimate activity for non-primary 
homes, we applied a conservative occupancy assumption of approximately 20% of the year (about 
10 weeks).  This scales down expected spending to reflect periods when homes are not occupied. 
 
We then allocated Town revenues (including sales tax, 
property tax, and certain fees) across household types 
based on these usage patterns.  This results in an 
estimated annual contribution of approximately $3,200 
per full-time household compared to approximately 
$1,200 per non-primary household, or a difference of 
roughly $2,000 per home per year. 
 
Applied across approximately 465 non-primary homes, 
this equates to an estimated $940K annual gap in 
revenue supporting general government services.  
Additional methodological details are provided in 
Appendix C for Council’s reference. 
 
To understand the budget reality of this under-use, staff built a detailed financial model.   
• Modeling assumption: non-primary homes occupied ~20% of the year (conservative vs. 
national trends). 
• Estimated unrealized sales-tax revenue: ≈$794,568 annually (≈$1,709 per underused home).  
• Broader unrealized taxes/fees: ≈$943,540 (≈$2,029 per non-primary home). 
 
This illustrates a fiscal imbalance: full-time residents shoulder fixed costs of a year-round town, 
while prolonged underuse reduces everyday spending that sustains services.

4) Practical Measures 
Staff suggests that if the Council pursues a tax of this nature that we focus on covering core services 
while ensuring Crested Butte is a highly desirable community for residents, second-homeowners, 
and visitors while maintaining affordability for local residents and respecting all property owners, 
including long-time part-time residents who care deeply about Crested Butte. 
 
To keep the excise focused and equitable, Council could: 
• Target the right properties — non-primary homes with very low annual occupancy; 
consider a narrower tier of non-STR, non-primary homes for the most vacancy-focused 
version.  
• Use practical definitions of “under-use” — not used as a primary residence e.g. no 
occupants make their primary home in the residence as demonstrated by voter registration, 
tax filings, driver’s license or other common measures.  
• Build sensible exemptions — full-time residences, long-term rentals, homes under 
renovation, medical/hardship cases, estate/probate situations. 
• Pair the measure with resident protections — the Town’s model includes an optional 
rebate tool to return a set amount to full-time households, keeping affordability front and 
center.  
• Require transparency — annual public reporting on collections, expenditures, and 
community outcomes.

There are key components for consideration should the Town Council proceed with this type of tax.  
Considerations include topics like:

Staff recommend that if the Council decides to pursue an excise tax, we set aside time over the next 
two months to delve further into these details. 
 
 
Resident Affordability — Current and Ongoing Efforts 
The Town is already investing in affordability across multiple policy areas.  Deed-restricted housing 
helps maintain year-round workforce housing and the programs necessary to administer it.  Water 
and trash rate structures keep basic household use affordable while applying higher rates to higher 
consumption, and utility assistance, including senior support such as berm clearing, reduces resident 
expenses.  Low- and no-cost recreation, youth programming, trails access, and shared community 
spaces reduce barriers to participation.  Investments in transit and mobility, such as contributions to 
Mountain Express and support for Late Night Taxi, lower transportation costs.  The General Fund 
subsidizes community services that are essential to livability but do not fully recover their costs.  In 
2026, the Town is projected to transfer $974,583 million from the General Fund to Street & Alley to 
support critical maintenance, reducing the property-tax increase that would otherwise have been 
required.  Finally, ongoing development of the Housing and Community Spaces strategies aims to

expand shared facilities and services, while reliance on visitor-supported revenues, especially sales 
tax, continues to fund municipal services through visitor spending rather than primarily through 
property taxes on year-round residents. 
 
 
Consideration of Property-Tax Rebates (General Fund) 
Because Colorado does not offer a universal homestead exemption, Council has asked staff to 
consider a rebate of some property taxes to target affordability for primary-residence households.  
To keep Street & Alley operating largely independently, staff focused only on General Fund 
property tax.   
 
In 2026, General Fund mills are set at 1.889, representing about 16% of the Town’s mill levy and 
estimated to generate $363,207.  Approximately 30% of General Fund property-tax revenue is paid 
by residential properties and 70% by commercial properties.  If the entire portion of the General 
Fund property tax for properties used as a primary residence were rebated, it would amount to 
about $70,000 in current dollars.  This is an illustrative figure to help frame the discussion.

Staff Report 
May 4, 2026 
 
 
 
To:   Mayor and Town Council 
 
Thru: Dara MacDonald, Town Manager 
 
From: Lauren Hawcroft, Finance and Administrative Services Director 
 
Subject:   Spring 2026 Community Grant Recommendations 
 
 
Summary 
Each year the Town supports the community by providing financial resources to non-
governmental organizations for special projects and programs that supplement municipal 
government operations and enhance quality of life for the community.  The 2026 budget 
includes $278,000 for the Community Grants Program.  Because grant funds awarded in the 
fall are paid out in the spring of the following year, the 2026 budget is comprised of awards 
made for both the fall 2025 and the spring 2026 grant cycles. 
 
Previous Council Action 
February 17, 2026:  Council awarded $226,486 in community grants comprised of $175,000 in 
nicotine-related grant funds and $51,486 in general community grant funds.  This action 
resulted in $51,514 being available for award during the spring 2026 grant cycle. 
 
Background 
General Community Grants are funded via the General Fund from various revenue sources.  
These grants account for $103,000 of the total $278,000 budgeted amount.  Nicotine-related 
tax revenues collected via the General Fund provide funding for the remaining $175,000.   
General Community Grants can be awarded to any qualifying applicant.  Nicotine Tax 
Grants are intended to support local organizations addressing mental and physical health, 
substance abuse, teen vaping, and other needs as Council sees fit. 
 
Per the Community Grant Guidelines adopted by Council in the fall of 2021, qualifying 
applicants include: 
 
Applicant Qualifications 
• An organization holding a current tax-exempt status under Section 501(c) of the IRS 
Code 
 
• Applicants should be organizations of the Crested Butte community (Upper Valley 
preference) 
 
16
2 
 
• Applicants must provide significant and measurable benefits (offsetting pandemic 
impacts) to the greater Crested Butte community 
 
• Applicants must be fiscally responsible with clear budgets, no past-due debts, etc. 
 
• Organizations may not be religious nor political in nature (Note: CRS §31-15-901(c) 
authorizes jurisdictions to appropriate funds to aid and foster charity organizations; 
however, no money can be provided to an organization wholly or in part under sectarian or 
denominational control) 
 
In addition: 
Strong Community Grant Proposals Should Demonstrate: 
• Funding requests must align with one or more of the guiding principles listed above 
 
• Community support is evident through local contributions / volunteer engagement 
 
• Organization has a track record of effective outcomes 
 
• Use of funds is clearly articulated within overall project budget 
 
• Good planning is evident in the project timetable, goals, and staffing 
 
• Plans for evaluating impact are clear, appropriate, measurable, and achievable 
 
 
Typically, individual awards for General Community Grants are limited to 50% of the total 
project/program cost up to a maximum award of $5,000.   Grant applications are reviewed 
and evaluated by the Community Grant Review Committee (Committee).  The Committee 
includes representation from the Town Council and Town staff.  The spring 2026 grant cycle 
Committee consists of Councilmember Kent Cowherd, Councilmember Gabi Prochaska and 
Finance and Administrative Services Director Lauren Hawcroft.  The Committee utilized 
existing grant guidelines and historical evaluation criteria to review all requests. Greater 
emphasis and funding were afforded to grant applications focusing on local health and 
human services initiatives.    
 
Discussion 
The Town has $51,514 in Community Grant funds after accounting for awards from the fall 
2025 cycle.  The Town received 22 separate grant applications for the spring 2026 cycle 
requesting $103,553 in financial support from the Town.  After adjusting the requests to a 
maximum of $5,000 per request, not exceeding 50% of the project/program, the level of 
financial request from applicants totaled $91,955.  The Committee determined two applicants 
for grant funding did not meet eligibility requirements:  one applicant already received the 
maximum funding for the same program during the fall 2025 cycle; one applicant sought 
funding for a program already required of them via a contract with the Town.   
 
The Committee aligned each of the General Community Grant requests to a primary 
Community Compass goal to help evaluate each request.  The Committee reviewed each 
application in the context of existing evaluation criteria.  The Committee determined the 
highest priority of funding should be given to applications demonstrating a positive impact to

General Community Grant funds during the spring 2026 grant cycle per the attached matrix.   
 
Recommendation 
The Community Grant Evaluation Committee recommends the Town Council approve the 
awards identified for the spring 2026 grant cycle, per the attached matrix.

3) Town Water Intake Early Warning-Detection Project – The Town has engaged with HydroLogik to
develop a proposal and cost estimate that would include the installation of an automated valve and
turbidimeter and/or ORP probe.  This system would enable the Town to detect changes in water
chemistry/quality and isolate the Town’s drinking water intake to prevent the contamination of the
Town’s raw water conveyance infrastructure.  Once a scope of work and cost estimate are received,
the Town will engage with Colorado Water Conservation Board (CWCB) to solicit funding assistance
for the project.
4) Emergency Alternate Water Supply - Due to the current drought conditions and potential wildfire
danger this season, Town staff are exploring the ability to employ a temporary diversion and pipeline
on the Slate River.  This system would deliver water to the Town’s Water Treatment Plant in the
event that the Coal Creek water supply would become compromised.  Staff met with engineers on
April 23rd to perform an initial site visit and develop preliminary concepts.

4) Oh Be Joyful solar array: The Oh Be Joyful (OBJ) Solar Array is producing power as the sun 
shines! The Town has committed to purchase enough renewable energy credits (RECs) to offset 
100% of the Town’s municipal electricity use. RECs allow the Town to financially support 
renewable energy on Tri-State’s electric grid to offset the Town’s use of partially fossil-fueled 
electricity use. In 2025, GCEA estimated the Town would pay an additional $33,000 annually for 
its RECs, depending on electricity use. GCEA expects to bill the Town for REC fees beginning 
in May 2026. REC costs are expected to remain relatively stable over time.
