The purpose of this work session is to build a shared understanding among Council and staff of the 
Town’s financial trajectory and the policy choices that may lie ahead. 
 
Specifically, the session will review: 
• The Town’s financial trajectory 
• Structural budget pressures 
• Planning commitments vs. fiscal capacity 
• Affordability impacts on year-round residents 
 
The goal of the discussion is not to reach decisions today, but to ensure Council shares a common 
understanding of the Town’s financial trajectory before exploring policy options in later work sessions.  
This session will not be used to decide on new revenue.  Rather, it should establish baseline facts, 
shared definitions, and shared values. 
 
To help keep the discussion focused at a policy level, staff suggests organizing the conversation 
around four strategic themes that connect financial decisions to community goals.  These themes can 
help frame Council’s discussion during this work session:  
 
While the memo includes references to specific projects or initiatives, the purpose of this work session 
is not to evaluate individual projects but rather to discuss how Council wishes to balance these 
broader strategic priorities.

2. Affordability Context: What Town is Already Doing –  
Before discussing financial pressures and potential future policy choices, it is important to recognize 
that the Town is already making significant investments and policy decisions intended to support 
affordability and the year-round community. 
 
These efforts span several policy areas including housing, municipal services, recreation access, and 
spending policy.  Taken together, these policies represent an intentional effort by the Town to support 
affordability through multiple policy areas rather than relying on a single program or funding source.   
The Town’s current affordability strategies include: 
• Deed-restricted housing investments and program administration to maintain a year-round 
workforce housing supply. 
• Water and trash rate structures that keep basic household use affordable while applying higher 
rates to higher levels of consumption. 
• Utility assistance and targeted services for qualifying residents such as senior utility support 
and berm clearing assistance. 
• Low- and no-cost recreation programming, youth programs, trails access, and community 
spaces that ensure residents can participate in community life without high financial barriers. 
• Investment in transit and mobility programs, including Mountain Express contributions and 
programs such as Late-Night Taxi that reduce transportation costs for residents and workers. 
• General Fund support of community services such as recreation programming and mobility 
programs that do not fully pay for themselves but are considered important to community 
livability. 
• 2026 General Fund transfer of $1.17 million to the Street & Alley Fund, which allowed the 
Town to complete critical infrastructure maintenance while reducing the size of the property 
tax increase that would otherwise have been required.

3. Where the Town Is Today: Financial Baseline –  
Before discussing future choices, it is helpful to briefly revisit where the Town stands financially today.  
Council and staff spent significant time in 2025 developing a clearer shared understanding of the 
Town’s fund structure, revenue sources, operational cost drivers, and reserve policies.  Actions that 
resulted from this effort included a new fund reserves policy, and stabilization of the Street and Alley 
fund through a one-time infusion from the General Fund and property tax increase. 
 
For budget purposes, the Town maintains 9 funds.  Four of these typically operate without support 
from the General or Capital funds – Open Space, Utility Enterprise, Conservation Trust, and Street & 
Alley.  These four funds are currently positioned to function independently and within their dedicated 
funding sources and thus are not the focus of this series of work sessions.  Rather we will focus 
discussions on the five remaining funds – General, Capital, Transit & Mobility, Parks Recreation & 
Trails (PR&T), and Affordable Housing – and their long-term financial sustainability. 
 
Present and prior Town Councils have been financially prudent.  That restraint, along with increasing 
revenues in recent years, has resulted in the Town being in a strong financial position, able to maintain 
operations and services without too much anxiety.  However, several structural trends suggest that the 
Town’s current trajectory may not be sustainable over the long term without policy adjustments.  At 
the same time, the Town has identified a number of important priorities and projects that exceed 
current financial capacity.   
 
Attached you will find the financial summary sheets from the 2026 budget for each of the five funds.  
Of note are the declining fund balances in four of the five – General, Capital, PR&T, and Transit & 
Mobility.  Transit & Mobility and PR&T both have residual fund balances reflecting the former policy 
of maintaining 100% of annual operations in reserve, so it is a couple of years before the Net 
Spendable Fund Balance goes negative.  In the financial summary sheets, we have not projected inter-
fund transfers beyond 2027.  Following are brief overviews of what is behind these declining balances.  
While the Affordable Housing fund is projected to maintain a positive fund balance, it should be 
noted that there is little in the way of new investment planned in the current budget. 
 
The following sections summarize the primary sustainability dynamics within the five funds that are 
most relevant to this discussion.  The goal is not to examine each fund in detail, but to highlight the 
structural trends shaping the Town’s long-term financial trajectory.

The following sections summarize the primary sustainability challenges within the five funds that 
are most relevant to this discussion. 
 
General Fund: The General fund is relied upon for general operations of the Town and includes

Legal and Town Council as well as shared general governmental expenses.  This fund is also 
relied upon to supplement one-time projects such as the 2026 Streets project and the purchase

The Town has built strong reserves in this fund in recent years.  In the attached financial

Even without these anticipated transfers, the Net Spendable Fund Balance is projected to

growing reliance of other funds on transfers from the General Fund could become concerning if

Capital Fund: The Capital fund is the backbone of funding for Town facilities, vehicles, other 
capital improvements, and maintenance.  Operations of the facilities division reside in this fund.  
Transfers of funds from the Capital Fund may be approved by the Town Council to the

renovation and remodel of the Marshal’s office, Jerry’s Gym renovation, and the Community 
Hub at 3rd & Maroon, in addition to ongoing facility maintenance, vehicle replacement and other 
smaller projects.  Prioritization and aggressive pursuit of grants and financing will be necessary 
to achieve some of the larger anticipated projects. 
 
Parks, Recreation & Trails Fund: The PR&T fund was created in 2024 with the rewriting of 
Chapter 4 of the Municipal Code.  With voter approval of an additional 0.5% sales & use tax for

use tax revenues are used properly. 
 
 There are two divisions within the PR&T fund – parks & trails and recreation.  Recreation

by the Capital fund.

C. Consultants 
Consultants are not intended to replace staff capacity or community leadership.  Instead, they 
provide specialized expertise and flexibility when needs exceed what a small organization can 
sustainably maintain in-house.  Consultants can provide several benefits to a small organization 
such as Crested Butte: 
 
1. Access to specialized skills and expertise 
2. Cost efficiency for intermittent or short-term needs 
3. Capacity relief during peak workloads 
4. Speed and project delivery 
5. Independence and objectivity 
6. Regulatory, Legal, or risk management needs 
7. Temporary or pilot programs 
8. Technology and systems implementation

D. Fund Balance Reserve Policy 
The Fund Balance Reserve Policy ensures the Town maintains a prudent financial safety net to 
protect against revenue volatility, unexpected costs, and economic uncertainty.  Reserves are 
designed to function as risk protection and cash-flow stability, not as an ongoing funding source 
for normal operations or service expansion.   
 
After much consideration and discussion, the Town Council made a prudent decision in 2025 to 
reduce the required fund reserve balances in most funds.  This decision made available substantial 
one-time funds in spendable fund balances.  An example of this is the 2026 transfer of $1.17M 
from the General fund to the Street and Alley fund to support an off-cycle mill and overlay 
project.   
 
While reserves can be used temporarily to address short-term shocks or one-time needs, policy 
and best practice limit their use for sustaining recurring services, and any drawdown must include 
a plan for replenishment.  In short, reserves can buy time, but they cannot solve structural budget 
gaps.  Long-term imbalances must be addressed through sustainable revenue, cost alignment, or 
service-level decisions.

Collectively, these plans represent a strong community vision for the Town’s future, but they also 
create expectations for implementation that must be aligned with financial capacity.  When the 
actions contained in these plans are compared against the Town’s projected financial capacity, it 
becomes clear that the full set of identified initiatives cannot be implemented simultaneously 
within current revenue assumptions.   
 
This dynamic is common in planning: community plans tend to identify the full range of desirable 
outcomes, while the annual budget process must determine what is realistically achievable within 
available resources.  As the Town’s financial projections show, several funds, including Capital, 
Parks Recreation & Trails, and Transit & Mobility, are already trending toward structural deficits if 
current spending patterns continue.  Aligning planning commitments with financial capacity will 
therefore require careful prioritization, phasing of projects, and identification of additional funding 
strategies.  
 
To better understand these tradeoffs, staff compiled a matrix of initiatives drawn from adopted 
and in-process plans and asked Council members to rank them according to relative priority.  
Rankings were submitted by 6 of the 7 Council members and averaged for each initiative or 
project.  When sorted by Council’s scoring, several clear patterns emerge.  The purpose of the 
priority matrix is not to determine the fate of individual projects.  Rather, the exercise helps 
illuminate where Council priorities are concentrated and how those priorities interact with the 
Town’s financial capacity. 
 
It is important to note that the priority matrix does not yet include potential Town-led projects or 
initiatives that may emerge from the Housing & Community Spaces Strategy currently underway.  
That effort is still in development and is expected to identify potential community-serving spaces, 
facilities, and programmatic initiatives that could carry significant capital and operational costs.  As 
those recommendations become more defined, they may introduce additional priorities that are 
not reflected in the current ranking exercise.  Council may therefore wish to keep some flexibility 
in its prioritization discussions to accommodate projects that emerge from that strategy. 
 
 
A. High-Priority and Structural Commitments 
A number of initiatives received consistently high priority scores and fall into the category of 
either committed or structurally important projects.  These include items such as the zoning and 
subdivision code update, corridor and mobility planning efforts, continued participation in 
Mountain Express operations, implementation of housing strategies, and certain facility and 
infrastructure needs. 
 
These items largely fall into one of three categories: regulatory requirements, core municipal 
services, or initiatives already underway through prior Council direction.  Several also carry 
operational or risk-management implications.  For example, zoning updates are necessary to 
maintain alignment with adopted housing and land use policy, while transit contributions and 
mobility programs reflect ongoing commitments within the Transportation Mobility Plan.  
Similarly, the Marshal’s facility project is tied to long-term public safety needs and aging 
infrastructure.

These high-priority initiatives also tend to fall into higher implementation cost bands (Bands 3–5), 
meaning they have a significant impact on the Capital and operating budgets.  As a result, 
advancing even a small number of these projects at the same time will require careful sequencing 
and potentially new revenue sources. 
 
B. Structural Pressure Areas 
The ranking exercise also highlighted several areas where the Town faces ongoing structural 
funding pressure rather than one-time project costs.  These include: 
 
• Transit and mobility operations, where the Transit & Mobility Fund already shows a 
structural gap between revenues and ongoing program costs. 
• Housing program administration and compliance, where expanding deed-restriction 
monitoring and housing program implementation may require additional staffing or 
consultant capacity. 
• Capital facility maintenance and replacement, particularly as the Town’s inventory of 
buildings and infrastructure grows. 
 
These areas represent long-term commitments that carry an “operating tail,” meaning new 
infrastructure, programs, or facilities often create ongoing staffing, maintenance, and operational 
costs.  As the Town’s portfolio of services and assets grows, these structural cost drivers will 
increasingly shape financial sustainability. 
 
 
C. Moderate-Priority Planning Initiatives 
A second group of initiatives received moderate priority scores.  These tend to include 
programmatic expansions, studies, and pilot projects that support broader plan implementation 
but are not immediately required.  Many of these fall within lower cost bands and may present 
opportunities for phased implementation or partnership with regional agencies, grant programs, or 
community organizations. 
 
Examples include mobility enhancements such as parking management strategies and bus stop 
improvements, as well as smaller planning and policy actions that support long-term goals around 
housing, climate, and community amenities. 
 
Because these items are generally more scalable, they may provide flexibility for Council to 
advance incremental progress even during tighter financial periods. 
 
 
D. Lower-Priority or Deferrable Projects 
At the lower end of the ranking exercise are several capital amenities and facility upgrades, such as 
certain park improvements, recreation facility expansions, and beautification projects.  While many 
of these projects remain consistent with adopted plans and community values, they were generally 
viewed by Council as discretionary in timing relative to other priorities. 
 
Many of these initiatives also fall into higher capital cost bands.  As a result, deferring or phasing 
these projects may represent one of the primary tools available to align the Town’s planning 
ambitions with its financial capacity.

E. Implications for Financial Sustainability 
Taken together, the ranking exercise highlights the fundamental tension between community 
aspirations and fiscal capacity.  The Town has built strong reserves and maintained prudent 
financial management, but the number of initiatives identified in adopted plans now exceeds what 
can reasonably be funded under current revenue assumptions.  In addition, the Housing & 
Community Spaces Strategy may identify future projects that are not yet reflected in the current 
priority matrix but could represent substantial capital or operational commitments. 
 
This does not mean that the plans themselves are unrealistic.  Rather, it suggests the need for 
clearer implementation pathways, including: 
 
• Phasing initiatives over longer time horizons 
• Identifying projects that require new revenue sources or grant funding 
• Distinguishing between essential commitments and aspirational initiatives 
• Considering the long-term operating costs of new programs and facilities 
 
Ultimately, aligning planning commitments with financial capacity will require ongoing dialogue 
between Council, staff, and the community about what outcomes are most important, how quickly 
they should be pursued, and how they will be funded.  Without this alignment, there is a risk that 
community plans may move forward more slowly than expected, which can create frustration 
when adopted priorities do not translate into visible progress. 
  
 
Council’s discussion at this session does not need to produce final decisions.  Rather, the goal is to 
provide staff with directional guidance that can inform future financial modeling and policy 
discussions in subsequent work sessions. 
 
Understanding where Council sees the greatest value and urgency will help staff evaluate options 
around revenue adequacy, service levels, capital phasing, and implementation strategies.  This early 
feedback will also help ensure that future planning efforts remain grounded in realistic pathways 
for implementation and financial sustainability. 
 
 
 
Council Discussion – Planning Priorities 
This first work session is intended to begin the process of aligning the Town’s planning 
commitments with its financial capacity.  Council is asked to review the priority rankings and 
the associated funding gaps and provide initial feedback on how they would like to see the 
Town balance competing priorities. 
 
In particular, staff is seeking Council’s initial perspectives on several key questions: 
• Which priorities does Council most want to protect or accelerate, even if financial 
constraints require tradeoffs elsewhere? 
• Which strategic areas could reasonably proceed more gradually, with implementation 
phased over a longer timeline? 
• Which efforts are viewed as essential commitments versus aspirational goals that may

4. Affordability Lens – who pays & who benefits? 
Financial sustainability for the Town is closely connected to questions of affordability and fairness.  
While Sections 2 and 3 focus on the Town’s fiscal trajectory and planning commitments, this 
section introduces an additional lens: who ultimately pays for the services the Town provides and 
who benefits from those services.  This perspective is particularly important in Crested Butte, 
where the local economy, recreation access, housing demand, and visitor activity create a unique 
mix of year-round residents, part-time property owners, and visitors contributing to the Town’s 
revenue base.  
 
Applying an affordability lens helps ensure that financial decisions are not evaluated solely through 
a budgetary perspective, but also through the values articulated in the Community Compass and 
other adopted plans, particularly the goal of enabling people who live and work in Crested Butte to 
thrive.  Affordability can be influenced through several policy areas, including utility pricing, 
housing investments, recreation access, and community spaces.  As a result, affordability is not only 
a housing policy question, but also a broader question about how municipal services are funded and 
delivered. 
 
 
A. Current and Potential Affordability Tools 
The Town already employs several policies that help moderate the cost of municipal services for 
residents.  These include rate structures designed to keep basic service levels affordable, such as 
lower base rates for water and trash services, with progressively higher rates applied to higher levels 
of consumption.  This approach allows essential household needs to remain relatively affordable 
while placing a larger share of the cost burden on larger users. 
 
The Town also provides targeted assistance programs for certain residents, including utility rate 
assistance for qualifying seniors and berm clearing services for residents who may not have the 
financial resources or physical ability to manage those responsibilities themselves.  While modest in 
scale, these programs reflect an intentional effort to ensure that essential municipal services remain 
accessible to residents with limited means. 
Beyond direct financial assistance, the Town supports affordability through low- and no-cost 
recreation programming offered through the PROST department.  Community recreation 
programs, youth activities, trails, and open public spaces provide meaningful opportunities for 
residents to enjoy the amenities of Crested Butte without significant financial barriers.  In a 
community where many private recreational opportunities carry high costs, maintaining accessible 
public programs and facilities is an important component of overall affordability and quality of life. 
The Town’s deed-restricted housing investments and housing program administration also 
represent a significant long-term commitment to affordability, ensuring that a portion of the 
housing stock remains available to people who live and work in the community.  These investments 
represent one of the Town’s most significant long-term strategies for maintaining a year-round 
workforce and community stability. 
Looking ahead, the emerging Community Spaces Strategy may further contribute to affordability 
and livability by identifying opportunities to create or preserve publicly accessible gathering spaces, 
workspaces, and community-serving facilities.  These types of spaces can help offset the high cost 
of private commercial or recreational alternatives while strengthening the social infrastructure that 
supports a year-round community. 
13
Page 12 of 14 
 
 
The Town could also consider additional affordability tools in the future.  One example that has 
been discussed conceptually is a property tax rebate program to offset local tax burdens for 
qualifying year-round residents.  While such a program is not currently in place, it represents one of 
several policy options that could be evaluated as part of future affordability discussions. 
 
 
B. Who Pays for Town Services 
The Town’s revenue system relies heavily on consumption-based taxes, particularly sales tax.  As a 
result, the cost of municipal services is distributed across several different groups: 
 
• Year-round residents, who contribute through sales taxes, property taxes, utility fees, and 
other charges, while also forming the workforce and civic backbone of the community. 
• Visitors, who contribute significantly through spending on lodging, dining, retail purchases, 
and recreation-related services. 
• Property owners whose homes are largely unoccupied, who contribute through property 
taxes and real estate transactions but may generate relatively less day-to-day sales tax activity 
depending on how frequently their homes are occupied. 
 
This mix of contributors has historically allowed Crested Butte to maintain relatively low property 
tax levels compared to many communities while relying more heavily on visitor-supported revenue 
streams.  However, as housing costs rise and service demands increase, questions naturally emerge 
about whether the distribution of costs remains aligned with community goals. 
 
 
C. Housing and Cost Burden Context 
Affordability concerns in Crested Butte are closely tied to the housing market.  Many households 
working in the community face high housing costs relative to income, while a significant portion of 
the Town’s housing stock is owned by part-time residents. 
 
This dynamic raises important policy questions about how the costs of maintaining community 
infrastructure, services, and amenities are shared among different groups.  Year-round residents rely 
heavily on services such as transit, parks, public safety, and infrastructure, but visitors and part-time 
residents also benefit from these same amenities that help make Crested Butte an attractive 
destination and property market. 
 
 
D. Equity and Fairness Considerations 
Viewing financial decisions through an affordability lens also raises broader questions of equity and 
fairness.  For example: 
 
• How does the cost burden of Town services vary between renters and homeowners? 
• Are year-round residents contributing proportionally more relative to the benefits they receive 
from Town services? 
• Should visitors or part-time property owners carry a greater share of the service costs 
associated with maintaining a high-amenity destination community? 
 
These questions do not necessarily have simple answers, but they are central to the broader 
conversation about financial sustainability and policy alignment.  These affordability considerations 
14
Page 13 of 14 
will become particularly important as Council evaluates future revenue tools, including whether 
certain revenue sources should prioritize reducing cost burdens for year-round residents. 
Crested Butte has built strong financial reserves and undertaken ambitious community planning in 
recent years.  The next phase of Council’s work is to align these aspirations with long-term financial 
sustainability.  This will require balancing several important community objectives  
a) maintaining affordability for residents,
b) sustaining core municipal operations,
c) protecting existing infrastructure, and
d) strategically advancing new capital investments.
The purpose of this work session series is to help Council establish a shared framework for making 
those tradeoffs.

Subject:    Approval from the Town Council for a Revised  Grant Application to 
History Colorado’s State Historical Fund for a State Historic Fund Grant for 
Phase 2 of Rehabilitation Town Hall (507 Maroon Avenue) and a Letter of 
Support. 
 
 
Summary 
The Town can apply for a competitive grant to continue historic rehabilitation work on Town Hall 
(507 Maroon Avenue) with the State Historical Fund (SHF) associated with History Colorado. 
Staff is seeking consent from Town Council to apply for the grant with the required local match.   
 
Background 
The Town was awarded a $15,000 grant in 2021 to conduct a historic structure assessment (HSA) 
on the Town Hall building. This assessment was completed and has given staff a comprehensive 
list and improvement priorities for the Town Hall building for coming years.  In 2022, the Town 
was awarded a $50,000 competitive mini grant through the SHF to complete construction 
documents (CD’s), specific to windows, mortar repair, structure for the gymnasium module’s stage 
roof and mechanical system.  In 2024, SHF grant # 2024-02-012 was awarded for $247,000 for the 
first phase of rehabilitation work on Town Hall, which focused on the exterior masonry and select 
windows for replacement.  This work was completed in 2025. The next phase of rehabilitation is to 
replace the remaining windows on the primary portion of Town Hall. The Town applied for 
funding for this next phase in the October 2025 SHF grant round and was not awarded due to a 
competitive project pool.  Staff  discussed the application with SHF staff and determined that it is 
worthwhile to reapply.   
  
Discussion 
The HSA gives the Town the ability to apply for SHF grants for rehabilitation projects for the 
building, including this competitive SHF grant.  Staff is seeking to apply to fund replacement of 
the remaining 48 existing non-historic metal windows on the main module of Town Hall in 2026. 
The total cost of the project is $663,880: 
23
• Windows and labor: $578,527 (increased by 2% from the October application due to a 
revised estimate by the contractor) 
• Architectural fees:$25,000 
• Contingency: $60,353 (required by the grant)  
This grant request is for $250,000.  There is a 25% cash match component from the Town, which 
will be exceeded by the remaining $413,880 project budget.  The Town Council  included 
$397,600 in the 2026 budget, $397,600 was approved for this project, but because the cost of the 
windows and labor increased by 2%, if the contingency is used, the staff may need to return to the 
Town Council for a budget amendment if the grant is awarded and the project proceeds this year.  
  
Climate Impact 
The windows do not close properly, bringing cold air and noise in during storm events. The 
exterior glass sliding panels are missing panels and do not function. For these reasons, the 
windows are challenging for individuals working within the Town Hall facility or using Town Hall 
for programs or meetings. New windows can fix these issues and support more energy efficiency 
at Town Hall.  
 
Financial Impact

Prepared By: Astrid Matison, Chief Building Official 
Thru: Mel Yemma, AICP, Community Development Director 
Subject:  Public Hearing: Ordinance No. 5 Series 2026: Adoption of the Colorado 
Wildfire Resiliency Code  
Summary:  This is the public hearing for Ordinance No. 5, which adopts the State of Colorado 
Wildfire Resiliency Code (CWRC). The State of Colorado adopted the CWRC on July 1st, 2025. 
Jurisdictions have until April 1st, 2026, to adopt the code and it cannot be less stringent than the 
state CWRC code. It is proposed for the effective date of enforcement of the CWRC to be June 1, 
2026, as the State requires it to be within 3 months of adoption. The Town of Crested Butte 
previously did not adopt the 2021 or 2024 International Wildfire Urban Interface Code (IWUIC).  
Previous Council Action:  The Town Council held the first reading of Ordinance No. 5, Series 
2026 on February 17, 2026. The Town Council adopted the 2024 International Building Codes on 
September 15, 2025. 
Background: As outlined in the first reading memo, the Colorado State Legislature adopted 
Senate Bill 23-166 in 2023 in response to increasing wildfire risk across the state. The bill created 
the Colorado Wildfire Resiliency Code Board to develop minimum statewide regulations to protect 
life and property from wildfire and structure-to-wildland fire spread. After more than a year of 
public meetings and advisory committee work, the Board adopted the Colorado Wildfire 
Resiliency Code (CWRC). Additional information is available at: 
https://dfpc.colorado.gov/WRCB. 
The CWRC establishes strategies to reduce wildfire risk through 
building hardening and defensible space around structures in the 
wildland-urban interface (WUI), defined as areas where human 
development meets or intermingles with wildland vegetation. 
Communities may adopt the State Wildfire Resiliency Map to 
determine where the code applies. The map classifies areas by fire 
intensity risk; the Town of Crested Butte is identified as a Class 2 
moderate-to-high fire intensity area. Staff recommends using the 
state map for clarity and consistency, though the CWRC also 
allows a ground-truthing process to review classifications. 
35
The code applies to new residential and commercial buildings, as well as certain modifications to 
existing structures such as changes of use, additions over 500 square feet, exterior siding or roof 
replacements exceeding 25%, and new occupiable accessory buildings within 50 feet of a primary 
structure. Requirements focus on vegetation management, fire-resistant materials, and design 
features that help harden buildings against wildfire. The attached summary sheet provides more 
specific details on meeting the code. 
 
Some materials allowed under the CWRC (such as vinyl siding, certain fencing types, and slate or 
concrete roof tile) are not currently permitted under the Town’s Design Standards. These conflicts 
will be evaluated during the ongoing Design Standards and Zoning Code updates. In the interim, 
compliant material options remain available, and staff has been coordinating with architects and 
designers to communicate these requirements for projects currently in review. 
 
Discussion: Ordinance No. 5, Series 2026 adopts the Colorado Wildfire Resiliency Code with the 
following amendments, which staff requests feedback from Council on whether to approve the 
ordinance as is with these amendments, or adopt with modifications: 
 
1. Map: Adopting the State Wildfire Resiliency Map  
a. As described above, staff recommends aligning with the State’s adopted map for 
clarity and simplicity.  
2. Building Envelope Buffer: Requiring the Structure Ignition Zone 1(0-5 feet) as measured 
from the face of the exterior wall of the structure to consist of only non-combustible, hard-
surface material, without exception for ignition-resistant plantings. 
a. Town staff recommends no plants in Zone 1 to make it easier for homeowners, 
landscapers and for building department final inspection. If this code provision is 
not amended the building department will need to become plant experts. 
b. Gunnison County is also in the process of adopting the CWRC code with this 
amendment. 
3. Rafters: Allowing an open soffit rafter tail roof construction can only be approved for 
Historic Structures (that have existing open soffit rafter tail construction that need to be 
replaced or repaired) as designated by the Town Historic Map. 
a. Open rafter tail design exposes structure to trapped heat and embers. Enclosed 
soffits are less vulnerable to heat and embers. 
4. NEW CHANGE SINCE FIRST READING – Voluntary Compliance for Historic 
Structures: Staff clarified the ordinance language to state that historic structures are 
exempt from the CWRC, while also providing a voluntary compliance pathway for 
property owners who wish to incorporate wildfire resiliency measures. 
a. For historic structures, the Town’s adopted Design Standards and the Secretary of 
the Interior’s Standards for contributing historic structures take precedence over 
CWRC material requirements. Since first reading, staff revised the ordinance 
language to clarify that while historic structures are exempt, owners may 
voluntarily comply with the code in a manner that does not conflict with these 
preservation standards. 
36
b. As part of the Town’s ongoing Design Standards update, staff will continue working 
with the state to better align historic material allowances with wildfire resiliency 
requirements and provide clearer guidance on how historic structures can meet 
resiliency goals while preserving historic integrity. 
c. It is important to note that additions to historic structures must still comply with the 
CWRC. The exemption and voluntary compliance pathway apply only to significant 
alteration or rehabilitation of the historic structure itself. 
 
Climate Impact: Wildfire in Colorado is increasing in frequency, intensity, and impact, with 20 of 
the state’s largest fires occurring since 2001 and four of the top five in just the past five years. 
Beyond the loss of homes and property, wildfires create widespread climate and community 
impacts, including watershed damage that threatens drinking water and aquatic life, increased 
flooding and erosion from post-fire runoff, and serious public health risks from smoke, especially 
for vulnerable populations. Wildfires also strain local economies through impacts to housing, 
tourism, recreation, and costly recovery efforts such as debris removal, erosion control, and 
infrastructure repair. The CWRC helps reduce these risks by requiring structure hardening and 
defensible space in the wildland-urban interface, where development meets vegetative fuels.
