1. Purpose of This Work Session
This work session is the third in the 2026 Long-Range Financial Planning (LRFP) series.  It builds
directly on the Budget History Analysis distributed to Council last week, which provides the full
17-year financial narrative, fund-by-fund history, and background on the LRFP process to date.
That document is available to interested members of the public.  This staff report does not repeat
that context.  This session asks Council to make two linked decisions: what revenue tool or
combination of tools Crested Butte will place on the November ballot, and how that revenue will
be deployed once voters approve it.  We present modeled revenue options at the $1.0M and $1.5M
target levels, share the property inventory and valuation data that underpin each option, and hope
to secure Council direction on the preferred revenue tool or mix and revenue target.  Voters will
want to know not just what they are being taxed, but what it will build and protect.  This report
also proposes a framework for Council to articulate an answer to the use-of-funds question.
The November 2026 ballot deadline is the hard constraint.  Council direction coming out of this 
session will determine whether staff prepares one ballot question or multiple, and will allow legal 
counsel and finance staff time to finalize language over the summer.
One framing note for Council: the Budget History Analysis’s Sections 10.4 and 10.5 present the 
analytical and values-based case for a longevity-based property tax rebate in some depth.  Council 
members may have already fielded constituent questions about it.  That analysis is staff’s framing 
of the question — not a pre-decided answer.  The longevity threshold (10, 20, or 30 years), the 
scope of the rebate, and whether it applies to the excise tax exemption, the General Fund property 
tax rebate, or both, are all open design questions for Council to decide at or following this session.

3. How New Revenue Will Be Deployed
This section sets out how new revenue would be deployed if approved by voters.  Council
direction at this session should address both tracks below.
3.1  Track A — Capital: Maintaining Existing Community Assets 
New revenue would be directed first to the maintenance and renovation of the community assets 
Crested Butte residents use every day: Jerry’s Gym, the Marshals’ Office, Rainbow Park, building 
maintenance, and a Town match toward development of the Mountain Express campus.  These are 
not aspirational projects, they are deferred maintenance and facility upgrades that serve full-time 
residents now.  Without new revenue, these projects are deferred indefinitely or funded by drawing 
down reserves to the point of fiscal risk. 
See Attachment B for the capital schedule with and without new revenue at each modeled level 
($1M through $3.5M annually).  The “without” scenario concretely illustrates needed deferrals. 
3.2  Track B — Housing: Targeted Investment in Existing Population Centers 
If Council directs any portion of new revenue toward affordable housing, staff recommends that 
Council also adopt explicit geographic policy guidance: housing investment should be sited within 
or immediately adjacent to existing Crested Butte population centers.  This addresses a real 
political vulnerability and reflects the lessons of the Whetstone development model, a project that 
created a new, isolated island of housing with traffic & parking challenges for Town, rather than 
reinforcing existing neighborhoods.   
5
Example A — Rental Development: Setting aside $500,000–$750,000 annually over 5–7 years 
could seed a 20–30 unit rental development targeting households at 60–100% AMI, sited within an 
existing Crested Butte population center. 
Example B — “Good Deed” Deed-Restriction Program: Setting aside $75,000–$100,000 annually 
would allow the Town to buy-down a market-rate home every 2-3 years, deed-restrict it with a 
workforce & residency requirement, creating permanent affordability with no ongoing subsidy. 
3.3  Proposed Allocation Framework 
Staff proposes that Council discuss the following as a starting point: approximately half of new 
annual revenue directed to the Capital Fund to address the maintenance and renovation backlog, 
and approximately half directed to an Affordable Housing reserve.  Council may adjust this split or 
establish a different threshold.  A key outcome from this session is that Council leaves with a 
stated position on use of funds that staff and the communications team can carry into ballot 
preparation.

Key Policy Questions for Council Direction 
• Flat fee or tiered by square footage? The stacked model is more equitable across property
sizes; the flat fee is simpler to administer and communicate.
• Which longevity exemption threshold — 10, 20, or 30 years?  Each roughly doubles the per-
parcel fee required to hit the revenue target.  Note: per legal counsel the ownership-duration
threshold is a single design decision that applies simultaneously to both the excise tax
exemption and the property tax rebate — not two separate choices.  The threshold Council
selects will therefore have a compounded effect on net revenue.
• Include or exclude the 183-day vacation rental exemption?  13 properties exceeded 183
nights rented in 2025. Council should assess whether the optics of the exemption (rewarding
active rental over passive vacancy) justify the complexity.
• Include vacant residential lots (53 parcels)?  They are already assessed at a higher property
tax rate.  Staff notes the question is whether materiality or equity optics should govern.
• Revenue target: $1.0M or $1.5M?

Key Considerations and Risks 
• Proposed Gunnison County Road & Bridge tax (+0.5%) would add to the total rate if
approved — this should be factored into any rate discussions.
• State marijuana tax share-back: Colorado permanently eliminates the 3.5% share-back of
retail marijuana sales tax to local governments beginning 2027 — a revenue reduction that
partially offsets any new sales tax gain. ~$40K in 2024.
• eCommerce: MuniRev outreach underway to understand mechanics of changing the tax rate
seasonally for online purchases. This is operationally unresolved.
• Resident burden: Full-time residents bear more of this tax than a vacancy tax.  Particularly
relevant for locals who do most of their shopping locally year-round.
• POS system burden on businesses: Seasonal rate changes require businesses to update point-
of-sale systems twice a year.  Voter approval required for new rate; voters must also trust
future Councils to implement 'tax holidays' in non-high-season months.
• Legal review: The implementation is fraught with difficulties and runs the strong risk of
failure on the administrative side and / or losing a legal challenge on various possible fronts.

6. Longevity-Based Property Tax Rebate
At the May 4 session Council signaled interest in pairing any new revenue tool with a General
Fund property tax rebate for long-tenured homeowners, consistent with the LRFP’s guiding
principle to limit the financial burden on year-round residents.  The analytical and values-based
case for such a rebate is laid out in Sections 10.4 and 10.5 of the Budget History Analysis, which
Council received last week.  That analysis frames the question; the design decisions below are for
Council to make.
6.1  How the Rebate Works 
The rebate would be applied against the Town's General Fund mill levy portion of the annual 
property tax bill.  At the current rate of 1.889 mills (net), the rebate per home equals the owner's 
assessed value multiplied by 0.001889.  At average assessed values, the annual rebate per 
qualifying home is approximately $182–$224.  The rebate reduces the Town's net revenue from 
any new tax by the aggregate rebate cost — which is why the combined net revenue modeling is 
essential.

• 20-year or 30-year threshold?  The 30-year threshold (purchased ≤1995) reaches back to the

7. Public Communications and Ballot Preparation
7.1  Communications Strategy 
A successful November 2026 ballot measure requires the public to understand both the financial 
pressure and what does not happen without new revenue.  The Budget History Analysis, now in 
circulation with the press and available to the public, provides the detailed analytical foundation. 
Council members who have shared it with constituents may already be fielding questions about 
specific revenue tools or the longevity rebate concept.  Staff and the Communications 
subcommittee are developing communications materials that build on that foundation, organized 
around three themes:
• What does not happen without new revenue?  Affordable housing development stalls for a
decade.  Jerry's Gym renovation cannot be funded.  Late Night Taxi and transit services face
curtailment.  Paving falls further behind.
11
• What steps has the Town already taken to manage costs?  8% across-the-board operating
cut; FTE cap; insurance savings; $7.5M Whetstone tap-fee decline; 5-year capital plan; Fund
Balance Reserve Policy.
• How does the Town's financial structure work?  Most Town services are funded by sales tax
— not property tax.  Visitors pay the largest share.  A new excise tax on non-primary homes
further shifts the burden toward non-resident owners.
7.2  Council Sub-Committee 
A communications sub-committee was appointed on May 18: Kate Guibert and Beth Goldstone, 
with Mayor Billick as alternate.  Meeting with the sub-committee is scheduled for May 28 
alongside communications consultant Miles Graham.
7.3  Polling 
We are in discussion with Keating Research to conduct community polling.  Proposal received 
following May 8 meeting; sub-committee meeting scheduled for May 28.  Polling results will 
inform messaging and help assess voter appetite for each revenue tool before ballot language is 
finalized.
7.4  Administration of New Taxes — July Discussion 
Council has directed that administration of any new tax be addressed at the July work session. 
Staff notes that both present meaningful implementation challenges:
• High-Season Sales Tax: Tyler/MuniRev system configuration for seasonal rate toggling;
POS system updates for businesses; e-commerce treatment; audit and compliance for
seasonal filings.
• Vacant Home Excise Tax: Parcel-level occupancy tracking; exemption database;
certification process (voter registration, DL, tax returns, STR booking data); appeals
process; cross-referencing with county assessor data.
If Council directs one or both of these tools forward at this session, staff will prepare a detailed 
administration plan for July.

Subject:   Resolution 12, Series 2026, A Resolution of the Crested Butte Town Council 
Amending the Paradise Park Workforce Rental Housing Tenant Selection 
Plan 
Summary: The Paradise Park Workforce Rental Housing Tenant Selection Plan (TSP) 
establishes eligibility requirements, application procedures, tenant selection processes, and 
ongoing compliance standards for the 14-unit scattered-site workforce rental development. Staff 
request Council approval of the amended TSP. 
Background: The Town adopted the TSP in May 2025 to guide the administration of the 
Paradise Park Workforce Rental Housing development. Since adoption, staff have leased up and 
operated the development under the TSP and identified areas where clarification and 
administrative cleanup would improve usability for both applicants, tenants, and staff.  In addition 
to improvements in language and general clarity, a summary of changes follow. 
1. Transition Away from Waitlist Selection
Tenant selection at initial lease up was conducted by lottery with ongoing vacancies anticipated to
be filled via the waitlist created by the most recent lottery for the specific unit size. The waitlists
were exhausted and grew stale faster than anticipated. Rather than spending staff resources
maintaining a rapidly changing waitlist, staff recommend transitioning to a first-come, first-served
selection process once the waitlist for each unit size is exhausted. Updates to the TSP in this area
include the following:
 References to lottery-based selection removed except for historical context.
 Addition of a first-come, first-served selection process once the waitlists for each unit size as
established in the most recent lottery are exhausted.
2. Updated Application Procedures
 Under the lottery selection process, staff provided a longer objection window because the timeline
for selecting tenants was fixed and less time sensitive. With the first-come, first-served selection
process, applicants are reviewed in the order they are received and units are offered to the first
eligible household in line. This system relies on timely processing to remain fair and efficient.
Staff recommend that upon receipt of a denial, applicants have two days to request a formal review
of their application with the Town Manager. This approach maintains timely processing while still
giving applicants a clear avenue to request a formal review.
26
 Require applicants to accept or decline an offered unit within three business days.
 Extended the shelf life of an approved application from 90 to 120 days.
3. Improvements to Application and Verification Processes
 With the transition to a first-come, first-served selection process, staff added clarity around review
priority and application completeness to protect against incomplete applications being submitted
as a “place holder.” Substantially complete applications are returned to the applicant for minor
corrections, and the updated application is reviewed upon receipt. Substantially incomplete
applications are returned to the applicant for correction, and the revised application is reviewed in
the order it is received with no place held for the original submission timing.
 Clarified the requirement that a household undergo requalification if the household composition
changes.
Final Impact: No significant fiscal impacts are anticipated.  
Legal Review: The Town Attorney has reviewed the amended Tenant Selection Plan. 
Recommendation: Staff recommend that Town Council approve Resolution 12, Series 2026 
amending the Tenant Selection Plan. 
Proposed Motion: A Council member make a motion to approve Resolution 12, Series 2026 
amending the Tenant Selection Plan, as part of the Consent Agenda.

Background: 
 
Chapter 6, Article 2 of the Town Code establishes the provisions and procedures governing liquor 
licensing within the Town.  Section 6-2-30 specifically outlines the process for renewal 
applications.  The Town Clerk is authorized to review and approve liquor license renewals on 
behalf of the Town Council, provided that no violations of the Colorado Liquor or Beer Code have 
occurred during the preceding year. 
 
At the July 21, 2025 Town Council meeting, the Council adopted Resolution No. 18, Series 2025, 
finding probable cause that a liquor code violation occurred when Clark’s Market failed to disclose 
the May 5, 2025, citation from the Liquor Enforcement Division on its liquor license renewal 
application as required by the application and Municipal Code Section 6-2-30 and further failed to 
notify the Town that it had entered into a Stipulation, Order and Agreement with the State Liquor 
Enforcement Division regarding that violation. The Order to Show Cause was subsequently issued 
by the Town Clerk and delivered via electronic mail to the Clark’s Market.  
 
The Order to Show Cause related only to the failure to disclose the violation on the renewal 
application, and it did not relate to the underlying violation of selling to a person under the age of 
21. Accordingly, the Council only determined if: 1) Clark’s Market violated the Town Code and 
State Liquor Laws by failing to disclose on its renewal application that it had been charged, and 
then failed to inform the Town during the renewal process that it had entered into a Stipulation, 
Order and Agreement with the State Liquor Enforcement Division, and 2) the Council determined 
that if the violation occurred, what an appropriate penalty for the violation was. Had Clark’s 
Market disclosed the violation pursuant to Municipal Code Section 6-2-30 they would have been 
required to appear before Council as part of the renewal process rather than being renewed 
administratively by the Town Clerk.  A public hearing on the matter was held on August 5, 2025, 
and was subsequently moved to September 2, 2025.  It was determined during the public hearing 
that Clark’s liquor license would be suspended for two days, but held in abeyance, pending no

Licensee displaying alcoholic beverages immediately adjacent to candy in violation of State

Summary: This agenda item presents a draft “Request for Concept Proposals” for the “Station 
One Challenge”, the first implementation item of the 5-Year Community Spaces Strategy. 
Previous Council Action: The Town Council adopted the 5-Year Community Spaces Strategy on 
May 18, 2026. 
Background: The 5-Year Community Spaces Strategy, along with the Town’s Facilities Plan, 
identifies the corner of Third and Maroon (Station One (306 Maroon Avenue) – former fire station, 
308 Third Street, and the adjacent parking lot), as a top priority for a community vitality hub. With 
the Fire District now settled into its new facility, and the Marshals planning to occupy 306 Maroon 
for the next year during their remodel, the timing is right to begin envisioning its future uses.   
Rather than launching a traditional RFP or conducting another round of community planning, the 
Strategy calls for a grassroots, partnership-driven process that invites the community to bring its 
own ideas to the table first. The Station One Challenge is that proposed process. Modeled loosely 
on a big ideas competition, it asks community members, organizations, and coalitions to submit 
early-stage concept proposals for what this corner could become, with an emphasis on community 
vitality, multi-use programming, and partnerships. 
The attached draft Request for Concept Proposals (RFCP) establishes the framework for that 
competition: the proposed timeline, the site parameters, the guiding goals, what a strong proposal 
looks like, and how submissions will be evaluated. Highlighted sections including links, maps, and 
floor plans will be added before the process is launched and document is published.

it is finalized and published.

honor the space, show a path to practicality and sustainability, fill gaps and add something
new) reflect Council's priorities for this site?
• Does the emphasis on partnership and multi-use feel appropriately weighted?

The Town of Crested Butte (Town) and Gunnison Watershed School District (GWSD) executed an 
Intergovernmental Agreement (IGA) to pay 50% of the costs associated with the design of the Red 
Lady roundabout, respectively.   Currently, final details of the 90% design are being completed 
and the 90% design package will be submitted to CDOT in the next week or two.  As attention 
now turns to the construction phase of the project, the Town has engaged GWSD in discussions 
regarding a second IGA to address cost sharing for the construction phase.

Background:   
The Red Lady intersection improvement was identified in the 2024 Crested Butte Transportation 
Mobility Plan (TMP), warranted by the Transportation Impact Analysis for the Crested Butte 
Community School’s (CBCS) expansion, authorized through CDOT Access Permit No. 324095, 
and affirmed in the Gunnison County Comprehensive Safety Action Plan. This intersection is 
directly adjacent to the school campus and is a key component in providing safe, efficient ingress 
and egress for school operations, as well as general community transportation needs for the north 
end of the valley.   
The GWSD has expressed a willingness to participate financially in the construction phase of the 
project, indicating a commitment to fund 50% of the construction costs. Additionally, in May GWSD 
identified a critical schedule constraint: they need to know the final construction cost as soon as 
possible in order to incorporate the expense into their budgeting and contracting for the new school 
facility. Due to this scheduling constraint, the District has been clear that it is not willing to assume 
any additional risk associated with construction cost escalation, change orders, or other unforeseen

funding, they want to finalize their obligation to the intersection improvements so they can allocate 
any remaining bond funds for other priorities and commit all funds before closing out the bond 
funding.

and construction of both roundabouts.  Currently, the County project is anticipated to go out to bid 
near the end of the calendar year.  Coordinating  the construction of both roundabouts has the 
potential to reduce mobilization and staging costs, improve contractor efficiency, and minimize 
disruptions to corridor traffic by reducing the number of separate construction mobilizations ; 
however, aligning the Town’s schedule with the County’s later bid timeline would prolong th e 
uncertainty regarding actual construction costs.
