Prepared By: Mel Yemma, AICP, Community Development Director

Subject:    Work Session: Corridor Plan Listening Session with Gunnison County 
 
Summary: This work session is a listening session with Gunnison County Staff and the Consultant 
Team for the Gunnison to Mt. Crested Butte Corridor Plan and Transportation Strategy (“Corridor 
Plan”).   
 
Previous Council Action:  
• April 20, 2026: Council discussed "Requests for Inclusion in the MOU with Gunnison 
County for the Corridor Plan" 
• June 15, 2026: Gunnison County Commissioners introduced the Corridor Plan process to 
Town Council 
• June 30, 2026: Gunnison County staff presented the plan's scope and process at the 
quarterly intergovernmental dinner 
• July 6, 2026: Under "other business," Council asked to begin discussing the Town's key 
values and priorities for the Corridor Plan 
• July 20, 2026: Town Council initiated discussion on key values and hopeful outcomes for 
the Corridor Plan, as a precursor for this listening session.  
 
Background: Gunnison County has kicked off the Gunnison to Mt. Crested Butte Comprehensive 
Corridor Plan and Transportation Strategy (Corridor Plan). This is intended to be the region's 
shared framework for land use, transportation, utilities, wildlife and environmental protection, and 
growth management along SH-135, in line with the One Valley Resiliency Roadmap. 
The Corridor Plan's goals, as defined in the project RFP, include: 
• Develop a land use vision that aligns future development with community priorities and 
identifies where and how growth should occur 
• Support affordable housing, walkable streets, and thriving neighborhoods through 
thoughtful long-term planning and design 
• Improve transportation, transit, and multimodal connectivity along the corridor 
• Identify infrastructure needs and constraints to support responsible development 
3
2 
 
• Protect natural resources, wildlife, and agricultural lands, minimize development impacts, 
and build resilience to a changing climate 
• Support the continued viability of agricultural operations in Gunnison County 
• Streamline permitting and regulatory processes in areas appropriate for development while 
constraining growth where it is not, increasing predictability for landowners and 
community members 
• Identify tools to help mitigate development impacts at a high level 
• Prioritize key areas for housing, infrastructure, commercial uses, schools, recreation, and 
land conservation, and show how they fit into an overall valley vision 
• Deepen coordination among jurisdictions and special districts to support consistent land use 
and infrastructure decision-making 
• Create a more equitable and inclusive region 
Discussion: This listening session is intended for the project team to gather initial high-level 
feedback from the Town Council on their key values and hopeful outcomes for the corridor plan. 
The attached “GVCP Shared Values Listening Session Discussion Guide” from the County include 
more information on their discussion questions for the listening session: 
• Affirming Shared Goals: 
o What goals are most important to you? 
o Are we missing anything? 
o Where do you see alignment and tension with your core values? 
• Corridor Specific Questions 
o What do you value most about the corridor today? 
o What forces and/or pressures are putting those values at risk? 
o What is your vision for the future corridor? In 5 years? In 10 years? In 2050? 
o What tradeoffs are you willing to make for the corridor’s future? 
Summarizing July 20 Values and Hopeful Outcomes Discussion: 
These draft values and hopeful outcomes are a summary of the July 20 council discussion as well 
as Council feedback shared individually after the meeting. 
Key Values 
Community Livability and Affordability 
The Gunnison Valley is a real, connected community, not a backdrop. People live, work, raise 
kids, and grow old here, which only works if the people who work here can afford to live here too. 
That means a local economy that serves residents first while welcoming visitors, a diversity of 
housing accessible to the workforce and year-round members of the community, and accessible 
places for people to gather and connect.  
Rural, Small-Town Scale 
We didn't move here for the view from Highway 135, we moved here to be part of it. Keeping that 
feel means the corridor stays open land, with clear edges between town and country rather than one 
long strip of buildout from Gunnison to Mt. Crested Butte. We value being immersed in the 
outdoors and in the rural, agricultural nature of the community, and development here should look 
like this valley, not like anywhere else. 
4
3 
 
Stewards of the Land 
The land, water, ranches, and wildlife are the reason we're here, not a backdrop. Stewardship 
means sustaining our agricultural heritage, supporting wildlife corridors and migration routes, 
maintaining intact and functioning ecosystems, and recognizing our role in larger issues like 
climate change. We inherited this place, and we intend to hand it off in as good or better shape. 
A Connected Corridor 
You shouldn't need a car to have a life here. We're not against cars, but this valley is built for 
people on foot, on bikes, and on the bus first. Staying connected means a trip across the valley 
doesn't have to start with getting in a car, and future infrastructure should connect us rather than 
keep us divided and stuck in traffic. 
Initial Strategies and Outcomes Council Requests the Plan Look Into  
• A clearer definition and picture of development's cumulative impact on community 
services, utility infrastructure, and transportation, with recommended tools to manage it 
• Better understanding development impacts and exploring the potential of impact fees, 
including financial analyses as part of project review so that costs of new development are 
borne by developers rather than the public 
• A clear framework, backed by updated regulations, that clusters and directs development 
toward areas with existing infrastructure and limits it where those services don't exist or 
where impacts to natural resources or wildlife areas would be significant, including a firm 
limit on development in critical wildlife zones as determined by CPW and critical 
agricultural zones as determined by the Gunnison Stockgrowers 
o Prioritize maximizing housing capacity within CB South before pursuing free 
market community housing in new locations, including supporting affordability 
there through mechanisms such as absorbing the costs of mass transit and 
roads/alleys rather than shifting them onto the working community. Progress in CB 
South can then inform how free market community housing could be approached 
elsewhere. 
• A strategy that leverages existing conservation funding and organizations to protect land 
where development isn't appropriate, along with meaningful incentives or support for 
agricultural operators 
• Meaningful requirements or incentives for community housing that is tied to documented 
housing needs in the community 
o If a strategy is pursued to expedite the approval of large housing projects, it should 
clearly articulate what success looks like. This includes defining expected housing 
production targets, identifying anticipated effects on existing home values and 
overall housing affordability, and explaining how the approach aligns with the 
community's rural character, quality of life, and long-term vision for growth. 
• Identifying key gaps, risks, and recommendations for future land needs, including schools, 
recreation space, gathering spaces, and childcare 
• Development regulations that account for traffic and parking impacts and connect into the 
valley's existing transportation system, with clear expectations set around parking 
availability and transit times during peak periods 
• A corridor-wide strategy that syncs the valley's two transit agencies 
5
4 
 
• Priority locations for park-and-rides, rec path connections, and future transit expansion, 
without adding significantly to travel times or impacting ridership 
• A framework to keep Highway 135 a two-lane road, leaning on multi-modal solutions 
instead of the default growth pattern of adding lanes 
• Following the county's approach with the City of Gunnison, formalize direct involvement 
of the Town of Crested Butte and Town of Mt. Crested Butte in decisions made within the 
three-mile zone, ideally through an intergovernmental agreement (IGA). 
Next Steps: Gunnison County and HDR are holding these listening sessions with all partner 
jurisdiction boards and will summarize and synthesize the feedback to incorporate into first key 
milestone on the project of identifying shared values and creating a case for action.

Subject:  Ordinance 11, Series 2026 : An Ordinance of the Crested Butte Town 
Council Authorizing the Sale of 806 Gothic Avenue, Crested Butte  
Summary: On July 20, the Town Council approved the purchase of 806 Gothic Avenue, a deed 
restricted unit, to add income and asset limits to the deed restriction and grant the Town the first 
right of refusal on future sales with the intent of selling the unit under the Town’s Affordable 
Housing Guidelines by lottery to a qualified buyer.  As is required by the Town Charter, staff 
request that the Town Council approve the sale of the unit.    
Previous Council Action: Over the past year, the Council has approved the purchase and sale of 
five deed restricted properties with the intent of updating the deed restriction. 
Background: Based on Town records, previous Staff carefully crafted sales prices based on target 
AMIs for the 30 homes that were constructed and sold by Bywater Construction in 2018-2020.  
The deed restriction on these homes includes an appreciation cap based on those sales prices as 
well as the requirement that one individual on the deed be a full-time participant in the local 
workforce and the standard prohibition on the ownership of other improved residential real estate.  
However, the restriction does not include an income restriction or a limit on the assets of potential 
buyers.  The ongoing expectation is that Town will step in to update the remaining 25 Bywater 
deed restrictions with then current Town policy objectives when their owners are ready to sell. 
Discussion: By purchasing 806 Gothic, the Town was able to release the current deed restriction 
and will record a new restriction at the sale of the property that accomplishes the following goals: 
 targets household incomes that are not served by the housing market;
 establishes asset limits, which ensures that those with high net value do not qualify to
purchase the home; and
 include a first right of refusal to the Town on all future sales.
Staff use a reverse affordability calculation to define the targeted income level for the unit.  The 
calculation converts the maximum allowable sales price into an affordable monthly housing cost 
and then converts that cost into an income and corresponding asset limit using standardized 
13
assumptions on loan terms and the 25% housing cost burden metric. A cushion is included in the 
interest rate to help protect against swings that could render the price of the home unaffordable at 
the designated income limit.   
 
The elements of the new deed restriction include: 
 Income limit of 160% AMI, or $158,400 for a household of two based on 2026 HUD 
issued Area Median Incomes. 
 Asset cap defined for the applicant’s household size and average age of the household 
members over 21 years of age.  See attached asset limit schedule. 
 A first right of refusal for the Town. 
The sales price for the one-bedroom, one-bathroom, partially ADA accessible, 794 square foot 
home is $289,579.60.  Applicants with a permanent mobility disability will be given priority. The 
property will be advertised in the CB News, on the Town’s Facebook and Instagram feeds, and 
announced via Gunnison County Housing Authority’s homeownership interest list. The three-week 
application window will open August 18th, and one open house will be held during that time frame.  
The lottery is anticipated to be scheduled in early September pending processing of applications 
and outcomes of the objection periods, with the sale closing 45-60 days after the lottery. Property 
information and the application are posted on the Town’s Community Housing website.   
 
Climate Impact: N/A 
 
Financial Impact: Staff anticipate that the net expense to purchase and resell the property will be 
approximately $4,000 for standard closing costs excluding the Real Estate Transfer Tax which is 
not required for a Town transfer of a deed-restricted property. 
 
Legal Review: Town legal counsel is engaged to support staff in this effort. 
 
Recommendation: For a council member to make a motion to set a public hearing for August 
17th to approve the sale of 806 Gothic Avenue and authorize the Town Manager to execute all 
documents related to the sale of the property.

Defining “Non-Primary” Homes 
Staff proposes using the IRS's 183-day benchmark — a home occupied less than six months (183 days) 
per year — as a clear standard for “non-primary,” consistent with the draft ballot language. Specific 
qualifying details, how each home reports and is audited, and any occupancy-based exemptions will be 
addressed in the September implementation ordinance; the ballot language itself is intended to stay 
simple by design.

Council's direction is needed on which to advance. Each option will be discussed in more detail below.

1) Ordinance 13: An Ordinance of the Crested Butte Town Council repealing 
and replacing Chapter 16 of the Crested Butte Municipal Code (Adopting the 
New Land Use Code) 
On June 30, BOZAR unanimously recommended Council adopt the new Land Use Code, with four 
suggested edits for Council's consideration (these suggestions are called out in “comments” in the 
attached draft code): 
• Keep the ADU height limit at 24' rather than raising it to 30' outside the historic core 
o Staff response: 
▪ Since the joint Council/BOZAR work session discussions indicated a desire 
not to distinguish between primary and accessory buildings, the final draft 
proposed that ADUs be allowed the same height as other buildings in 
residential zones (30 ft). This would apply to all zones outside the historic 
core overlay zone. Accessory buildings not intended for living space 
(garages, sheds) would remain at the current height limit of 20 ft.  
▪ In the historic core overlay, which has a different height limit of 28 ft, the 
historic Design Standards lead staff to propose keeping ADUs subordinate 
to the principal building: ADU height would be equal to or less than the 
principal building's height, up to a maximum of 28 ft.  
▪ BOZAR was concerned about adding mass and impacting the character of 
Town alleys, and finds that 24' is sufficient to design a livable unit.  
▪ Staff's recommendation is to consider a compromise that allows more of an 
incentive for ADUs: a height limit of 28 ft outside of the historic core and 
within the historic core, a height limit of 24 ft or the principal building's 
height, whichever is less.  
• Add a parking minimum category for 5+ bedroom units (minimum 3 spaces), but simplify 
the administrative waiver for deed restricted units so any deed restricted unit qualifies, (i.e. 
remove the 500' bus stop or parking management area requirement) 
o Staff response: Staff supports adding a minimum of 3 spaces for residential units 
with 5 bedrooms or more, as well as loosening the administrative waiver for deed 
restricted units. If incorporating this, residential unit parking requirements would 
read as (please note, mobile homes have a separate requirement of 2 spaces per 
unit): 
▪ 2 or less bedrooms: 1 space minimum, 2 spaces maximum 
▪ 3 or 4 bedrooms: 2 spaces minimum, 3 spaces maximum

8 
 
▪ 5 or more bedrooms: 3 spaces minimum, 4 spaces maximum 
▪ A Parking Waiver may be granted by the Director if it is for the purposes of 
a Deed Restricted Unit.  
Strongly oppose allowing digital marquee signs

with allowing this type of sign for the school and the Center for the Arts. A sign 
allowance for governmental uses can be restricted and limited to those uses alone. 
Extending the allowance to arts centers or nonprofit organizations would require 
opening it up more broadly, i.e., across the entire Sixth Street Corridor. 
o Staff's recommendation is to consider a compromise with BOZAR: allow digital

3) Ordinance 16: An Ordinance of the Crested Butte Town Council repealing
portions of chapter 16 of the Crested Butte Municipal Code and adding articles
18 and 19 of chapter 18.
Ordinance 16 moves sections on tree permits and fabric structures from the Zoning Code (Chapter 
16) to the Building Code (Chapter 18), since both are building permit functions and the move will
improve administration and clarity. This ordinance also cleans up Chapter 18 to remove references
to Appendix A, an outdated appendix that once held the Town's fee schedule. The primary changes
include:
• Tree permits: The tree permit process itself is unchanged, but is being relocated to
Chapter 18. At the fee schedule update at the next meeting, the $25 permit fee will be
removed to eliminate a barrier to conforming with the new Colorado Wildfire Resiliency
Code. The code also adds language allowing the Building Official to grant a permit on the
basis of conformance with that code (for example, no planted materials within 5' of the
building footprint). As a reminder, all other landscaping requirements will be captured in
the Design Standards update during Phase 2 and current requirements will remain until
the new Design Standards are updated.
• Fabric structures: Fabric structures (i.e. tent coverings) currently require BOZAR
approval, but in practice, allowance is driven more by building and fire code than by design
review, which is why the recommendation is to move them to Chapter 18. Fabric structures
are not allowed for more than 14 days, with the following exceptions:
1. Restaurant use to cover an approved outdoor seating location

public benefit for musical or cultural events  (for example, the Alpenglow tent on
the south side of the Center for the Arts)
3. Large-scale commercial and/or municipal projects greater than 5,000 sq ft, in place
only for the duration of the building permit (for example, the temporary tent used
during the wastewater treatment plant construction project to help house equipment
prior to installation)
