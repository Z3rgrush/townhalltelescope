Staff Report

Prepared By:  Jessie Earley, Planning Manager  
Thru: Mel Yemma, AICP, Community Development Director 
Subject:    Joint Town Council/BOZAR Work Session #1: Design Standards 
Update: Alignment on Approach, Goals, and Structure 
 
Summary:  
This joint work session is the first of three sessions with Town Council and the Board of Zoning 
and Architectural Review (BOZAR) to discuss implementation of the Historic Preservation Plan 
(HPP), specifically through the update to the Town’s Design Standards and Guidelines, which is 
phase two of the zoning code update. This first session will focus on: 
1. Recap on process and why the Design Standards are being updated 
2. Alignment on the approach identified in the Historic Preservation Plan 
3. Discussion on draft Design Goals for the updated document 
4. Discussion on proposed structure of design objectives, standards, and guidelines  
The following two work sessions will dive deeper into a first draft of proposed updates, with a 
focus on: 
September 7: All projects (landscaping, fencing and site design) and areas outside of the 
historic districts 
October 5: The mining era period of significance (the historic core) and the newly proposed 
early recreation era period of significance  
 
Background:  
HPP Recap: 
The HPP, adopted December 16, 2024, was led by the Compass goal to “retain the unique 
character and traditions of Crested Butte.” The HPP focused on how the Town can ensure that its 
architectural identity reflects Crested Butte’s deep sense of community and its evolution over time, 
not just 1880 to 1952.   This Plan was funded through a State Historic Fund (SHF) mini grant.  
The adopted HPP found that Crested Butte has so strongly preserved its mining era history that 
new construction has unintentionally begun to dilute it, with everything new emulating that era's 
styles and new neighborhoods starting to look the same. The plan resulted in a strategy to update 
3
2 
 
Crested Butte’s approach to Design Standards, where instead of requiring the full Town to be 
designed to emulate the Town’s mining era of its history, the HPP called for: 
1. Continuing to preserve the mining era period of significance of a smaller district of the 
historic core and a buffer zone around it 
2. Establish an early recreation era period of significance to preserve architecturally 
significant buildings from the Town’s transition from a mining town to a ski/recreation 
town 
3. Allow more flexible design in all other areas with design standards focused on mass, scale, 
form and pedestrian orientation.  
Updating the Design Standards: 
Following its adoption, to implement the HPP, the Town of Crested Butte partnered with MIG, Inc 
in the fall of 2025 to update the Design Standards (with grant support from State Historical Fund). 
Other members of our consultant team are Miles Graham with GBSM and Jessica Reske with 
Form + Works Design Group.  Simultaneously, the Town also partnered with Tatanka Historical 
Associates to survey the Early Recreation Era properties to better understand architectural 
significance and the scope of the early recreation era period of significance (with grant support 
from Certified Local Government program).   
As a reminder, the Zoning Code (which is up for adoption at this same meeting) focuses on land 
use, density, fit and additional regulations.  The Design Standards and Guidelines are identified 
and given authority in the zoning code to provide a framework for architecture of new and existing 
development and focus on visual appearance, style, architectural detail, orientation and 
landscaping/site design. 
About the Current Design Standards and Guidelines: Architectural Design Guidelines have 
influenced Crested Butte’s built environment for decades. The Town of Crested Butte is unique in 
that it requires design review by BOZAR for all projects.  The most recent Design Standards and 
Guidelines Plan was adopted in 1992 (with the most recent update in 2020). This document 
includes policies to protect historic integrity of existing buildings as well as to provide BOZAR a 
basis for making informed, consistent decisions during the design review process. In general, the 
Design Standards and Guidelines focus on ensuring all projects support Crested Butte’s mining era 
of its history with standards focused on emulating this period for all projects. 
The Town uses its Guidelines and Standards to inform the design review process for any project 
that needs a building permit, including new builds, landscaping, and updates to historic or non-
historic structures. This document helps ensure that all development reflects community values and 
supports a cohesive, expected look and feel for public realm features.  
About the Update: The update overalls seeks to make the document more clear, graphical and user 
friendly and implement the approach identified in the HPP. This work session focuses on ensuring 
there is alignment from Council and BOZAR on that approach, as well as determine alignment on 
high level design goals and structure to inform the specific design standards. The following two 
work sessions will review and discuss proposed content updates.

Discussion:  
Discussion 1: Affirmation of the HPP Approach  
The preservation strategy identified in the HPP includes the following priorities:   
• Update Design Standards document to be clearer, more concise, and graphically oriented.  
• Reduce the local historic district from town-wide coverage to a buffer around the National 
Historic District. (District change under consideration of adoption at this meeting as part 
of the new zoning map) 
• Adopt a new Period of Significance (POS) relating to the Early Recreation/ Ski Era (1960-
1984).  
• Allow more flexible design in all other areas with design standards focused on mass, scale, 
form and pedestrian orientation.  
• Revise the zoning code sections regarding demolition, provision of incentives, and 
potential for administrative level review. (Demolition section reviewed and updated in the 
new zoning code, while level of review will be discussed as part of this process) 
5
4 
 
 
 
Overall Approach: 3 Categories: Mining Era, Rec Era, and All Other Projects  
1. Mining Era: Currently, the local historic district encompasses the entire Town of Crested 
Butte (light purple):  
 
 
This means all properties within the Town currently are required to follow the Design Standards 
and Guidelines which requires all projects to emulate Crested Butte’s mining history. 
A smaller boundary within this district represents the National Historic District, which we often 
refer to as the core or historic core.  This area reflects the Period of Significance (POS) of the Coal 
Mining Era (1880-1952) (dark purple).   
The new zoning code (under consideration of adoption at the August 17 meeting), establishes a 
historic core overlay zone, which includes the historic code and a buffer around it (that includes 
previously designated “core zones” that fell outside of the national district boundary). For 
properties within this overlay, residential properties have a height allowance of 28’ (instead of 30’) 
and a smaller FAR allowance (.32 for the principal building and .48 for all buildings, instead of .4 
for the principal building and .50 for all buildings). Additionally, being inside the boundary means 
those properties would adhere to the “Mining Era” section of the new Design Standards and would 
continue to follow architectural standards like today when it comes to the mining era’s context.   
6
5 
 
The Design Standards update focuses on improving clarity for the mining era section, as well as 
clearly distinguishing standards for historic contributing buildings (contributing to the historic 
district), non-contributing buildings (historic buildings that have been altered over time), and infill 
projects (new construction or alterations to non-historic buildings in this district). The first draft of 
updated content for this chapter will be presented and discussed with Council and BOZAR at the 
October 5 work session.  
 
 
2. Proposed Early Recreation Era: As identified in the HPP, a second POS representing 
Early Recreation/Skiing (1960-1984) is currently being surveyed. The survey will 
determine which properties are architecturally significant and contribute to this era, and the 
Design Standards update will identify a new set of design standards and guidelines to apply 
to this period. This will help preserve their unique forms from that era, rather than risking 
them being required to conform to mining era standards, as the document currently reads. 
The first draft of these standards will be presented and discussed with Council and BOZAR 
on October 5.  
 
272 properties were surveyed from this time period (all buildings built from the time period 
are highlighted in the map below in orange, but not all will be determined to be 
architecturally significant to include in the new period), and the latest draft has identified 
88 as potentially architecturally significant. Outreach with these property owners is planned 
for September 15 to review the survey results and draft standards. 
 
7
6 
 
 
 
3. All Other Projects: For all other projects outside of these two districts, the HPP called for 
more flexible standards that only focus on mass, scale, form, and pedestrian orientation to 
ensure Crested Butte’s architecture can continue to evolve over time and be an expression 
of the people and stories in the community. This would mark a big change from how 
Crested Butte has approached architectural regulation in the past. The first draft of 
proposed standards for projects outside of the historic districts will be presented for Town 
Council and BOZAR review and discussion on September 7.  
 
Other Proposed Updates: Integrating Affordability, Climate Action, and Wildfire Resiliency and 
Improving Clarity  
In addition to the three overall categories of projects (mining era, rec era, and all other projects) , 
the revised document will be more intuitive, graphically legible, and also will better integrate 
options for more flexible or affordable design/buildings options, the Town’s climate action goals 
and integration with the Town’s all electric building code, as well as integrate wildfire resiliency 
requirements in the Town’s building code.  
The project also aims to improve the Town’s design review process for more consistent, 
transparent, and objective decision-making.  Improvements that were identified during the gap 
analysis and assessment are as follows:  
1. Navigation of the document should be logical and easy to understand, even for first-
time readers. 
8
7 
 
2. Readership (target audience) should always be considered, and the document should be 
written accordingly. 
3. Content length should be as minimal as possible to convey the required information. 
4. Writing should be clear and simple, avoiding industry jargon, redundancy, or 
colloquialisms.  
5. Writing should be intentional with word choice to minimize chance of subjectivity, 
misinterpretation, or confusion. This is especially important when using key title terms 
such as “standards” and “guidelines”, as well as key action terms such as “consider”, 
“should/shall”, “minimize”, “prefer”, etc. 
6. Graphics should be used to improve understanding but only included when necessary. 
7. Topic areas (subchapters) should be aligned and consistent with each other. 
8. Topic areas (subchapters) and content should align with the zoning regulations set out 
in Ch. 16 of the Municipal Code to eliminate redundancies and inconsistencies  
9. Topic areas (subchapters) and content should align with other town plans, documents, 
and initiatives such as the Ch. 16 Municipal Code (Zoning Code), Climate Action Plan, 
Transportation Mobility Plan, Community Plan, Housing Action Plan, etc. 
10. Development Review procedures should be as simple and efficient as possible – 
introducing and utilizing staff review and approval for a specific list of topics to be 
determined through the process, while reserving the most complex applications for 
BOZAR review. 
Discussion Questions: As we continue to move through this update to the Design Standards, we 
want to ensure that there is alignment between Town Council and BOZAR on the overall approach 
identified in the HPP: 
• Are there questions relating to the approach on the three categories of projects with 
different standards for each (Mining Era, Rec Era, and All Other Projects) 
• Is there alignment on integrating affordability, climate action, and wildfire resiliency, as 
well as improving the document based on the gap analysis?  
• Are there other components you think should guide the direction for this update? 
  
 
Discussion 2: Design Goals 
Design Goals are intended to serve as the north star for the document, by providing the nexus 
between the Town’s Compass goals and the specific objectives and standards within the document.  
They are meant to provide the high level rational for what the design objectives and standards are.  
 
In the current Design Guidelines, these items are referred to as design policies. For this update, it is 
recommended to rename them as goals, since they represent the intended direction of the document 
to guide the outcomes it aims to achieve. The new draft Design Goals were identified by the 
advisory committee:  
 
Goal A: Honor what was built here. Protect the historic buildings, streets, and neighborhoods that 
make Crested Butte recognizable and irreplaceable. 
Goal B: Build for this place. Design buildings to be functional and respond to the unique 
environment - for big winters, mountain conditions, and a community that gets around on foot. 
9
8 
 
Goal C: Keep the street human. Maintain the scale, rhythm, and walkability that keep the streets 
and buildings vibrant. 
Goal D: Make room for future design. Encourage creative and contemporary design while 
retaining the small-town characteristics of modesty and simplicity. 
Goal E: Build for everyone. Support a built environment that reflects the full community. 
Goal F: Steward the land. Design with the landscape, the climate, and the community’s finite 
natural resources, and to ensure long-term sustainability is prioritized.  
 
Discussion Questions:  
• Do these goals speak to the overall direction and purpose for the Design Standards?  
• Is there anything missing from this list of Design Goals? 
• What general feedback does Council and BOZAR have on these draft goals?  
 
 
Discussion 3: Proposed Structure of Design Standards and Guidelines  
 
To help with clarity and document navigation, the updated document will be rearranged into five 
key chapters. 
1. Introduction and Design Goals (tonight’s focus) 
2. All Projects (Landscaping and Site Design) (September 8 work session) 
3. The Historic Core (Crested Butte’s Mining Era) (October 5 work session) 
4. Early Recreation Era Properties (October 5 work session) 
5. All Other Projects Outside Historic Districts (September 8 work session) 
Within each chapter, there will be sub-sections that distinguish between commercial and 
residential projects (as well as broken down by single family, multi-family, mobile home, and 
ADUs), as well as different types of historic buildings (such as contributing and non-contributing).  
Appendices will include the History of Crested Butte, a glossary, and more information about 
historic resources in both periods of significance. 
 
Currently, the Design Guidelines document is structured to make policy statements that describe a 
desired statement or condition.  Then, there are Standards and Guidelines that describe a desired 
design treatment furthered with supplementary information that describes additional requirements 
or gives expanded explanations. There is currently ambiguity between how standards vs. 
guidelines are distinguished. Today: 
• Design Guidelines are a set of design parameters used by the Design Review Committee 
(DRC) to evaluate a project’s design and are only advisory in nature.   
• Design Standards on the other hand, are a set of obligatory design elements required for 
any project and are mandatory.  These are intended to ensure all development achieves a 
minimum quality of design.   
10
9 
 
However, BOZAR today runs into challenges between whether or not they can make a decision 
based on whether something is a guideline (advisory) vs. a standard (required) and they are not 
always clearly distinguished as a standard vs. guideline in the document.  
Within the update to the Design Standards, the suggested structure would be to move to 
Objectives/Standards/Guidelines, which would look like:  
• Objective: Describes the desired result. All standards and guidelines supporting the 
objective must then help accomplish that specific objective. 
o Standard: A required outcome to meet the objective. Standards use the word shall 
and must be met for a project to be approved. They establish the non-negotiable 
baseline for design in Crested Butte. 
 Guideline: A recommended approach that gives flexibility/options to meet a 
standard. Guidelines use the word should and allow flexibility in how the 
intent of the standard is achieved. They acknowledge that good design can 
take more than one form. 
Discussion Questions: General feedback on the proposed structure of chapters, as well as 
objective > standard > guideline is sought. This discussion is meant to inform whether the 
proposed structure for the design review system creates a clearer, more objective, and more 
consistent process, specifically by clarifying which elements should be mandatory Standards and 
how Guidelines should function.

Background: The Town of Crested Butte has been updating its Land Use Code to align with the

As part of these updates, the Town assessed its Parking PIL fee and ROAH program, as neither has 
been updated since 2009 (Parking Payment in Lieu) and 2016 (ROAH). Parking PIL is the fee 
assessed on commercial properties that opt to pay a fee in lieu instead of providing their required 
parking space. ROAH is the Town's housing impact program, which calculates employee 
generation based on development size and type, and then requires housing mitigation (via a built 
unit or a fee for a fraction of a unit). 
As a reminder, the updated zoning code provides several incentives to waive ROAH by providing 
a deed restricted ADU or unit for residential properties, or deed restricted commercial and 
residential space in the commercial/business districts. The updated code also provides options to 
reduce commercial parking requirements as part of those incentives in the Belleview and Sixth 
Street districts.

PIL fee based on the CDOT construction cost index, to better reflect the actual cost of building a 
parking space. The current fee of $13,000 has not been updated since 2009. Applying the CDOT 
construction cost index from 2009 to today produces a proposed updated fee of $29,600, which 
would then be updated annually going forward.

residential ROAH mitigation rate to 100% and maintain the commercial and lodging mitigation

 establishes asset limits, which ensures that those with high net value do not qualify to
purchase the home; and
 include a first right of refusal to the Town on all future sales.
Staff use a reverse affordability calculation to define the targeted income level for the unit.  The 
calculation converts the maximum allowable sales price into an affordable monthly housing cost 
and then converts that cost into an income and corresponding asset limit using standardized 
25

interest rate to help protect against swings that could render the price of the home unaffordable at 
the designated income limit.

 Income limit of 160% AMI, or $158,400 for a household of two based on 2026 HUD
issued Area Median Incomes.
 Asset cap defined for the applicant’s household size and average age of the household
members over 21 years of age.  See attached asset limit schedule.
 A first right of refusal for the Town.
The sales price for the one-bedroom, one-bathroom, partially ADA accessible, 794 square foot 
home is $289,579.60.  Applicants with a permanent mobility disability will be given priority. The

Why is the tax not based on home value? 
Colorado's Constitution includes a uniformity clause (Art. X, Sec. 3) requiring that property within 
the same class be taxed uniformly. Thirty-eight states offer a homestead exemption that reduces 
property taxes for primary residences, however, Colorado is not one of them. State uniformity 
requirements prevent municipalities from adopting a local homestead exemption tied to a home's 
value, which is why that tool, referenced by several Council members and illustrated against 
comparable Texas parcels in the May 5, 2026 report, is not available to Crested Butte as a property 
tax mechanism. 
 
This distinction matters because it reflects a goal Council has emphasized throughout the Long-
Range Financial Planning process: closing the Town's capital funding gap without further 
exacerbating the affordability challenges already facing full-time residents. A homestead exemption 
would have been an appealing tool for exactly that reason.  A homestead exemption protects 
primary-residence owners from a tax increase directly, by design, while asking only non-primary 
owners to contribute more. Because that specific tool is constitutionally unavailable to the Town, 
the excise tax under consideration is structured to reach the same protective outcome through a 
different legal mechanism: it imposes no new charge on primary residences, full-time resident tax 
bills remain exactly as they are today, and the entire new revenue burden falls on non-primary 
properties. Viewed this way, the excise tax is not a departure from Council's affordability goal, 
rather, it is the best available substitute for the tool Council would have preferred to use if Colorado 
law permitted it. 
 
The central trade-off of this approach is that a flat rate does not scale with value, so a smaller non-
primary condominium and a large non-primary home could pay the same amount. Council directed 
staff on July 20 to explore graduated structures in part to address this equity consideration, and the 
eight tiered scenarios integrated into ballot examples in Attachment B are available for Council's 
consideration. 
 
Definition of "Primary" Homes 
At the August 4 work session, Council discussed the move to apply the tax to homes that are not 
occupied as a primary residence.  The draft definition, which will be finalized in September as part 
of the implementation ordinance, is:  
 
Primary residence means a residence which is the usual place of return for housing as documented by 
the property owner of record signing an affidavit to that effect and providing at least two of the 
following: Colorado driver’s license, Colorado state identification card, voter registration, proof of 
employment in Gunnison County during six months of the taxing period, or tax documents.  A 
person can have only one primary residence.  Leased properties where the tenant occupies the 
property as the tenant’s primary residence for a period of at least 180 days would also qualify.

Parcel vs. Unit Clarification 
Measurement basis — two distinct definitions.  
• "Parcel" basis treats the entire lot as one taxable unit. This is the basis behind the existing 
~410 parcel count used throughout this report. Parcels with multiple dwellings, at least one 
of which is occupied as a primary residence, are excluded from this estimate. For parcels 
without any occupancy as a primary residence, the residential square footage (inclusive of 
ADUs and other accessory buildings) was used to estimate the appropriate tier. 
• "Unit" basis instead assesses each qualifying dwelling on a parcel separately, at its own square 
footage — a parcel with a non-primary home plus an unrestricted second dwelling, not 
occupied as a primary residence, would generate two separate assessments rather than one 
combined total. There are ~460 non-primary units.

taxable line items a single parcel generates.

Summary 
On July 20, 2026, Council discussed forming a citizen budget advisory committee and directed staff to 
return with options for its mandate, representation, and process. This report summarizes that direction, 
outlines timing considerations for the current budget cycle, and presents a recommended phased approach 
that allows community members to be involved in this year's budget process while building the fully 
representative committee Council described for future cycles. 
 
Background 
Council's discussion was informed by a petition signed by more than 200 community members requesting 
greater public input into Town spending and budget transparency. Council referenced the model used for 
the Affordable Housing Stewardship Advisory Committee as a possible template. Direction to staff included 
the following elements: 
● Purpose: increase transparency, educate the public on municipal finances and the tax code, and 
provide recommendations to Council. 
● Representation: include both second homeowners and full-time residents, with attention to broad 
representation and professional background. 
● Role: strictly advisory. The body would not have decision-making authority; Council retains final 
budget authority. 
● Function: serve as a bridge between staff, Council, and the public given the complexity of budget

Town Charter Limitations 
Article 8 of the Town's Home Rule Charter requires that members of a permanent board or commission be 
residents of the Town. A body formed as a time-bound committee, rather than a permanent board or 
commission, is not subject to this residency requirement. 
 
This distinction is central to reconciling Council's direction to include non-resident second homeowners 
with the Charter's residency requirement: a committee bound to the 2027 budget process can include non-
resident community members; a permanent board or commission, as currently structured under the Charter, 
45

cannot. If Council later wishes to establish a permanent board or commission that includes non-resident 
members, that would require asking voters to amend the Charter at a future election. 
 
Recommended Plan of Action:  
Budget Advisory Committee for the 2027 Budget Process  
Staff recommend forming a committee bounded to the 2027 budget process. This structure satisfies 
Council's direction on broad representation while remaining within the Home Rule Charter's current 
residency requirement and incorporates the prior Council input. 
● Duration: bounded to the 2027 budget process - formed in fall 2026 and concluding after adoption 
of the 2027 budget or delivery of the committee's final recommendations, whichever Council 
prefers. 
● Eligibility: open to Town community members, including non-resident second homeowners.  
● Composition: a mix of members with financial/budgeting background and general community 
members. 
● Staff role: support only - providing documents, data, and explanations on request. Staff will not lead 
the committee or set its agenda;  
● Committee Function during the cycle: provide real-time input and recommendations to Council as 
the 2027 budget is developed. 
● Committee Final deliverable: recommendations to Council on ways to improve the budget process, 
transparency, and communication going forward which will also inform whether Council wishes to 
pursue a permanent board or commission in the future. 
 
Future Consideration: A Permanent Board or Commission 
The committee's final recommendations may lead Council toward establishing an ongoing, permanent 
citizen board or commission on budget and finance, modeled on bodies like BOZAR or the Public Art 
Commission. Such a permanent body would be subject to Article 8's residency requirement as currently 
written. If Council wishes that future body to include non-resident members, Council could refer a Charter 
amendment to voters at a future election. This is not a decision staff is asking Council to make now. It is 
flagged here for planning purposes, since it may affect how the 2027 committee's work is scoped and 
communicated to participants.

Recommended Selection Process 
Staff recommend an open application process with Council appointment: 
 
● Application: a short form covering name/contact, and a brief statement of interest and relevant 
background. 
● Outreach: publicize the application broadly (Town website, Newspaper, Social Media posts, etc) and 
direct emails to community members who have submitted written comments on the LRFP process 
or ballot question. 
● Review: staff compiles applications for Council. 
● Selection: if applications exceed a manageable group size, Council can choose to prioritize for 
balance across residency status and background.  Staff recommend the committee be kept to a 
manageable size of no more than 9 to 11 members. 
● Appointment: Council appoints by motion, targeted for the September 8th meeting per the timeline 
above.
