Subject:  Resolution 19, Series 2026 A Resolution of the Town of Crested Butte Town 
Council Authorizing the Purchase of 806 Gothic Avenue #5A, Crested Butte, 
Colorado and Authorizing the Town Manager to Execute All Documents Related 
to the Purchase 
 
 
Summary 
Staff seek approval from the Town Council to purchase 806 Gothic Avenue #5A, a deed 
restricted unit, with the intent update to the new form of deed restriction and add income and 
asset limits and to grant the Town the first right of refusal on future sales.  Staff anticipate that 
Town will take ownership of the unit on or about August 17 and intends to sell it by lottery with 
the revised deed restriction shortly thereafter.  Staff will return to the Council to request approval 
for the sale of the unit and present the income restriction.    
 
Previous Council Action: Over the past year, Council has approved the purchase of five units 
with the purpose of updating to the current form of deed restriction to improve administrative 
efficiency, include the Town’s first right of refusal on future sales, and address the exclusion of 
income and asset limits in the deed restriction. 
 
Background 
Based on Town records, previous Staff carefully crafted sales prices based on target AMIs for 
the 30 homes that were constructed and sold by Bywater Construction in 2018-2020. The 
original deed restriction on these homes includes an appreciation cap based on those sales prices 
as well as the requirement that one individual on the deed be a full-time participant in the local 
workforce and the standard prohibition on the ownership of other improved residential real 
estate. However, the restriction does not include an income restriction or a limit on the assets of 
potential buyers. The ongoing expectation is that Town will step in to update the remaining 
Bywater deed restrictions with then current Town policy objectives when their owners are ready 
to sell. 
 
Discussion: Purchasing 806 Gothic #5A enables the Town to release the current deed restriction 
and record a new restriction that targets household incomes that are not served by the housing 
market and establishes asset limits, which ensures that those with high net value do not qualify to 
purchase the home. Additionally, the new deed restriction will grant the Town with a first right 
of refusal to purchase the unit and improve administrative efficiency. 
 
16
 
 
Staff use a reverse affordability calculation to determine the target income limits for the home.  
This approach converts the maximum allowable sales price into an affordable monthly housing 
cost and then converts that cost into an income and corresponding asset limit using standardized 
assumptions on the loan for the home. A cushion is included in the interest rate to help protect 
against swings that could render the price of the home unaffordable at the designated income 
limit. 
 
Pending the Council’s approval, staff will announce the property on the Town’s “Available 
Properties” webpage, through Town social media accounts, in the CB News, and through the 
Gunnison County Housing Authority’s homeownership interest list.  The home will be sold via 
lottery in accordance with the Town’s Affordable Housing Guidelines at the maximum allowable 
sales price of approximately $290,000.  
 
Climate Impact: N/A 
 
Financial Impact: Staff anticipate that the net costs to purchase and resell the property will be 
approximately $4,000 for standard closing costs excluding the Real Estate Transfer Tax which is 
not required for a Town transfer of a deed-restricted property. 
 
Legal Review: Town legal counsel is engaged to support staff in this effort. 
 
Recommendation: For a council member to make a motion, followed by a second, to purchase 
806 Gothic #5A and authorize the unplanned expenditure of approximately $290,000 and the 
Town Manager to execute all documents related to the sale.

Memorandum 
July 20, 2026 
 
 
 
To:    Mayor and Town Council 
 
Prepared By: Lauren Hawcroft, Finance & Administrative Services Director 
 
Subject: Long-Range Financial Planning Work Session – Ballot Framework / 
Council Policy Direction 
 
 
1. Background  
The Town of Crested Butte is engaged in a multi-year Long-Range Financial Planning (LRFP) 
effort, anchored to the priorities established in the Community Compass, to ensure the Town can 
continue delivering the services and quality of life the community values now and for decades to 
come. The effort responds to the reality that rising costs for personnel, infrastructure, and services 
are outpacing revenues under the Town’s existing funding model, and it is designed to identify 
these structural gaps early and address them thoughtfully and transparently in partnership with the 
community. 
Since Council began formal LRFP work sessions in early 2025, the process has moved from 
establishing a factual baseline of the Town’s funds, revenues, and reserves, through articulating 
strategic objectives and identifying structural cost drivers, to evaluating specific revenue-
generation tools and testing community support for those tools through polling. This work session 
builds directly on that foundation. Additional background materials, prior work session packets, 
and the full history of the LRFP process are available at 
https://townofcrestedbutte.colorado.gov/lrfp. 
 
During the June 1st LRFP work session Council directed staff to undertake the following: 
a. Conduct polling of voter sentiment on two possible revenue options – an excise tax on 
non-primary residences and reallocation of RETT revenues – and on community values 
around LRFP.  
b. Model impact of exempting vacation rentals with different thresholds of annual nights 
rented and model exemptions for homes owned for 25+ years in addition to prior 20+ 
and 30+ year modeling 
c. Continue working on public outreach and communications around budgeting and long-
range financial planning 
 
 
2. Purpose of This Work Session 
This work session follows directly from our previous Work Sessions, where Council identified the 
Town's long-term structural funding needs, reviewed ways to reduce or control costs, evaluated 
18
potential revenue tools, and narrowed consideration of revenue generation to either reallocation of 
the RETT or a non-primary home excise tax for the November 2026 ballot. This work session is 
intended to obtain Council policy direction necessary to draft ballot language and prepare for the 
August 17, 2026 ballot decision. 
 
3. Polling Results Summary 
Community polling on the excise tax and a companion Real Estate Transfer Tax (RETT) 
reallocation measure has been completed (Attachment A) and will be presented by Keating 
Research. This report focuses on the polling results that inform Council decisions, including the 
preferred revenue mechanism, the value of simple ballot language, dedicating revenues to specific 
purposes, and prioritizing protection of existing community assets. 
Keating Research conducted a hybrid phone and text-to-online poll of 103 likely November 2026 
Town voters between June 22 and July 1, 2026, weighted to reflect the anticipated electorate by 
party registration, gender, age, modeled income, and modeled educational attainment (margin of 
error ±9.8%). Key findings include: 
• Before hearing arguments, 66% of voters supported the non-primary home excise tax and 
33% opposed it; after hearing arguments for and against, support increased to 68% and 
opposition fell to 27%, with half of all voters (50%) saying they would “definitely” vote 
yes. 
• The companion measure to reallocate the RETT was supported by 58% of voters and 
opposed by 37%, essentially unchanged after voters heard supporting and opposing 
arguments. 
• Support for the excise tax was broad-based across party affiliation (84% of Democrats, 
59% of Unaffiliated voters), gender (70% of men, 62% of women), and age (75% of voters 
18–49, 57% of voters 50+). 
• The leading reasons voters gave for supporting the tax were that it benefits the long-term 
community (19%), that there are too many vacant homes (18%), that wealthy second-home 
owners should pay their fair share (15%), that it makes taxation more balanced (14%), and 
that it generates revenue for the Town (13%). The leading reasons cited by opponents were 
that the tax is unfair to second homeowners who already contribute (38%), concerns about 
how the Town manages its budget (29%), and general opposition to new or increased taxes 
(29%). 
• In message testing, the most persuasive arguments in favor of the measures were 
preserving existing services without raising taxes on residents (73% found convincing) and 
investing in workforce and community housing (78% convincing); framing the tax as 
functioning like a homestead exemption for full-time residents also tested well (75% 
convincing). 
• A majority of voters (69%) preferred applying the excise tax equally to all non-primary 
homes rather than exempting long-held homes, even though doing so would require a 
higher per-home rate; 56% said pairing the tax with a longevity-based property tax rebate 
would make them more likely to support it. 
• A 72% majority of voters said the Town should prioritize maintaining existing services, 
buildings, and facilities over new investments, consistent with the Capital Fund allocation 
approach outlined in this report.

4. Proposed Ballot Measure 
Given the polling results and support for the excise tax on non-primary residences, does the 
Council wish to narrow in on the excise tax and move away from considering a re-allocation of the 
RETT? Assuming so, the proposed ballot measure consists of a flat annual excise tax on non-
primary residential homes. The draft framework assumes a maximum TABOR revenue 
authorization of $1.5 million annually, a proposed annual fee of approximately $3,400 per eligible 
home, and approximately 465 eligible non-primary homes out of 1,291 total residential units. The 
TABOR ballot question must identify the maximum amount of revenue authorized in the first full 
fiscal year. 
Council has previously discussed the possibility of structuring the excise tax in response to home 
size.  Attachment B includes a summary of what a tax structure could look like if the tax amount 
varied over three ranges of home size - <1,000 sq ft, 1,000-2,000, and >2,000 sq ft.  Council 
should direct if they wish to pursue assessing different tax amounts based upon sq. ft. or whether 
they prefer to have a flat amount per unit. 
Staff also suggests the Council consider asking the voters if the tax amount would be increased 
annually with CPI or some other inflationary benchmark.  The State of Colorado uses the US 
Bureau of Labor Statistics Consumer Price Index (CPI) for Denver-Aurora-Lakewood to define 
inflation for purposes of TABOR calculations.  Inflation has averaged 3.5% over the past decade 
under this metric.  Linking to this index would help ensure that the revenues keep pace with 
increasing expenses over time.

5. Ballot Structure & Exemptions 
For simplicity and in response to polling, staff recommends the ballot question itself not include 
exemptions of any kind, including for short-term rentals (STRs), accessory dwelling units, or 
longevity of home ownership. Instead, the measure would apply uniformly to all non-primary 
homes and vacant residential lots. Council retains authority to adopt exemptions later by 
ordinance, after voter approval, should it choose to do so.  Town Attorney Karl Hanlon has 
provided an initial list of possible exemptions (Attachment D) to be considered for inclusion in an 
implementation ordinance. 
The Council has previously expressed strong interest in exploring a property tax rebate tied to 
longevity of ownership of residential property.  The appropriate mechanism for this would be

through an annual resolution as part of the budget cycle providing for a rebate of the prior year’s

properties and approximately $64,000 form the General Fund. 
 
6. Revenue Dedication & Investment Framework 
Consistent with Council’s previous discussions, the ballot measure could direct any revenue 
generated to be restricted to the Town’s Capital Fund. Revenue would then be limited to the uses 
defined for that fund. Any new revenues supporting the Capital fund would also offset the growing 
dependency on transfers from the General fund. 
Uses of the Capital fund are defined in Section 4-1-30 of the municipal code.  Generally, use of 
these funds includes acquisition, additions to, and maintenance of structures, procurement of 
vehicles and equipment, and maintenance for streets and parks.  The code also allows transfers 
from the Capital fund to Affordable Housing, Open Space, Transit & Mobility, Parks Recreation 
and Trails, and the Utility Enterprise funds.  It does not allow transfers from the Capital to the 
General fund. 
By directing new revenue to the Capital fund, the ballot measure would reflect the community’s 
sentiment in the polling of long-term benefit to the community and the Town’s ability to prioritize 
maintaining existing services, improving public buildings and keeping existing parks & facilities 
in good condition.  Polling also showed strong support for affordable housing and community 
serving spaces which could be funded by allocating revenues to the Capital fund.    
Rather than identifying specific projects for funding, this work session is intended to establish the 
policy framework that will guide future budgeting and ballot communications.  Future Town 
Councils would prioritize projects and allocate funds for specific projects, but through the lens of 
the intention that these revenues be used for Capital and related projects rather than for general 
operations. 
6.1 Priority 1 – Protect Existing Community Assets 
The first investment priority is preserving and maintaining the public assets that residents rely 
upon every day. This includes maintaining and renovating existing Town facilities, parks and 
recreation amenities, fleet and equipment, public safety facilities, and other critical infrastructure. 
Representative examples of investments within this priority include: 
• Jerry's Gym Renovation  
• Marshals' Office  
• Rainbow Park improvements  
• Commercial building improvements and facility maintenance  
• Fleet and equipment replacement  
• The Town's contribution toward the Mountain Express campus  
These projects represent the types of investments that could be supported through the Capital Fund 
and are intended to preserve existing community assets and reduce deferred maintenance over 
time.

6.2  Priority 2 – Strategic Community Investments 
The second investment priority is making strategic investments that strengthen the Town's long-
term sustainability, with a primary focus on affordable and workforce housing. The full impacts of

expected to remain an ongoing challenge for the community, however, and staff recommends 
planning for a significant, right-sized project every 5-7 years so that the physical and fiscal scale of 
investment remains something the community can absorb and integrate. Examples include: 
 
• Rental Housing Development Example

 serving households between 60% and 100% of Area Median Income (AMI). 
 
• Good Deed / Deed Restriction Program Example 
Setting aside approximately $300,000 annually could allow the Town to periodically 
acquire deed restrictions on existing homes, creating permanently affordable workforce 
housing while preserving the existing neighborhood fabric. 
 
These examples are intended to illustrate potential implementation strategies only and are not

Our consideration of internal control was for the limited purpose described in the preceding paragraph

A deficiency in internal control exists when the design or operation of a control does not allow 
management or employees, in the normal course of performing their assigned functions, to prevent, or 
detect and correct, misstatements on a timely basis. A material weakness is a deficiency, or a 
combination of deficiencies, in internal control over financial reporting, such that there is a reasonable

We consider the following deficiency in the Town’s internal control to be a material weakness:
Journal Entry Review and Approval
During our audit, we noted that the Town’s current journal entry review and approval process could be 
strengthened to improve internal controls over financial reporting.  Specifically, certain journal entries 
were prepared and posted without review and approval prior to posting to the general ledger.
An effective journal entry control process is important to help ensure that entries are accurate, properly 
supported, recorded in the correct accounting period, and authorized by appropriate personnel.  Lack of 
documented review procedures may increase the risk of errors or unauthorized adjustments occurring 
and not being detected in a timely manner.  Our testing revealed material journal entries, which had 
limited supported documentation, posted to incorrect general ledger accounts (causing cash to not 
reconcile appropriately), and transactions recorded in the incorrect period.  A formalized review process 
could have prevented these errors from occurring. 
We recommend the Town implement a journal entry review process whereby all manual journal entries 
are supported by adequate documentation and independently reviewed and approved prior to posting. 
Evidence of the review and approval should be maintained, either electronically or through written sign -
off, and should include verification of the purpose, supporting documentation, account coding, and 
mathematical accuracy of the entry.
Management’s Response:
The Town will enhance its existing journal entry review and approval procedures by formalizing 
documentation standards, review expectations, and month-end reconciliation processes. 
Supporting documentation will be maintained electronically and centralized through Tyler Content 
Manager where feasible. Management will continue strengthening review procedures to ensure 
journal entries are appropriately supported, coded, reviewed, and recorded in the proper 
accounting period. As part of this effort, the Town will continue to formalize month -end reporting, 
reconciliation confirmations, and documentation review procedures.
