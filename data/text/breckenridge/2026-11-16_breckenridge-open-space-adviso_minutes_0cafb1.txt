E. Peak 10 Trails Project 
OST staff and the commission reviewed the proposed project led by the White River National 
Forest (WRNF), which is pending approval to be included in WRNF’s 2027 workplan and 
could begin next year with a multi-year process involving NEPA analysis and public 
comment periods. The project aims to inventory user-created social trails on the National 
Forest, roughly between Lehman Creek and White Cloud Drive, with the goal of striking a 
balance between recreation and conservation by formalizing some and decommissioning 
others.  Planned community outreach will involve key stakeholders such as HOA groups and 
the Breckenridge Ski Resort, and the general public. Discussion touched on potential town 
involvement, easements (noted by Mr. Barlow), and potential threats/constraints (noted by 
Mr. Beckerman).

H. B&B Vault Toilet 
OST staff reported on the successful installation of the new double vault toilet, which cost 
around $100,000 and had a 6-month material lead time. Positive feedback was received 
regarding the low-profile design. Despite minor permitting challenges, coordination with 
contractors and Summit County was smooth. The commission agreed to consider future 
installations at locations like the Wellington Bike Park and Illinois Creek, keeping in mind 
the 6-month lead time. 
 
I. Aspen Alley and North Barton Trails 
Staff presented preliminary data from the Aspen Alley microtransit shuttle program, which 
recorded 15,671 total passengers over 22 days of operation, with rider direction split 
approximately equally between uphill and downhill trips. Overall trail usage along Aspen 
Alley grew from 16,100 to 17,553 users during the same timeframe, suggesting the shuttle 
operation increased trail access. Operating costs for the pilot totaled $40,215, with 90% of 
expenses covered through grant funding (The Town provided a 10% match, amounting to 
$4,021, from open space funds), and user feedback was overwhelmingly positive; staff 
emphasized that while the service was designated for hikers, traffic management on Boreas 
Pass Road during the duration of the shuttle remained busy but manageable, with support 
provided by the Summit County Sheriff’s Office. 
 
Staff confirmed the North Barton Trail will feature “trail portal” access similar to other 
trailheads without dedicated parking.

2026 Naturalist Report (0:26:35) 
 
Ms. Ella Garner and Ms. Jayden Floyd presented the 2026 Naturalist Field Report, detailing 
conservation, education, visitor monitoring, and seasonal traffic counts: 
 
Visitor Use & Traffic Monitoring in Cucumber Gulch Preserve: 
o Visitor flow patterns during the seasonal closure indicated 51% of visitors were moving 
south from the Peaks Trail entrance, while 48.3% accessed trails from residential access 
points.

o During this targeted monitoring, 58 total users were recorded (75% hikers, 24% mountain 
bikers). 
o Entrance usage analysis showed Toad Alley and Shock Hill had the highest traffic 
volume due to proximity to the Breckenridge Ski Area Gondola. 
o Manual reviews of camera trap data revealed lower overall human traffic during the 
closure compared to previous years. 
o For the first time, Cucumber Gulch Preserve had zero dogs recorded during the seasonal 
closure, attributed to increased signage and closures, and improved enforcement. 
 
Wildlife: 
o Wildlife Activity: While noted species diversity remained similar to previous years, the 
overall number of wildlife sightings increased, particularly related to ungulate species 
like moose, mule deer, and elk. Multiple sightings of moose with twin calves, increased 
bear activity, and a rare blonde bear sighting were particular highlights. 
 
Guided Hikes & Flora Discoveries: 
o Guided hikes covered areas including Cucumber Gulch, Illinois Creek, Bald Mountain, 
and the newly added Horseshoe Bowl area (where 3 hikes were held in partnership with 
the Breckenridge Ski Resort). Group sizes were capped between 7 and 15 participants 
based on ecosystem sensitivity. 
o During a Black Powder Pass hike, naturalists discovered Hoosier Pass Ipomopsis—a rare 
endemic wildflower—and reported the location to the USFS. 
o The program maintained strong community engagement, with an estimated 20–30% 
repeat participation. Many attendees noted joining guided hikes due to hesitation around 
hiking solo. 
 
Wildflower Week & Community Education: 
o Naturalists led 11 guided hikes during the inaugural Wildflower Week, drawing over 150 
participants and representing 60–70% of total event programming in partnership with the 
local tourism office and businesses. All hikes were fully booked, and staff plan to refine 
the waitlist system for next year. 
o Additional educational offerings included cocktail workshops with the Breckenridge 
Distillery, "Tea Time" workshops, and stationary educational hubs. 
o Staff received public feedback requesting more accessible, non-hiking program options 
(e.g., stationary talks) and are considering additional venues like the local library for 
future presentations.
