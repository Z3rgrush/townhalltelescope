PROCLAMATION RECOGNIZING OCTOBER AS DOMESTIC VIOLENCE AWARENESS 
MONTH 
 
In recognition of Domestic Violence Awareness Month, we aim to raise public awareness about 
domestic, intimate partner, and dating violence and to educate communities about how to be 
involved in important prevention work. Domestic and intimate partner violence is widespread and 
impacts every person in every community.     
  
WHEREAS, domestic violence harms individuals and families throughout Breckenridge and 
Summit County and can include physical, sexual, emotional, psychological, and financial abuse; 
and   
  
WHEREAS, a safer Breckenridge includes ending domestic violence, and requires the leadership 
of survivors and the collaborative efforts of advocacy programs, law enforcement, local 
governments, public health officials, schools, businesses, and community members working 
together to support survivors, prevent violence and promote healthy relationships; and   
  
WHEREAS, survivors in our mountain community may face added barriers to safety and support 
due to long travel distances, limited transportation, and concerns about privacy; and   
  
As such, we must strive to create strong, connected communities that stand with survivors, uplift 
their voices, take action to promote community education and awareness, and work together to 
change our society so it is safe and healthy for all.    
  
NOW, THEREFORE, the Town Council of Breckenridge does hereby proclaim October 2026 as 
Domestic Violence Awareness Month.    
  
Furthermore, all residents of Breckenridge are encouraged to learn about domestic violence in 
addition to believing survivors and connecting them with support. We urge all citizens to actively 
participate in scheduled activities and programs sponsored by ARISE Summit County and other 
community organizations to work toward the elimination of domestic, intimate partner, and dating 
violence for a safer Breckenridge.

Subject: 2027 Property Tax Mill Levy 
Summary 
Please find attached the ordinance setting the mill levy within the Town of Breckenridge for 2027 at 
5.07 mills. The ordinance is submitted for first reading.  
B
ackground  
The 5.07 mill levy is the amount the Town is authorized to impose and cannot be increased without 
voter approval. This mill levy has maintained the same rate since 2014. For the 2027 budget year, we 
are forecasting the 5.07 mill levy to result in property tax revenues of $5.48M. If there are any 
adjustments made to our 2027 gross total taxable assessed valuation at the County or State level, we 
will come back to Town Council with an appropriation. 
T
own Council Goals 
The proposed mill levy is a critical tool to help achieve our Town Council’s long-term goals. By securing 
additional funding, we can invest in key areas such as public works, recreation, community 
development and public safety—aligning with our commitment to responsible stewardship of resources. 
This levy supports the council’s vision of a thriving, resilient town by ensuring it remains a place that 
visitors return to and residents can live and work. Through this measure, we can meet today’s needs 
while planning for tomorrow’s opportunities, in line with our values of adaptability, innovation, and 
community. 
F
inancial Implications 
The 5.07 mills are for the purpose of defraying expenses within the General Fund.  
E
quity Lens 
As we consider the mill levy proposal, we are mindful of our commitment to fostering a community that 
values diversity, equity, and inclusion. This initiative aligns with our core values by ensuring that all 
residents, regardless of background or socioeconomic status, can benefit from the enhanced services 
funded by the mill levy. By prioritizing equitable resource distribution and inclusive decision-making, we 
aim to strengthen our community fabric and create opportunities for all to thrive. This commitment will 
guide both the planning and implementation phases, ensuring that no group is left behind. 
S
taff Recommendation 
Staff recommend that Town council approve the Mill Levy at 5.07 Mills upon first reading.

Summary 
The Finance Department is submitting the resolution to adopt the 2027 budget and the 2027-2031 capital 
improvement plan. 
Background 
In development of the 2027 budget, all departments considered the 2024 and 2025 actuals for analysis 
along with updated 2026 projections based on the 2026 budget. 2026 projections and the 2027 budget 
were developed collaboratively with the Town budget team. Although the Town faces fiscal pressures in 
2027, including the working capital demands of Workforce Housing projects, Utility projects, the Capital 
Improvement Plan (CIP), anticipated revenue softening, and increased personnel and operating costs, 
staff across all departments demonstrated notable fiscal discipline in responding to these pressures. 
The 2027 budget assumes Sales Tax, Accommodations Tax, and Parking income to be flat from 2026 
Budget. This assumption includes a slight increase from 2026 projection due to the lack of snow this past 
winter. On the expense side, personnel costs increase up to 4% and operating costs assume a 
continuation of the current purchasing plan. Several Town priority projects, including the Runway 
Neighborhood workforce housing, the critical rehabilitation of the Gary Roberts Water Treatment Plant, 
replacement of essential Town vehicles/equipment, software upgrades, Town facility maintenance, and 
the expansion of Little Red School House Childcare Center are driving expenses in 2027. These 
increases are offset by remaining funds that are, in part, available as a result of budget conscious 
expense allocations. 
To look at the impact of future capital projects across all Funds, we present a 5-year projection. 
Assumptions for the projection include flat revenue growth beginning in 2027 and a general 3% increase 
in operational expenses each year based upon anticipated inflation. In addition, we applied the planned 
purchases for future projects of our major Funds including Capital, Workforce Housing, Child Care, Utility, 
Facilities, Garage, and Golf. 
The 5-year forecast process is designed to project working capital needs of the Town through 2031. The 
Town Council CIP review on September 8th resulted in prioritization of projects and the Town will continue 
to pursue the CIP and the operational budget as proposed while monitoring cash flow during the year to 
determine if the 2027 budget remains viable. 
Mission: The Town of Breckenridge protects, maintains, and enhances our sense of community, historical heritage, and 
alpine environment. We provide leadership and encourage community involvement.

Staff provided Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) in accordance with Government Finance Officers 
Association (GFOA) best practices. These KPIs evaluate the Town’s financial health across several 
dimensions, including liquidity, fund balance stability, capital investment sustainability , and long-term 
financial flexibility. Collectively, the indicators show the Town remains in a strong financial position even 
while navigating substantial capital commitments. Taken together, the KPIs provide an important context 
for understanding both the resilience of Town finances and sustainability of the projected budget.  
Public outreach/engagement 
At the Town Council work session held on September 8, 2026, the public was invited to attend the Town 
Council work session presentation and discussion of the Capital Improvement Plan. The October 13, 2026 
budget retreat, also open to the public, allowed residents to hear directly from departments and senior 
leadership regarding their budget proposals. Before the adoption presentation on October 27, 2026, the public 
has further opportunities to provide input and feedback during open meetings. 
Financial Implications 
The 2027 budgeted revenues (net of transfers) are $138.8M and expenses (net of transfers) are $160.3M. 
The total 2027 forecast, including continuing operations and long-term financing, results in an overall deficit of 
approximately $25M. A significant portion of this deficit (approximately $17M) is attributable to the 
rehabilitation of the Gary Roberts Water Treatment Plant, which is planned to begin construction in 2027. 
Although 2027 is proposed as a budget deficit year, it is driven heavily by intentional capital investment and is 
well supported by our current cash balance. Ongoing operating revenues remain stable, and long-range KPIs 
indicate the Town continues to be financially strong. 
 
Equity Lens 
The Town considered the impact to our residents and community to provide continual support for Town 
programs and offerings. Whether revenue is tax or fee based, each department considered the welcome factor 
for residents and visitors to remove barriers and remain an inclusive community. The budget process embodies 
Town Council’s philosophy of considering who benefits from the Town’s various programs, the intention and 
impact of those expenditures, and how we as a community uplift and build trust with underrepresented groups. 
Staff Recommendation 
Staff recommend adoption of the 2027 Budget and appropriation of funding via the attached resolution.

Background  
The Joint Upper Blue Master Plan (JUBMP) provides policy direction on several land use issues in the Upper 
Blue Basin. The JUBMP was adopted by the towns of Breckenridge and Blue River and Summit County. One 
of the major policy discussions that occurred in the 2011 update to the JUBMP was density for affordable 
housing. A policy related to this issue requires the Town of Breckenridge to extinguish density it owns to 
account for density in new affordable housing projects. 
 
On January 28, 2020, the Council adopted an ordinance amending the Town’s Development Code as follows: 
 
E. Density For Employee Housing Projects: When new employee housing projects are developed 
within the corporate limits of the Town, the Town government may, in its sole discretion, transfer 
density it owns to the employee housing project at a one to one (1:1) ratio (i.e., transfer 1 
development right for every 1 employee housing project units permitted to be built).

   None

Background 
The Little Red Schoolhouse was constructed in 2004. Its floor plan has remained largely unchanged, with 
minor exterior improvements, including solar panels and play areas. The Town is partnering with the 
school to accommodate additional students and improve the facility for students and staff, consistent with 
the Town Council’s goal of expanding childcare. 
The project retains the six existing classrooms and adds classroom/flex space and offices. It also expands 
the entrance and reconfigures the existing multipurpose area for storage and circulation. The Phase 2 
plan reorganizes the age groups among seven classrooms. The expansion is anticipated to provide seven 
additional infant/toddler childcare spaces and two additional staff members per day in Phase 2. These 
operating figures are separate from building-code occupant loads. 
The Planning Commission reviewed the project at a work session on September 15, 2026, and a Town 
Project Hearing on October 6, 2026. At the October 6th hearing, the Commission voted unanimously (5–
0) to recommend approval to Town Council, expressing support for expanding childcare services in the 
community. The project is now before Town Council for final consideration. 
Under Sections 9-14-2 and 9-14-3, Town Council has final authority over the location and design of a 
Town Project. Town standards guide the design insofar as practical unless Council determines otherwise.

The Town Council must appoint qualified Town residents to the Planning Commission; four 
Commissioners' terms expire at the end of October. 
 
Background  
On October 20th, a sub-committee consisting of Council member Carol Saade, Planning Commissioner 
Ethan Guerra, and planning staff members Chris Kulick and Sarah Crump interviewed nine applicants 
for the four upcoming vacancies on the Planning Commission. The four vacancies are due to Planning 
Commissioners’ four-year terms expiring at the end of October 2026 (Mike Giller, Allen Frechter, Susan 
Propper, and Mark Leas). There are seven total members appointed to the Planning Commission. The 
remaining three seats will expire in 2028. Interviews were conducted with the following applicants:

language advertising and direct outreach to the Breckenridge Social Equity Advisory Commission, to 
attract qualified applicants who reflect Breckenridge's diverse population. The Town's outreach staff 
supported planning staff in this effort. 
 
Financial Implications 
There are no financial implications related to the appointment of Planning Commission members.

Briefly describe your interest in the Breckenridge Planning Commission.
I’m interested in serving on the Planning Commission because Breckenridge is at an important moment: the Town
is updating its Comprehensive Plan and the decisions made in this process will shape how the community grows
for decades. As an urban and environmental planner, I care deeply about the relationship between the built and
natural environments, and in particular how communities can adapt to long-term changes in climate. Mountain
towns like Breckenridge face real and growing exposure to wildfire, drought, and shifts in water supply, alongside
pressures on housing affordability and land use. I’d like to help ensure that long-term environmental planning
becomes a core part of how the Town plans and builds, while respecting the character and history that make
Breckenridge the place it is.

Please describe how your background will contribute to the Planning Commission, including any
experience you have with the following: historic preservation, history, architecture, landscape
architecture, architectural history, prehistoric or historic archeology, planning, building trades, cultural or

I would bring experience in long-term planning for climate and hazard risk, with a focus on practical strategies that
fit a community’s existing character. I served on Mayor Michael Bloomberg’s Special Initiative for Rebuilding and
Resiliency, formed after Hurricane Sandy to develop New York City’s long-term climate resilience strategy, and later
served as Executive Director of a recovery NGO in the Philippines after Typhoon Yolanda. Those experiences led me
to found Matter + Form, a consulting firm that continues this work while shifting more of our focus from post-
disaster recovery to prevention and mitigation. We advise state and local governments and private companies on
strategic planning for hazard risk reduction.
That work has spanned very different built environments, from the density of New York City to coastal and rural
Maine, where my firm advised on the State’s infrastructure resilience strategy. In Texas, we advised on the State’s
post-Hurricane Harvey rebuilding strategy. Each setting showed that good planning starts with understanding a
place’s specific built environment, landscape, and history, and tailoring solutions to the local needs.
My work also touches real estate and development decisions directly. My firm partnered with FEMA, the State of
Texas and Texas A&M University to develop and launch Buyers Aware, an advanced mapping and risk
communication tool that provides parcel-level flood risk information to homebuyers, developers, and lenders to
inform their choices about where to build, buy and lend for development.
I hold a Master’s in City Planning from MIT, with a focus on environmental policy and planning, and I would bring
that training, along with practical experience in risk communication and working with residents and stakeholders,
to the Commission’s work.
Is there anything else you would like us to know about you?
I’m a Breckenridge parent of a toddler, and our family is directly affected by the recent decision to close
Breckenridge Elementary. That decision reflects pressures the Planning Commission’s work helps shape: housing
affordability, land use, and whether working families can afford to stay. I want Breckenridge to remain a place
where families can choose to raise their kids, and I believe the Comprehensive Plan is one of the best opportunities
to make that possible.
