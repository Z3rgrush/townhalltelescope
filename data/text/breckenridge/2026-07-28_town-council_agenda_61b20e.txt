Underutilized Parcels Redevelopment Scenario Exercise 
The Commission then participated in redevelopment scenario exercises for both the F-Lot and City 
Market parcels. The exercises included two hypothetical redevelopment possibilities for each site, a low-
medium density and high-density redevelopment alternative. This exercise is part of the Comprehensive 
Plan revisions and mirror exercises that the Comprehensive Plan Advisory Committee already completed. 
The input from the Advisory Committee and Planning Commission will be used to create immersive 3-D 
modeled redevelopment scenarios that will be open for the public to experience and provide comment on 
later in fall 2026. 
For F-Lot, common themes to the Advisory Committee emerged, including making the parcel a 
community focused civic park by relocating surface parking underground and allowing for meaningful 
connections to the Blue River. To accomplish underground parking, market rate housing would likely be 
part of the redevelopment. Density was concentrated on the south end of the parcel with mixed-use retail 
and residential and a small hotel. Other ideas included: areas for outdoor restaurants, a playground, an ice 
skating rink/summer outdoor movie theater, additional public restrooms near the Riverwalk Center, and 
an expanded rec path connection to the downtown core. For the high-density alternative, Commissioners 
discussed a maximum building height of four-stories but with added opportunities for market-rate and 
workforce housing. It was important to the Commission that building height be higher closest to the 
Village and step down from south to north.

Summary 
Staff recommends that the Town Council approve the attached code amendments related to Accessory 
Dwelling Units (ADUs) at second reading. The proposed ordinance reflects direction provided at the June 9, 
2026 work session by removing the mandatory deed‑restriction requirement for non‑incentivized ADUs while 
maintaining the Town’s ability to require deed restrictions when positive points or other Town‑approved 
incentives are requested. Council approved the ordinance at first reading on July 14, 2026. There are no 
changes to the ordinance since first reading; the second reading is presented as originally approved. 
 
Background  
Under the current Development Code, residential space that meets the definition of an Accessory Dwelling Unit 
(ADU) must be deed‑restricted for local workforce occupancy. This approach was established prior to the 
Town’s current short‑term rental license cap system and associated regulations. 
 
At the April 14, 2026 Town Council work session, staff presented a broader ADU framework that included both 
non‑deed‑restricted ADUs and deed‑restricted/incentivized ADUs. Council expressed interest in providing 
additional flexibility but sought further clarity on how certain changes could impact short‑term rentals, particularly 
within Zone 1. 
 
On June 9, 2026, staff returned with a narrower discussion focused on the interaction between ADUs and the 
Town’s short‑term rental regulations. Staff reviewed how an existing single‑family residence with additional 
residential space is treated under the current regulations, including bedroom‑count review, occupancy limits, 
licensing requirements, regulatory fees, and the rule that a property receives only one short‑term rental license 
rather than separate licenses for different portions of a home. 
 
Following that discussion, Council supported moving forward with limited code amendments to remove the 
mandatory deed‑restriction requirement for non‑incentivized ADUs and expressed interest in monitoring 
outcomes before pursuing broader ADU changes.  Council approved the ordinance at first reading on July 14, 
2026. There are no changes to the ordinance since first reading; the second reading is presented as originally 
approved. 
 
To implement this direction, the proposed ordinance includes several targeted amendments: 
 
 
Page 7 of 103
Section 9-1-5, Definitions: 
• The definition of “Accessory Dwelling Unit” is revised to remove trigger language related to separate 
access, wet bars, bathrooms, potential sleeping rooms, and detached structures with domestic water 
service. This change reduces unintended classification of spaces as ADUs based solely on certain 
fixtures or layout characteristics. 
• The definition of “Kitchen” is revised to remove the rule limiting one kitchen per dwelling unit. This 
acknowledges that modern homes may contain multiple food‑preparation areas without creating 
separate dwelling units. 
• The definition of “Wet Bar” is streamlined by eliminating overly specific regulatory criteria related to 
room size, location, voltage, cooktops, and ovens, replacing them with a simplified definition. This 
reduces confusion and improves code usability. 
 
Policy 24R, Social Community: 
• Preserves the points-based deed-restricted ADU pathway by allowing three positive points for a deed-
restricted ADU when the unit is encumbered by a restrictive covenant in a form acceptable to the Town 
Attorney. 
• Removes a duplicative one-point award for single-family and duplex residential projects that provide an 
ADU. 
 
Policy 51A, Accessory Dwelling Unit: 
• Retains basic ADU requirements related to density, ownership, and compliance with applicable Town 
Code requirements. 
• The ordinance removes the size limitation tying ADU size to the primary dwelling, providing greater 
flexibility and supporting the Town’s goal of allowing more practical, functional housing options. 
• Removes the mandatory deed-restriction requirement for ADUs when no positive points or other Town-
approved incentive is requested. 
• Clarifies that a deed restriction or restrictive covenant is required when positive points are awarded for 
an ADU under Policy 24R, or when otherwise required by another provision of the Code, or a 
development agreement. 
• Preserves the existing workforce occupancy, lease term, rental rate, accommodation unit, and vacancy 
restrictions for ADUs that receive positive points. 
 
The proposed amendments are intentionally limited in scope. The purpose of this first phase is to allow more 
flexibility for accessory residential space and to assess whether removing the mandatory deed‑restriction 
requirement increases interest in ADU creation. 
 
Under this two‑step approach, the ordinance allows unrestricted ADUs when no Town incentive is requested 
and the property otherwise complies with Town Code requirements. At the same time, the ordinance maintains 
the existing points‑based deed‑restricted ADU pathway, preserving the Town’s ability to require a restrictive 
covenant when positive points or another incentive is awarded. 
 
Staff intends to monitor ADU activity following adoption, including the number of new units or conversions, 
whether ADUs are proposed on properties with short‑term rental licenses, whether bedroom‑count increases 
are requested, and whether spaces are created through new construction or conversion of existing areas. This 
information will inform a future discussion on whether additional incentives or code changes are needed for 
deed‑restricted workforce ADUs. 
 
Public outreach/engagement 
This item is policy-focused and no formal public outreach has been conducted to date.  
 
Financial Implications 
No new financial commitments are proposed at this time, outside of staff time to prepare code amendments. 
  
Page 8 of 103
Equity Lens 
Providing more flexibility for residential space may create additional ADUs for workforce housing, support 
multigenerational living, in-home care, aging in place, caretaker housing, and smaller-scale long-term rental 
opportunities. The proposed ordinance does not remove the Town’s ability to require deed restrictions for ADUs 
that receive positive points or other Town incentives. A future discussion regarding deed-restricted and 
incentivized ADUs may provide an additional opportunity to evaluate workforce housing outcomes and equity 
considerations. 
 
Staff Recommendation 
Staff recommends the Town Council approve the proposed Code amendments related to Accessory Dwelling 
Units at second reading. 
 
Staff will be available at the meeting to answer questions.

Summary 
The Town of Breckenridge Water Utility is recommending the Breckenridge Town Council adopt a six-month 
moratorium that prohibits the installation of exterior fire suppression systems on any residential or commercial 
building or property served by the Town of Breckenridge’s water utility. The proposed moratorium would allow 
Town staff time to consider the benefits and costs of exterior fire suppression systems and return to Town 
Council with an ordinance to address the installation of these systems. 
 
Background  
In response to growing regional and national wildfire concerns, a market has emerged for private wildfire defense 
systems that combine exterior sprinkler networks (typically mounted along the roofline of eaves of a structure), 
high pressure pumps, and water . These systems, commonly referred to as wildfire defense, mitigation, or 
suppression systems, are designed to protect residential and commercial properties from advancing wildfires. 
 
Municipal water distribution systems are designed to meet daily community needs such as domestic drinking 
water, sanitation, and conventional structure fires, not to combat wildland fires. Installation and use of exterior fire 
suppression systems create the risk of overwhelming the municipal water system’s capacity, thereby hindering 
effective firefighter response communitywide.  
 
Currently, without regulations, if these exterior fire suppression systems were allowed to either directly or indirectly 
connect to the water utility, there could be significant impact to the utility and its ability to provide service. The 
major impacts could be reduced or zero water pressure for certain pressure zones of the water system.  In addition 
to loss of water pressure, the tank refill rates may be greatly reduced and create a situation where the water tank/s 
may not be able to keep up with the demand, ultimately draining water storage tank/s. This potential loss of water 
storage may compromise water availability for other essential uses throughout the system. Exterior suppression 
systems may also pose cross-connection and contamination risks to the water distribution system. 
 
Adoption of a six-month moratorium will allow staff time to review related water utility policies and establish 
requirements for review and possible approval of such systems prior to installation. The Town’s building codes 
currently focus on ignition-resistant construction, fuel reduction, and urban forest management rather than on 
municipal water-based wildfire suppression.

Financial Implications 
During the moratorium period, staff time will be dedicated to evaluating these systems connections, and their 
relative costs and benefits. The necessary connection evaluations are similar in staff time to review of out-of-
Town Water service connections that require evaluation of pressure zones and potential impacts to the water 
utility and pressure zone areas.

Related to the Town’s Equity Blueprint, this policy is neutral with regard to the Equity Blueprint’s goals for the 
Town of Breckenridge.

Staff recommend that Town Council approve a six-month moratorium that prohibits the installation of exterior 
fire suppression systems on any residential or commercial building or property served by the Town of 
Breckenridge’s water utility.

Retrocycle Reusable Cup Program Update 
 
Since Memorial Day weekend, six Breckenridge cafés have piloted the Colorado Cup, a reusable drinkware program developed 
by Retrocycle, a Colorado nonprofit, in partnership with SustainableBreck and the Town of Breckenridge. Participating cafés 
include Semplice Café, The Crown, Mountain View Coffee Bar, Cool River Coffee House, LoLo Juice, and Ullr Café at the Grand 
Colorado on Peak 8. 
 
In the program's first 30 days, May 22 through June 21, customers borrowed 227 Colorado Cups and returned 75, producing a 
33% gross return rate. Return rates vary considerably across the network, ranging from 13% at some locations to 77% at others. 
That variance is instructive: Retrocycle is actively analyzing the customer profiles, café contexts, and operational differences 
behind the higher-performing locations to identify what is replicable and build from it across the network. 
 
This reflects a program operating in its earliest weeks, without paid advertising, with barista familiarity still developing, and in a 
market encountering the concept for the first time. The gap between borrows and returns is expected at this stage and is the 
central focus of Retrocycle's next phase of work: updated point-of-sale prompts, a barista incentive program, and targeted 
customer-facing messaging designed to close the loop on cups already in circulation. 
 
Across the network, the program displaced approximately 7.6 single-use cups per day, representing 1.75% of estimated to-go 
drink volume. The infrastructure is in place. The adoption curve is what comes next. 
 
Breckenridge is now the first mountain community in the United States to operate an open-loop reusable cup network, meaning 
cups borrowed at one café can be returned at any other. That distinction matters beyond Breckenridge: the data generated this 
summer in a tourism-driven, high-altitude context does not exist anywhere else and will inform reuse program design across 
comparable communities nationwide. 
 
Over the next 30 days, Retrocycle will pursue participation growth through updated point-of-sale signage, targeted digital 
advertising, and a barista incentive program. The expansion into Frisco extends the network's reach into a second Summit 
County community, increasing touchpoints for visitors traveling the corridor and strengthening the case that reuse works at a 
regional scale, not just within a single location.

V) OPEN SPACE DISCUSSION 
 
Trails Workplan (0:27:50) 
 
Mr. Overlock led the Trails Workplan discussion, which focused on refining the Trails Matrix 
Work Plan—a draft tool designed to prioritize a multi-year “wish list” of potential and 
proposed trails. BOSAC commissioners debated whether the "Community Context" scoring 
criteria was too rigid, suggesting a change from "and” to "and/or" so specialized trails aren't 
penalized for not meeting every connectivity metric. There was also discussion about the 
similarity between the User Experience and Community Context columns and that they could 
perhaps be combined and the Community Context column could be relabeled as 
“Connectivity”.  Concerns were also raised over discrepancies between localized wildlife 
habitat data and on-the-ground observations in French Gulch, prompting the group to propose 
adding dedicated location assessment column to better evaluate real-world impacts and 
parking access. 
 
Working through the exercise, specific trail evaluations highlighted trade-offs between user 
experience, cost, and redundancy. A proposed Phase 1 of the Tiger Road Rec Path scored 
high for user experience and neighborhood connectivity (Vista Verde, Shores, Highland 
Greens, Wayside, Vic’s Landing, Ten Mile Vista and other neighborhoods in the area east of 
HWY 9), but low on management due to its $1M-per-mile cost, prompting debate over 
whether Open Space should fund construction of recpaths while Streets & Parks handles 
maintenance. Concepts like American Gulch to Lincoln Park received moderate scores due to 
redundancy with Forest Queen Road, and the Rose Trail sparked conversation over beginner 
safety and access constraints. 
 
Moving forward, BOSAC agreed that the matrix serves as a helpful discussion tool rather 
than a final decision-maker. To allocate resources effectively across competing projects, staff 
will adjust the matrix wording and consider developing a secondary prioritization tool. The

Recent targeted communication initiatives include:  
• Wildfire Mitigation – Staff prioritized widespread communications on the importance of adhering to current 
Stage 2 fire restrictions. This ongoing educational campaign is designed to not only remind residents and 
visitors to be mindful of these vital safety measures but also to provide guidance on how to be fully prepared in 
the event of a wildfire emergency. The community's response to this messaging has been highly engaged, 
with residents taking action to stay connected by signing up for SC Alerts and Town newsletters, and following 
Town accounts on social media. For example, a single post prompting people to sign up for SC Alerts 
generated over 300 link clicks. Overall, our recent wildfire mitigation and preparedness posts have successfully 
reached 26,946 accounts and generated 47,284 total views. 
• 4th of July – Staff executed a comprehensive 4th of July communications campaign utilizing a dynamic mix of 
organic content, custom graphics, and engaging visual media. This strategic approach was designed to get 
residents and visitors excited for the holiday weekend, showcase the events and activations happening around 
town, and reflect on the success of the festivities. Overall, our dedicated 4th of July posts achieved a total reach 
of 17,184 accounts and generated 24,146 views. 
• RaceBreck - July was a major milestone for the RaceBreck series, anchored by the Firecracker 50 and 
Independence 10K. By utilizing an organic, grassroots media strategy featuring authentic photos, course 
videos, and participant highlights, we achieved a total reach of 25,971 accounts and 50,722 views. This digital 
engagement drove real-world participation, overcoming weather-related course modifications to our largest 
races. Compared to 2025, the Independence 10K grew to 501 racers from 430, and the Firecracker 50 
increased to 922 participants from 908. Long-term commitments also surged, with Summit Mountain 
Challenge and Summit Trail Running full series participants growing to 86 and 83, respectively.  
• Breck Free Ride – Staff began a campaign to boost engagement, trust and ridership of the Free Ride, starting 
with a Bus to Work Day event on June 23rd and a radio campaign focused on gas prices, trail access, and 
connectivity. This campaign continues through 2027, Free Ride’s 30th Anniversary.

Sentiment Data 
Over the past quarter, social media sentiment across 127 analyzed comments was highly polarized by topic. Initiatives 
highlighting community beautification and summer events drove an overwhelming 53 positive comments (42%), 
including praise for local workforce and the Town during Wildflower Week. Meanwhile, neutral feedback made up 31 
comments (24%), consisting primarily of residents asking practical customer service questions regarding event logistics, 
weather delays, parking, and transit schedules. In contrast, regulatory policies and infrastructure projects attracted some 
pushback, accounting for 43 negative comments (34%). The Dark Sky ordinance drove the majority of these negative 
comments.

Community Engagement 
In alignment with the Division's commitment to meaningful community engagement, staff continues to partner with the 
Breckenridge Social Equity Advisory Commission (BSEAC) to strengthen relationships with residents and expand 
opportunities for community participation. In collaboration with the Community Development Department, BSEAC hosted 
its inaugural Open House in the Vista Verde neighborhood. The event welcomed more than 150 community members 
and provided an opportunity for attendees to engage directly with BSEAC Commissioners and Town staff while sharing 
feedback on the Town’s Comprehensive Plan. Outreach efforts for this event and all Comprehensive Plan social media 
posts for the last month reached 33,694 views and 13,518 account interactions.  
 
Representatives from Police, Housing, Community Development, and Communications & Community Engagement were 
available to engage with attendees and answer questions. Family-friendly activities, including kids’ games and interactive 
vehicle demonstrations with the Police Department, contributed to a welcoming and inclusive atmosphere. Community 
feedback highlighted appreciation for the event's culturally inclusive approach. Participants expressed interest in seeing 
more events that reflect the diversity of the community  through food, music, and cultural elements, and intentional 
opportunities to build relationships and foster belonging.  
 
Building on this engagement, the Communications & Community Engagement team recently launched the Town's first 
WhatsApp channel to better connect with Spanish- speaking community members. The channel was developed in 
response to feedback received during a BSEAC “Cafecito” or Coffee-Talk event on June 18th and provides a new platform 
for sharing Town news, events, and important community updates in Spanish. 
 
Breckenridge Tourism Office Business and Calendar Impact 
The GoBreck.com calendar is free for businesses  and automatically links in the B -Like Breckenridge app (Annual 
GoBreck.com calendar page views: approximately 170k; Over 250 business event submissions currently live on the

Connect with Breck newsletters, collaborate on business initiatives, and join BTO staff during business walks. Questions 
specific to business outreach can be directed to Tessa Breder at the BTO. 
 
Upcoming Public Outreach/Engagement August 2026 
Looking ahead to August, our communications strategy will pivot to support several key civic initiatives and upcoming 
community programs. We are excited to add a live YouTube feed for Council meetings, and we will reintroduce Council 
wrap-up videos to increase accessibility of our meetings and current topics. Additionally, our messaging will prioritize 
upcoming Kaizen recreation program registrations, showcase the installation of our new Dark Sky-compliant Town 
fixtures, and continue emphasizing the benefits of using the Free Ride local transit system. Finally, we will sustain our 
highly successful, organic RaceBreck campaign to maintain momentum and drive participation through the late-summer 
racing season and fall/winter events.
