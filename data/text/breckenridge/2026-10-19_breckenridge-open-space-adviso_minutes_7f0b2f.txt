III) PUBLIC COMMENTS 
 
Teague Holmes expressed appreciation to BOSAC and staff for listening to user input 
regarding the potential addition of natural-style, hand-built downhill mountain bike trails to 
the town network. He noted that hand-built trails can often be less expensive, faster to 
construct, and lower-impact than machine-built alternatives, citing examples where minimal 
raking and deadfall removal allow trails to naturally “burn in”. Acknowledging that 
progressive downhill trails differ from traditional bench-cut “traversy” routes, Mr. Holmes 
highlighted SCOMBA’s (Summit County Mountain Bike Association) commitment to 
assisting with trail design, grant writing, hand-crew labor, and ongoing maintenance—
drawing a comparison to larger hand-built projects like the Palisade Plunge—and reiterated 
SCOMBA’s desire to remain active partners in navigating policy and environmental 
challenges.  Mr. Holmes also responded to a question from Ms. Zanca, stating that he 
believed the trails needed to be downhill-only but could be open to hiking.

G. Aspen Alley and North Barton Trail Proposals 
Staff and commissioners reviewed U.S. Forest Service naming ideas for two proposed 
trails located primarily on National Forest land: Barton Connector and the new Aspen 
Alley uphill alignment. While Barton Connector was universally supported, the Forest 
Service's recommendation of Aspen Incline for the uphill route drew concerns that users 
might confuse "Incline" with the steep Manitou Incline Trail and not understand that the 
new trail is intended for uphill and downhill hiking use. Commissioners discussed 
balancing user expectations with the well-established "Aspen Alley" brand, ultimately 
agreeing to offer constructive feedback to the Forest Service in proposing alternatives 
like Aspen Ascent or Back Alley without disrupting the agency's approval process.

that implementation of the new Aspen Alley uphill trail is pending imminent U.S. Forest

J. Wellington-Oro Water Treatment Plant 
Staff provided an update on efforts to optimize the French Gulch Water Treatment Plant 
to ensure regulatory compliance under a binding Consent Decree with the EPA and 
Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment (CDPHE). The decree, 
established in connection with the Town and Summit County’s 2005 acquisition of the 
B&B mining claims to prevent a federal Superfund designation, obligates the local 
governments to treat metal-heavy mine discharge in perpetuity. Because the aging plant 
frequently experiences downtime and is often unable to meet strict numerical standards 
for cadmium and zinc, the Town is budgeting for engineering consultants to evaluate 
operational improvements- specifically pilot-testing an upstream aeration pond and 
converting to a surface discharge system. 
 
Working backward from a critical 2029 Water Quality Control Commission hearing, staff 
are partnering with County staff and Northwest Colorado Council of Governments 
(COG’s) specialized water quality legal counsel to demonstrate good-faith operational 
progress. At the hearing, the Town and County aim to prove they are taking tangible 
steps to improve plant performance while advocating for localized numeric standards in 
French Gulch, which contains multiple unmanaged contamination sources outside 
municipal jurisdiction. Commissioners affirmed the importance of monitoring the issue 
closely as staff navigate regulatory risks, potential visual impacts, and long-term financial 
commitments ahead of the 2029 deadline. 
 
K. Aspen Alley Seasonal Management Plan 
Staff shared positive updates on the seasonal Aspen Alley microtransit shuttle, noting 
strong public compliance with parking restrictions and successful weekend capacity

funding to maintain the program in future seasons to manage parking demand. Quick

overflow parking through signage, cones, and police enforcement.

Staff presented the preliminary Open Space & Trails (OST) budget ahead of the October 13th 
Town Council budget retreat, highlighting a conservative fiscal approach based on flat sales 
tax revenue projections. 2026 projected revenues exceed operational expenses by 
approximately $210,000 for the current year and by $450,000 in 2027. Additionally,

Breckenridge History Funding Recommendation (1:06:36) 
Commissioners reviewed proposed funding contributions for Breckenridge History’s 2027 
capital work plan following an update from Executive Director Larissa O'Neil during the 
August 2026 BOSAC meeting. The total project scope was reduced after pulling the Arctic 
Stamp Mill restoration due to permitting complexities on the National Forest, leaving priority 
efforts focused on cabin restorations at Mountain Pride, repairs to the Dry Gulch Shop, and 
ongoing work at the Reliance Dredge.  
 
Staff highlighted that historic preservation aligns with conservation values identified in the 
Open Space Master Plan and noted that any contribution from the Open Space Fund directly 
offsets draws on the Town’s General Fund. Following discussion among the Commission 
recognizing inflation, rising project costs, and the immense value historic sites add to the 
backcountry trail experience, Commissioner Tennal moved to recommend an increased 
annual allocation of $125,000 for the 2027 budget year (a $25,000 increase over the historical 
baseline), which passed with a unanimous 7/0 vote. 
 
Looking ahead, the commission emphasized maintaining a simple annual grant structure 
rather than line-item scrutiny, while establishing long-term "trust but verify" oversight 
measures. Commissioners directed staff to request formal annual grant reports—similar to 
requirements for FDRD and CFI—detailing how funds are spent and tracking external grant 
applications, such as State Historical Fund grants. Additionally, commissioners asked staff to 
explore a more formulaic funding framework for future cycles, including evaluating 100% 
funding for core Master Plan priorities, and potentially encouraging Summit County OSAC to 
increase its cost-sharing on jointly owned properties such as Mountain Pride.

VI) OTHER MATTERS RELATED TO OPEN SPACE TOPICS 
Staff shared that they recently had an informal meeting between local Open Space staff 
(Duke Barlow, Tony Overlock, Katherine King), USFS representative Tyler Kirkpatrick, and

The meeting focused on understanding advocate concepts, sharing agency constraints, and 
building common ground across jurisdictions. Moving forward, staff plan to vet specific trail

Mr. Barlow alerted the commission to a national USFS Notice of Intent to prepare an 
Environmental Impact Statement for major Travel Management Rule revisions that carry 
significant local implications for the Dillon Ranger District of the White River National 
Forest. Key proposed changes include reversing a fundamental rule to all routes being 
assumed open unless signed closed, creating new allowances for off-road motorized access, 
exempting Class 1 e-bikes from motorized restrictions, and mandating annual route updates 
without providing supplemental funding or staffing. To preserve standing for future 
involvement, staff are submitting an initial comment letter before the September 23rd deadline 
outlining capacity, enforcement, and policy concerns, while coordinating outreach with 
regional municipal partners such as Summit, Pitkin and Eagle counties.
